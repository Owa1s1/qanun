Question 1 · Amino acids, haem and nucleotides · easy

A 24-year-old woman with PKU kept to her diet through childhood but stopped it as a teenager. She is now 8 weeks pregnant with her first baby and feels well. Her phenylalanine level is 15 mg/dL (the target in pregnancy is 2-6). Her husband is not a carrier, so her baby does not have PKU.

What is the risk to the baby?

  1. A. None, because the baby does not have PKU
  2. B. Only mild jaundice
  3. C. Albinism
  4. D. Neural tube defects
  5. E. Microcephaly and heart defects
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Microcephaly and heart defects

Phenylalanine is actively transported across the placenta, so fetal levels are even higher than the mother's. If a woman with PKU has high phenylalanine in pregnancy, the baby, even without PKU, is at high risk of microcephaly, learning disability, congenital heart disease, facial features and growth restriction (maternal PKU syndrome). This can be prevented by restarting a strict diet before conception and keeping phenylalanine low throughout pregnancy. All girls with PKU should be counselled about this.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. None, because the baby does not have PKU: High maternal phenylalanine crosses the placenta and harms the fetus even though the baby does not have PKU.
  • B. Only mild jaundice: The risk is far greater than jaundice: brain and heart development are damaged in early pregnancy.
  • C. Albinism: Albinism is caused by tyrosinase deficiency; maternal PKU does not cause it.
  • D. Neural tube defects: Neural tube defects are not the main problem; microcephaly, learning disability and heart defects are.

Key point: Uncontrolled maternal PKU causes microcephaly, learning disability and heart defects; restart the diet before conception.

Question 2 · Carbohydrate metabolism · medium

A 6-month-old baby, well on breast milk, starts vomiting, sweating, becoming pale and drowsy soon after her mother begins giving her mashed fruit and sweetened juice. During one episode her blood glucose is 36 mg/dL; her liver is 3 cm below the costal margin and her ALT is 145 U/L (normal under 40). She refuses sweet foods later.

Which enzyme is deficient?

  1. A. Fructokinase
  2. B. Galactose-1-phosphate uridyltransferase
  3. C. Aldolase B
  4. D. Glucose-6-phosphatase
  5. E. Lactase
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Aldolase B

Hereditary fructose intolerance is an autosomal recessive deficiency of aldolase B. Babies are well on breast milk (lactose) and become ill when fruit, juice, sucrose or sorbitol is introduced at weaning, with vomiting, hypoglycaemia, sweating and lethargy, and later liver and kidney damage and failure to thrive. Older children often develop a strong dislike of sweet foods and have very healthy teeth. Treatment is strict avoidance of fructose, sucrose and sorbitol, including in syrups and medicines.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Fructokinase: Fructokinase deficiency (essential fructosuria) is harmless, causing only fructose in the urine, without hypoglycaemia or liver damage.
  • B. Galactose-1-phosphate uridyltransferase: Classic galactosaemia makes babies ill in the first days on breast milk; she was well on breast milk and became ill only with fruit.
  • D. Glucose-6-phosphatase: Von Gierke disease causes hypoglycaemia whenever feeds are delayed, from birth, whatever is eaten; her episodes follow fruit and juice.
  • E. Lactase: Lactase deficiency causes bloating and watery diarrhoea after milk, not hypoglycaemia and liver damage after fruit.

Key point: Hereditary fructose intolerance (aldolase B deficiency) presents at weaning with vomiting and hypoglycaemia after fructose or sucrose.

Question 3 · Chemistry of biomolecules · easy

The oxygen dissociation curve of haemoglobin is S-shaped, while that of myoglobin is a simple hyperbola.

What causes the S-shaped curve of haemoglobin?

  1. A. Haemoglobin has only one haem group
  2. B. Iron in haemoglobin is in the ferric (Fe3+) state
  3. C. Binding of one oxygen makes the other subunits bind oxygen more easily
  4. D. Haemoglobin binds oxygen more tightly than myoglobin at all pressures
  5. E. 2,3-BPG binds to oxygenated haemoglobin
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Binding of one oxygen makes the other subunits bind oxygen more easily

Haemoglobin A has four subunits (two alpha, two beta), each with a haem whose ferrous iron binds one oxygen. Deoxyhaemoglobin is in the taut (T) state, with low affinity. As each oxygen binds, the subunits shift towards the relaxed (R) state, raising the affinity of the remaining sites. This cooperativity gives the sigmoid curve: haemoglobin loads oxygen fully in the lungs and releases a lot in the tissues over a small fall in pressure. Myoglobin has one chain and one haem, so it has no cooperativity and a hyperbolic curve.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Haemoglobin has only one haem group: Haemoglobin has four haem groups, one in each subunit; myoglobin has one.
  • B. Iron in haemoglobin is in the ferric (Fe3+) state: Oxygen binds ferrous (Fe2+) iron; ferric iron forms methaemoglobin, which cannot carry oxygen.
  • D. Haemoglobin binds oxygen more tightly than myoglobin at all pressures: Myoglobin binds oxygen more tightly than haemoglobin, which is why it takes oxygen from it in muscle.
  • E. 2,3-BPG binds to oxygenated haemoglobin: 2,3-BPG binds deoxyhaemoglobin and lowers affinity; it does not make the curve S-shaped.

Key point: Haemoglobin binds oxygen cooperatively (T to R), giving a sigmoid dissociation curve.

Question 4 · Hormone action, metabolism in health and disease, clinical biochemistry · medium

A 58-year-old man in septic shock from a perforated bowel remains hypotensive at 80/45 mmHg despite fluids and noradrenaline. An infusion of vasopressin is added, and his blood pressure rises within an hour. The same hormone, at much lower levels in health, concentrates the urine.

Which pathways mediate the vascular and the renal effects?

  1. A. Vessels: V2, Gs and cAMP; kidney: V1, Gq and IP3
  2. B. Vessels: V1, Gq, IP3 and calcium; kidney: V2, Gs and cAMP
  3. C. Both act through a nuclear receptor that changes gene expression
  4. D. Both act through cGMP made by guanylyl cyclase
  5. E. Vessels: tyrosine kinase; kidney: JAK-STAT signalling
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Vessels: V1, Gq, IP3 and calcium; kidney: V2, Gs and cAMP

Vasopressin has V1 receptors on vascular smooth muscle (Gq: phospholipase C splits PIP2 into IP3 and DAG; IP3 releases calcium, causing contraction) and V2 receptors on collecting duct cells (Gs: cAMP and protein kinase A move aquaporin-2 channels into the apical membrane). Other Gq hormones include oxytocin, alpha-1 agonists, angiotensin II, GnRH, TRH and histamine H1. Other Gs hormones include PTH, TSH, ACTH, LH, FSH, glucagon, calcitonin and beta-agonists. A simple FCPS approach is to learn the second messenger for each receptor rather than for each hormone, because some hormones use more than one.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Vessels: V2, Gs and cAMP; kidney: V1, Gq and IP3: The receptors are reversed: V1 on vessels uses Gq, while V2 in the collecting duct uses Gs and cAMP.
  • C. Both act through a nuclear receptor that changes gene expression: Vasopressin is a peptide acting on surface G protein-coupled receptors, not a nuclear receptor.
  • D. Both act through cGMP made by guanylyl cyclase: Raising cGMP through guanylyl cyclase is how ANP and nitric oxide act, and they relax vessels.
  • E. Vessels: tyrosine kinase; kidney: JAK-STAT signalling: Neither vasopressin receptor is a tyrosine kinase or JAK-linked; both are G protein-coupled.

Key point: Vasopressin acts via V1 (Gq, IP3 and calcium) in vessels and via V2 (Gs, cAMP, aquaporin-2) in the kidney.

Question 5 · Enzymes, bioenergetics and free radicals · easy

A 40-year-old man drinks car antifreeze in a suicide attempt. He appears drunk at first without smelling of alcohol. Over the next day he develops deep breathing and a high anion gap acidosis with a high osmolar gap, then acute kidney injury. His calcium is 7.1 mg/dL. Urine microscopy shows envelope-shaped crystals.

Which metabolite causes the kidney damage?

  1. A. Formic acid
  2. B. Acetaldehyde from the liver
  3. C. Uric acid from cell breakdown
  4. D. Lactic acid alone
  5. E. Oxalate
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Oxalate

Ethylene glycol is converted by alcohol dehydrogenase to glycolaldehyde, then to glycolic acid, glyoxylic acid and oxalic acid. The acids cause a high anion gap metabolic acidosis, and oxalate combines with calcium to form calcium oxalate crystals that block and damage the kidney tubules, causing acute kidney injury and low calcium. Early on there is also a raised osmolal gap. Treatment is fomepizole or ethanol to block alcohol dehydrogenase, bicarbonate, and haemodialysis. Thiamine and pyridoxine help divert glyoxylate to harmless products.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Formic acid: Formic acid comes from methanol and damages the optic nerve; it does not form crystals in the kidney.
  • B. Acetaldehyde from the liver: Acetaldehyde comes from ethanol and causes flushing and nausea, not crystals in the tubules.
  • C. Uric acid from cell breakdown: Urate crystals block tubules in tumour lysis after chemotherapy, not after antifreeze.
  • D. Lactic acid alone: Lactate adds to the acidosis but forms no crystals and does not lower calcium.

Key point: Ethylene glycol is metabolised to oxalate, forming calcium oxalate crystals and acute kidney injury.

Question 6 · Lipid metabolism and lipoproteins · easy

A 40-year-old obese woman with four children, who recently lost weight quickly on a crash diet, has repeated pain under the right ribs after fatty meals, spreading to her shoulder blade. Ultrasound shows gallstones, which turn out to be mostly cholesterol. Her bile is supersaturated with cholesterol.

Which situation favours cholesterol gallstones?

  1. A. Too much bile acid relative to cholesterol in bile
  2. B. Increased activity of 7-alpha-hydroxylase
  3. C. High haemoglobin breakdown
  4. D. Frequent emptying of the gallbladder
  5. E. Cholesterol-supersaturated bile
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Cholesterol-supersaturated bile

Cholesterol is insoluble in water and is kept in solution in bile by bile acids and phospholipid (lecithin) in mixed micelles. Bile acids are made from cholesterol in the liver; 7-alpha-hydroxylase is the rate-limiting step. If there is too much cholesterol relative to bile acids and lecithin (obesity, oestrogen, rapid weight loss, ileal disease losing bile acids, some drugs), bile becomes supersaturated, cholesterol crystallises and stones form, especially with gallbladder stasis. Pigment stones (calcium bilirubinate) follow chronic haemolysis or infection.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Too much bile acid relative to cholesterol in bile: More bile acid relative to cholesterol keeps cholesterol dissolved and protects against stones.
  • B. Increased activity of 7-alpha-hydroxylase: More 7-alpha-hydroxylase activity turns more cholesterol into bile acid, lowering the risk.
  • C. High haemoglobin breakdown: High haemoglobin breakdown, as in haemolysis, favours black pigment stones, not cholesterol stones.
  • D. Frequent emptying of the gallbladder: Stasis from rapid weight loss or pregnancy promotes stones; frequent emptying protects.

Key point: Cholesterol gallstones form when bile is supersaturated with cholesterol relative to bile acids and lecithin.

Question 7 · Cell and molecular biology, genetics · easy

A 30-year-old woman has breast cancer. Her brother had a sarcoma at 16, her mother had a brain tumour at 40, and her nephew had an adrenocortical tumour at 2. She has a germline mutation in TP53. Her oncologist avoids radiotherapy if possible, worried about new tumours.

What does normal p53 do when DNA is damaged?

  1. A. It speeds up the cell cycle so damage is diluted
  2. B. It phosphorylates Rb to release E2F
  3. C. It lengthens the telomeres
  4. D. It removes damaged bases directly
  5. E. It halts the cycle in G1 or triggers apoptosis
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. It halts the cycle in G1 or triggers apoptosis

p53 is a tumour suppressor, called the guardian of the genome. When DNA is damaged, p53 rises and switches on p21, which inhibits cyclin-dependent kinases, so Rb is not phosphorylated and the cell stays in G1. This gives time for repair. If the damage cannot be repaired, p53 triggers apoptosis (through BAX). Loss of p53 lets damaged cells divide. TP53 is mutated in about half of all cancers. Inherited mutation causes Li-Fraumeni syndrome, with early sarcomas, breast cancer, brain tumours, leukaemia and adrenal cancers.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. It speeds up the cell cycle so damage is diluted: p53 slows the cell cycle to allow repair; it does not speed it up.
  • B. It phosphorylates Rb to release E2F: Phosphorylating Rb is done by cyclin D-CDK4/6 to push cells into S phase; p53 opposes this through p21.
  • C. It lengthens the telomeres: Telomere lengthening is done by telomerase, not p53.
  • D. It removes damaged bases directly: Damaged bases are removed by repair enzymes; p53 coordinates the response rather than repairing DNA itself.

Key point: p53 arrests the cell in G1 via p21 and triggers apoptosis; germline loss causes Li-Fraumeni syndrome.

Question 8 · Nutrition, vitamins and minerals · easy

In a district of Thar where families drink water from deep tube wells, many children have chalky white and brown mottled, pitted teeth, while children in a nearby town on piped river water do not. Some older adults have stiff spines, and X-rays show dense bones with calcified ligaments.

What is the cause?

  1. A. Dental fluorosis
  2. B. Fluoride deficiency in the water
  3. C. Tetracycline staining of enamel
  4. D. Enamel hypoplasia from rickets
  5. E. Chronic lead poisoning
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Dental fluorosis

Fluoride at about 1 mg/L in drinking water strengthens enamel (forming fluorapatite) and reduces dental caries. Excess intake while teeth are forming causes dental fluorosis: chalky white spots, brown mottling and pitting of enamel. Very high long-term intake causes skeletal fluorosis, with dense bones, calcified ligaments, joint stiffness and nerve compression. High groundwater fluoride is a problem in parts of Pakistan, India and Africa; treatment of water to remove fluoride or alternative sources are the remedy.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Fluoride deficiency in the water: Too little fluoride raises tooth decay but does not mottle teeth or calcify ligaments.
  • C. Tetracycline staining of enamel: Tetracycline staining gives grey-yellow bands and is linked to childhood drug use, not drinking water.
  • D. Enamel hypoplasia from rickets: Rickets causes enamel defects with bowed legs, but not dense bones with calcified ligaments.
  • E. Chronic lead poisoning: Lead poisoning causes anaemia, abdominal pain and gum lines, not mottled enamel.

Key point: Optimal fluoride prevents caries; excess causes dental and skeletal fluorosis.

Question 9 · Amino acids, haem and nucleotides · easy

A 3-year-old boy, whose mother applies traditional surma (kohl) to his eyes every day, has had abdominal pain, constipation and irritability for months. He is pale; his haemoglobin is 9.1 g/dL with an MCV of 68 fL. The blood film shows coarse dots in the red cells. His free red cell protoporphyrin is high, and urine aminolevulinic acid is raised.

Which enzymes of haem synthesis does lead inhibit?

  1. A. ALA synthase and PBG deaminase
  2. B. ALA dehydratase and ferrochelatase
  3. C. Uroporphyrinogen decarboxylase only
  4. D. Tyrosine hydroxylase
  5. E. Haem oxygenase
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. ALA dehydratase and ferrochelatase

Lead binds thiol groups and inhibits ALA dehydratase (so ALA rises in urine) and ferrochelatase (so zinc protoporphyrin builds up in red cells). The result is a microcytic anaemia with basophilic stippling (from inhibition of pyrimidine 5'-nucleotidase) and ring sideroblasts. Children develop abdominal colic, constipation, irritability, learning problems and, at high levels, encephalopathy; adults may have wrist drop, a blue line on the gums and kidney damage. Sources include old paint, batteries, some traditional cosmetics such as surma and kohl, and contaminated spices. Treatment is removing the source and chelation (succimer, edetate calcium disodium, dimercaprol).

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. ALA synthase and PBG deaminase: Lead largely spares ALA synthase and PBG deaminase; its main targets are ALA dehydratase and ferrochelatase.
  • C. Uroporphyrinogen decarboxylase only: Uroporphyrinogen decarboxylase is the enzyme in porphyria cutanea tarda; lead mainly blocks ALA dehydratase and ferrochelatase.
  • D. Tyrosine hydroxylase: Tyrosine hydroxylase makes dopamine and is not part of haem synthesis.
  • E. Haem oxygenase: Haem oxygenase breaks haem down to biliverdin; it is not part of haem synthesis.

Key point: Lead inhibits ALA dehydratase and ferrochelatase, causing anaemia with basophilic stippling; surma can be a source.

Question 10 · Carbohydrate metabolism · medium

During a sudden fright, both glucagon and adrenaline rise. Liver glycogen is broken down to raise blood glucose, while muscle glycogen is broken down only for the muscle's own use.

Why does muscle glycogen respond to adrenaline but not to glucagon?

  1. A. Muscle has beta receptors but few glucagon receptors
  2. B. Muscle glycogen is a different molecule from liver glycogen
  3. C. Glucagon cannot leave the blood vessels in muscle
  4. D. Muscle phosphorylase is inhibited by glucagon
  5. E. Adrenaline converts muscle glycogen into blood glucose directly
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Muscle has beta receptors but few glucagon receptors

Tissue responses to hormones depend on receptor expression. The liver has glucagon receptors and beta- and alpha-adrenergic receptors; both hormones activate glycogen phosphorylase (through cAMP and protein kinase A, and calcium), and glucose-6-phosphatase allows free glucose to be released into the blood. Skeletal muscle has beta-2 adrenergic receptors but very few glucagon receptors, so adrenaline (and calcium from contraction, and AMP) stimulates glycogen breakdown for the muscle's own glycolysis. Muscle lacks glucose-6-phosphatase, so it cannot release glucose; it supplies the liver indirectly through lactate and alanine. Glucagon's role is mainly to maintain blood glucose between meals.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Muscle glycogen is a different molecule from liver glycogen: Muscle and liver glycogen have the same structure; the difference lies in receptors and glucose-6-phosphatase.
  • C. Glucagon cannot leave the blood vessels in muscle: Glucagon is a small peptide that reaches muscle easily; muscle simply has few glucagon receptors.
  • D. Muscle phosphorylase is inhibited by glucagon: Glucagon does not inhibit muscle phosphorylase; it has almost no effect on muscle.
  • E. Adrenaline converts muscle glycogen into blood glucose directly: Muscle lacks glucose-6-phosphatase, so it cannot turn its glycogen into blood glucose.

Key point: Muscle responds to adrenaline (beta-2 receptors) but not glucagon; it uses its glycogen only for itself.
