Question 1 · Developmental disturbances of teeth, jaws and oral tissues · easy

A 38-year-old man attends a dental hospital in Kohat for extraction of a grossly carious, non-restorable upper left second molar (27). The upper left third molar (28) is unerupted. During forceps delivery of 27, unexpected resistance is felt and both 27 and the unerupted 28 are delivered together as a single mass. Gross examination and decalcified histological sections of the specimen show that both teeth have completely separate dentine and pulp chambers, and their roots are united solely by a thick layer of secondary cellular cementum.

What is the pathological diagnosis of this root union?

  1. A. Ankylosis
  2. B. Concrescence
  3. C. Enameloma
  4. D. Fusion
  5. E. Hypercementosis
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Concrescence

Concrescence is a form of union in which the roots of two adjacent teeth are joined by cementum alone, without any confluence of their underlying dentine. It occurs most frequently in the posterior maxilla, uniting a permanent second molar with an impacted or crowded third molar. True (developmental) concrescence occurs during root formation when lack of interdental bone allows the dental follicles to approximate and share cementoblasts. Acquired concrescence occurs after root completion, stimulated by chronic periapical inflammation or crowding that resorbs the interseptal bone and induces hypercementosis. Recognising overlapping molar roots on pre-extraction radiographs prevents inadvertent fracture of the maxillary tuberosity or tearing of the sinus floor during extraction.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Ankylosis: Ankylosis is the direct anatomical fusion of root cementum or dentine with the surrounding alveolar bone, not with an adjacent tooth root.
  • C. Enameloma: An enameloma is a small ectopic nodule of enamel at the root furcation and does not bind two adjacent molar roots together.
  • D. Fusion: True fusion requires confluence of the dentine of two developing tooth germs, whereas these teeth are joined exclusively by cementum.
  • E. Hypercementosis: Isolated hypercementosis produces bulbous thickening of a single root without uniting it to the root of a neighbouring tooth.

Key point: Define concrescence as the cemental union of two adjacent tooth roots without dentinal confluence, most commonly affecting maxillary molars.

Question 2 · Bone, soft-tissue and salivary gland pathology · easy

A 27-year-old woman who is 24 weeks pregnant attends a dental clinic in Mardan with a soft, red, lobulated lump on the gingiva between her upper right central and lateral incisors, 1 cm across. It grew over six weeks, bleeds easily when touched and is not painful. She has generalised gingivitis with heavy plaque. Radiographs show no bone involvement.

What is the most appropriate management?

  1. A. Urgent excision and biopsy to exclude cancer
  2. B. Antibiotics for two weeks
  3. C. Plaque control, review after delivery
  4. D. Extraction of the adjacent incisors
  5. E. Radiotherapy
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Plaque control, review after delivery

Pyogenic granuloma is a common reactive proliferation of capillaries in response to local irritation, such as plaque, calculus or trauma. It is neither pyogenic nor a true granuloma. It appears as a soft, red, often lobulated or pedunculated mass that bleeds easily, most often on the gingiva. Hormonal changes in pregnancy enhance the vascular response to plaque, so pregnancy epulis (granuloma gravidarum) is common in the second and third trimesters. Histology shows lobules of proliferating capillaries in an oedematous, inflamed stroma, often with surface ulceration. Management in pregnancy is plaque control and observation, because many lesions shrink after delivery; persistent lesions are excised with removal of irritants, and the specimen is examined.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Urgent excision and biopsy to exclude cancer: An urgent biopsy is not needed for a typical pregnancy epulis; excision is delayed unless it bleeds severely or interferes with eating.
  • B. Antibiotics for two weeks: Antibiotics do not treat a pyogenic granuloma.
  • D. Extraction of the adjacent incisors: The teeth are healthy and should not be extracted for a gingival lesion.
  • E. Radiotherapy: Radiotherapy has no role for a benign reactive lesion.

Key point: Manage a pregnancy-associated pyogenic granuloma with plaque control and review.

Question 3 · Cysts of the jaws and odontogenic tumours · easy

A dental student in Lahore reviews 200 consecutive jaw cyst specimens from a hospital pathology archive. She finds that one type far outnumbers all the others combined, and that it was almost always found at the apex of a tooth that did not respond to vitality testing.

Which cyst is most common?

  1. A. Dentigerous cyst
  2. B. Odontogenic keratocyst
  3. C. Nasopalatine duct cyst
  4. D. Radicular cyst
  5. E. Lateral periodontal cyst
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. Radicular cyst

Radicular (periapical) cysts account for roughly half or more of all jaw cysts. They are inflammatory odontogenic cysts that develop from the epithelial rests of Malassez in a periapical granuloma at the apex of a non-vital tooth. Dentigerous cysts are the commonest developmental odontogenic cysts and the second commonest jaw cysts overall, followed by odontogenic keratocysts. The WHO classification (2022) groups jaw cysts into odontogenic cysts of inflammatory origin, odontogenic developmental cysts, and non-odontogenic cysts, with pseudocysts (lacking an epithelial lining) considered separately.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Dentigerous cyst: Dentigerous cysts are the commonest developmental odontogenic cyst, but second in overall frequency.
  • B. Odontogenic keratocyst: Keratocysts are important because they recur, but they are less common than radicular cysts.
  • C. Nasopalatine duct cyst: The nasopalatine duct cyst is the commonest non-odontogenic cyst, but much less common than radicular cysts.
  • E. Lateral periodontal cyst: Lateral periodontal cysts are uncommon and occur beside vital teeth.

Key point: State that the radicular cyst is the commonest cyst of the jaws and how jaw cysts are classified.

Question 4 · Epithelial pathology, potentially malignant disorders and oral cancer · easy

A 34-year-old man attends a dental clinic in Rawalpindi with a painless white growth on his soft palate that he noticed two months ago. It is 6 mm across, pedunculated, and has many finger-like projections giving a cauliflower appearance. There is no other lesion in the mouth or on the genitals, and no neck lymph nodes. Excision biopsy shows papillary fronds of keratinised stratified squamous epithelium supported by thin fibrovascular cores, with some koilocytes.

Which virus types are most often found in this lesion?

  1. A. HPV 16 and 18
  2. B. Epstein-Barr virus
  3. C. HPV 6 and 11
  4. D. Herpes simplex virus 1
  5. E. Human herpesvirus 8
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. HPV 6 and 11

Squamous papilloma is a common benign proliferation of the oral epithelium, most often on the soft palate, tongue and lips. It is a pedunculated or sessile exophytic lesion with finger-like or cauliflower projections, white if heavily keratinised. Histology shows papillary fronds of stratified squamous epithelium around thin fibrovascular cores; koilocytes (cells with clear cytoplasm and shrunken nuclei) may be seen. It is associated with low-risk HPV types 6 and 11, and has very low infectivity. Treatment is conservative excision including the base; recurrence is uncommon. Multiple broad-based lesions suggest condyloma acuminatum or focal epithelial hyperplasia.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. HPV 16 and 18: HPV 16 and 18 are high-risk types linked to oropharyngeal and cervical cancer, not to benign oral squamous papilloma.
  • B. Epstein-Barr virus: EBV causes oral hairy leukoplakia and some lymphomas, not papillary squamous growths with koilocytes.
  • D. Herpes simplex virus 1: HSV causes vesicles and ulcers that heal, not a persistent papillary growth.
  • E. Human herpesvirus 8: HHV-8 causes Kaposi sarcoma, a purple vascular lesion, not a white papillary growth.

Key point: Identify oral squamous papilloma and its association with low-risk HPV types 6 and 11.

Question 5 · Caries, pulp and periapical disease, and oral infections · easy

A 3-year-old girl from Sukkur is brought to a dental clinic with brown, broken upper front teeth. The upper incisors are severely decayed on their labial and palatal surfaces, the upper and lower first primary molars are also decayed, but the lower incisors are almost free of caries. She falls asleep every night with a bottle of sweetened milk.

Why are the lower incisors spared?

  1. A. The lower incisors erupt later and have had less exposure
  2. B. The lower incisors have thicker enamel
  3. C. Tongue and pooled saliva cover them
  4. D. The lower incisors are not exposed to sugar at all
  5. E. Fluoride in the bottle protects only the lower incisors
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Tongue and pooled saliva cover them

Early childhood caries (previously called nursing bottle caries) is caused by frequent and prolonged exposure to sugary drinks, especially from a bottle at night when salivary flow is low. The pattern is characteristic: the upper incisors are affected first and most severely, followed by the first primary molars, then the canines, while the lower incisors are usually spared because the tongue covers them during sucking and saliva from the submandibular and sublingual ducts in the floor of the mouth pools around them. Prevention includes not putting children to bed with sugary drinks, using a cup by about one year, brushing with fluoride toothpaste as soon as teeth erupt, and fluoride varnish.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. The lower incisors erupt later and have had less exposure: The lower incisors usually erupt before the upper incisors, so this does not explain sparing.
  • B. The lower incisors have thicker enamel: Primary lower incisors do not have thicker enamel than the upper incisors.
  • D. The lower incisors are not exposed to sugar at all: The milk reaches all teeth, but the lower incisors are protected by the tongue and saliva.
  • E. Fluoride in the bottle protects only the lower incisors: Fluoride would protect all teeth, and none is mentioned here.

Key point: Explain the pattern of early childhood caries and why the lower incisors are spared.

Question 6 · Immunological, skin, blood and metabolic disease; injuries and forensic odontology · easy

A 22-year-old university student in Lahore has had crops of two or three painful, round, shallow ulcers, 4-6 mm across, on her inner lips and tongue tip every month since she was 14. Each has a yellow-grey base and a red halo and heals in about 10 days without scarring. She has no genital ulcers, eye problems, skin rash or bowel symptoms. Full blood count, ferritin, B12, folate and coeliac antibodies are normal.

What is the most likely diagnosis?

  1. A. Minor aphthous ulcers
  2. B. Behçet disease
  3. C. Recurrent intraoral herpes
  4. D. Pemphigus vulgaris
  5. E. Squamous cell carcinoma
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Minor aphthous ulcers

Recurrent aphthous stomatitis is the commonest cause of recurrent oral ulceration, usually starting in childhood or adolescence. Minor aphthae (about 80%) are round or oval ulcers under 1 cm with a yellow-grey base and red halo, on non-keratinised mucosa (lips, buccal mucosa, tongue tip, floor of mouth), healing in 7-14 days without scarring. Major aphthae are larger, deeper, last weeks and scar; herpetiform aphthae are crops of many tiny ulcers. The cause is unknown; contributing factors include genetics, stress, trauma, hormonal changes and haematinic deficiency. Systemic causes (iron, B12, folate deficiency, coeliac disease, Crohn disease, Behçet disease, HIV) should be excluded. Treatment is symptomatic: topical anaesthetics, chlorhexidine and topical corticosteroids.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Behçet disease: Behçet disease requires recurrent oral ulcers plus other features such as genital ulcers or eye disease, which she does not have.
  • C. Recurrent intraoral herpes: Recurrent intraoral herpes affects keratinised mucosa (hard palate, gingiva) as clusters of tiny vesicles, not round ulcers on the inner lips.
  • D. Pemphigus vulgaris: Pemphigus causes persistent erosions that do not heal in 10 days.
  • E. Squamous cell carcinoma: Carcinoma is a single persistent ulcer, not recurrent crops that heal.

Key point: Diagnose minor recurrent aphthous stomatitis and exclude systemic causes.

Question 7 · Developmental disturbances of teeth, jaws and oral tissues · medium

A 16-year-old girl attends a dental clinic in Swat worried about spaces between her upper front teeth. Her father and older sister have similar spacing and missing back teeth. She has normal hair, nails, and sweating, and has never had dental extractions. Intraoral examination shows microdontia of the remaining incisors and retained primary molars. A dental panoramic tomogram reveals the congenital absence of eight permanent teeth (18, 15, 12, 22, 25, 28, 35, and 45).

Which gene is most often linked to this non-syndromic hypodontia?

  1. A. AMELX
  2. B. DSPP
  3. C. GNAS1
  4. D. MSX1
  5. E. PTCH1
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. MSX1

Oligodontia is defined as the developmental absence of six or more teeth, excluding third molars, whereas hypodontia refers to fewer than six missing teeth. Non-syndromic familial tooth agenesis is most commonly linked to autosomal dominant loss-of-function mutations in the homeobox gene MSX1 (particularly affecting second premolars and third molars) or the paired-box transcription factor PAX9 (predominantly affecting permanent molars). WNT10A and AXIN2 mutations are also implicated; AXIN2 carries an added predisposition to colorectal polyposis. Because this patient has normal hair, nails, and sweat glands, ectodermal dysplasia (EDA1 mutation) is excluded. Recognising the genetic basis allows the dentist to counsel the family and plan multidisciplinary orthodontic and prosthodontic care.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. AMELX: AMELX mutations cause X-linked amelogenesis imperfecta with defective enamel matrix formation rather than congenital absence of multiple permanent tooth germs.
  • B. DSPP: DSPP mutations cause dentinogenesis imperfecta and dentine dysplasia with obliterated pulps, not agenesis of premolars and lateral incisors.
  • C. GNAS1: Postzygotic GNAS1 mutations cause fibrous dysplasia and McCune-Albright syndrome, presenting with ground-glass jaw expansion rather than missing teeth.
  • E. PTCH1: PTCH1 tumour suppressor mutations cause Gorlin syndrome with multiple odontogenic keratocysts, not isolated familial tooth agenesis.

Key point: Associate non-syndromic familial oligodontia of premolars and incisors with mutations in the MSX1 and PAX9 transcription factor genes.

Question 8 · Bone, soft-tissue and salivary gland pathology · easy

A 42-year-old man attends a dental clinic in Lahore with a painless, firm, smooth, pink lump on his left buccal mucosa along the line where his teeth meet. It is 8 mm across, sessile, and has been present for two years without change. He admits to biting his cheek when he chews. Excision shows dense, poorly cellular collagen covered by thin stratified squamous epithelium.

What is the diagnosis?

  1. A. Fibroepithelial polyp
  2. B. Lipoma of the cheek
  3. C. Pyogenic granuloma
  4. D. Neurofibroma of the cheek
  5. E. Mucocele
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Fibroepithelial polyp

The fibroepithelial polyp (irritation or traumatic fibroma) is the commonest benign soft-tissue swelling of the mouth. It is a reactive hyperplasia of fibrous connective tissue in response to chronic trauma such as cheek or lip biting, sharp teeth or dentures. It forms a smooth, firm, pink, sessile or pedunculated nodule, most often on the buccal mucosa along the occlusal line, the lips or the tongue. Histology shows dense, relatively avascular collagen covered by stratified squamous epithelium, which may be keratotic. Treatment is conservative excision with removal of the cause; recurrence is uncommon. A similar lesion under a denture flange is a denture-induced granuloma (epulis fissuratum).

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Lipoma of the cheek: A lipoma is soft and yellowish, made of fat, not dense collagen.
  • C. Pyogenic granuloma: A pyogenic granuloma is red, soft and bleeds easily, with many capillaries.
  • D. Neurofibroma of the cheek: A neurofibroma contains spindle cells with wavy nuclei and is not linked to cheek biting.
  • E. Mucocele: A mucocele is a soft, bluish, fluid-filled swelling, usually of the lower lip.

Key point: Recognise a fibroepithelial polyp as a reactive fibrous hyperplasia from chronic trauma.

Question 9 · Cysts of the jaws and odontogenic tumours · easy

A 35-year-old man attends a dental clinic in Faisalabad for a routine check. His upper left lateral incisor (22) is discoloured, had a large restoration years ago, and does not respond to cold or electric pulp testing. A periapical radiograph shows a round, well-defined radiolucency, 15 mm across, with a thin corticated margin, continuous with the periodontal ligament space at the apex. The adjacent teeth are vital.

From which epithelium does this cyst arise?

  1. A. Reduced enamel epithelium
  2. B. Rests of Serres in the gingiva
  3. C. Epithelial rests of Malassez
  4. D. Nasopalatine duct remnants
  5. E. Oral surface epithelium
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Epithelial rests of Malassez

Radicular cysts develop at the apex of a non-vital tooth from the epithelial rests of Malassez, remnants of Hertwig's epithelial root sheath in the periodontal ligament. Bacterial products and inflammation from the necrotic pulp stimulate the rests to proliferate within a periapical granuloma; the central cells degenerate, forming a cavity lined by non-keratinised stratified squamous epithelium, often with arcading pattern, Rushton hyaline bodies, cholesterol clefts and inflammation in the wall. Radiographically, a round, well-defined radiolucency continuous with the apical periodontal ligament of a non-vital tooth is typical. Treatment is root canal treatment, with apical surgery or enucleation for persistent or large lesions, or extraction.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Reduced enamel epithelium: Reduced enamel epithelium gives rise to dentigerous cysts around unerupted crowns.
  • B. Rests of Serres in the gingiva: Rests of Serres (dental lamina remnants) give rise to lateral periodontal and gingival cysts and keratocysts.
  • D. Nasopalatine duct remnants: Nasopalatine duct remnants form cysts in the incisive canal, between the central incisors, with vital teeth.
  • E. Oral surface epithelium: Surface epithelium does not normally form periapical cysts.

Key point: Identify the epithelial rests of Malassez as the origin of radicular cysts.

Question 10 · Epithelial pathology, potentially malignant disorders and oral cancer · easy

A 46-year-old non-smoker attends a dental clinic in Hyderabad with a white patch on the right lateral border of her tongue. It is rough, cannot be wiped off and lies exactly against the sharp edge of a fractured lower right first molar (46). She has no other mucosal lesions. The dentist smooths the fractured cusp and reviews her three weeks later.

What finding at review best supports frictional keratosis?

  1. A. The patch is unchanged in size
  2. B. The patch has turned red and ulcerated
  3. C. The patch now involves both sides of the tongue
  4. D. The patch has faded away
  5. E. The patch can now be wiped off
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. The patch has faded away

Frictional keratosis is a white patch of thickened keratin (hyperkeratosis) caused by chronic rubbing, such as from a sharp tooth, an ill-fitting denture, cheek biting or chewing on edentulous ridges. It is reactive and not considered potentially malignant. The key diagnostic test is to remove the cause and review after 2-4 weeks: the lesion should fade or disappear. If it persists, it should be classified as leukoplakia and biopsied, because some apparently frictional lesions on high-risk sites, such as the lateral tongue, are actually dysplastic. Histology of frictional keratosis shows hyperkeratosis and acanthosis without dysplasia.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. The patch is unchanged in size: An unchanged patch after the cause is removed should be biopsied, as it may be leukoplakia.
  • B. The patch has turned red and ulcerated: A red or ulcerated change raises concern for dysplasia or carcinoma and needs urgent biopsy.
  • C. The patch now involves both sides of the tongue: Spread to the other side would not be explained by one sharp tooth and suggests another cause.
  • E. The patch can now be wiped off: A patch that wipes off suggests pseudomembranous candidosis, not keratosis.

Key point: Use removal of the cause and review to distinguish frictional keratosis from leukoplakia.
