Question 1 · Antibacterial drugs · medium

A 10-year-old boy from Mingora has had three days of fever and refusal to bear weight, with severe pain over the right distal femur. MRI shows acute osteomyelitis and a bone aspirate grows Staphylococcus aureus sensitive to clindamycin and meticillin. He developed generalised urticaria and wheeze after amoxicillin last year. Temperature is 38.5 °C, he is haemodynamically stable and he will take medicines by mouth once the initial intravenous phase is complete.

Which pharmacokinetic property best supports the use of clindamycin in this infection?

  1. A. Excretion unchanged in the urine at high concentration
  2. B. Free passage across an intact blood-brain barrier
  3. C. Poor oral absorption, giving higher levels after infusion
  4. D. A half-life of several days that allows weekly dosing
  5. E. Good penetration into bone and abscess fluid
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Good penetration into bone and abscess fluid

Clindamycin binds the 50S ribosomal subunit and blocks peptide chain elongation; it is bacteriostatic against most organisms and also suppresses staphylococcal and streptococcal toxin production. It is almost fully absorbed orally and penetrates bone, abscesses and macrophages well, so it is a first-line option for staphylococcal osteomyelitis and for skin and soft-tissue infection in penicillin-allergic patients, where it is not cross-reactive with penicillins. It has no activity against enterococci or gram-negative aerobes, and CSF levels are low. The main adverse effect is Clostridioides difficile infection, which is the main reason to avoid prolonged courses. Check the isolate for inducible resistance by the D-test, since erythromycin-resistant staphylococci may fail on treatment. Take oral capsules with a full glass of water.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Excretion unchanged in the urine at high concentration: Clindamycin is mostly metabolised by the liver, and urinary excretion is irrelevant to osteomyelitis.
  • B. Free passage across an intact blood-brain barrier: Clindamycin penetrates the CSF poorly, and this has no bearing on a femoral infection.
  • C. Poor oral absorption, giving higher levels after infusion: Oral absorption of clindamycin is excellent (about 90 percent), which allows the switch to oral therapy.
  • D. A half-life of several days that allows weekly dosing: Its half-life is about 2 to 3 hours, so it is given three or four times daily.

Key point: Relate the bone and abscess penetration of clindamycin to its use in staphylococcal osteomyelitis.

Question 2 · Antifungal drugs · medium

A 22-year-old woman with tinea capitis caught from her young nephew takes griseofulvin 500 mg daily with fatty meals for eight weeks. She also takes a combined oral contraceptive pill correctly. Three weeks into treatment she has breakthrough bleeding, and a month later her pregnancy test is positive.

Which property of griseofulvin explains this?

  1. A. It inhibits CYP3A4
  2. B. It binds hormones in the gut
  3. C. It blocks oestrogen receptors
  4. D. It causes vomiting
  5. E. It induces liver enzymes
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. It induces liver enzymes

Griseofulvin binds fungal microtubules and disrupts the mitotic spindle; it is deposited in newly formed keratin, making it resistant to fungal invasion, so treatment lasts weeks to months while infected hair or skin grows out. Absorption improves with a fatty meal. It induces hepatic CYP enzymes, lowering levels of combined oral contraceptives and warfarin, so additional contraception is needed during treatment and for a month afterwards. It is also teratogenic (and aneugenic), so pregnancy must be avoided during treatment and for a month after, and men should avoid fathering a child for 6 months afterwards. Other effects are headache, photosensitivity and disulfiram-like reactions with alcohol. Terbinafine is now often preferred.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. It inhibits CYP3A4: Would raise hormone levels.
  • B. It binds hormones in the gut: Griseofulvin does not bind hormones in the gut; it induces their liver metabolism.
  • C. It blocks oestrogen receptors: Griseofulvin does not block oestrogen receptors.
  • D. It causes vomiting: Vomiting can reduce absorption, but she has not vomited; enzyme induction is the cause.

Key point: Griseofulvin induces liver enzymes (contraceptive failure) and is teratogenic; it works by binding fungal microtubules.

Question 3 · Autonomic drugs · medium

For a rapid-sequence intubation, a 35-year-old man receives intravenous suxamethonium. Within 30 seconds, fine twitching ripples over his chest and limbs, then he becomes completely limp. Train-of-four stimulation shows four weak but equal twitches, with no fade.

Which statement about this block is correct?

  1. A. Neostigmine will reverse it quickly
  2. B. It is caused by competition with acetylcholine
  3. C. It is a phase I depolarising block
  4. D. Sugammadex will reverse it
  5. E. It will last several hours in most people
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. It is a phase I depolarising block

Suxamethonium is a nicotinic agonist that is not broken down by acetylcholinesterase. It first opens the channels (fasciculations), then keeps the end-plate depolarised so voltage-gated sodium channels nearby stay inactivated and the muscle cannot respond: phase I (depolarising) block, with reduced but equal twitches on train-of-four and no fade or post-tetanic potentiation. Cholinesterase inhibitors deepen this block. With prolonged exposure or repeated doses, a phase II block develops that resembles non-depolarising block (fade). It is hydrolysed by plasma cholinesterase in minutes. Side effects: hyperkalaemia, muscle pains, bradycardia (especially in children with repeat doses), raised intraocular and intragastric pressure, and malignant hyperthermia.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Neostigmine will reverse it quickly: It can prolong a depolarising block.
  • B. It is caused by competition with acetylcholine: That is a non-depolarising block.
  • D. Sugammadex will reverse it: Sugammadex binds rocuronium and vecuronium only.
  • E. It will last several hours in most people: A suxamethonium block usually lasts 5 to 10 minutes, unless plasma cholinesterase is deficient.

Key point: Suxamethonium causes a phase I depolarising block (fasciculations, no fade); cholinesterase inhibitors do not reverse it.

Question 4 · Antiprotozoal and anthelmintic drugs · medium

A 30-year-old man returns to Lahore after work in Nigeria and has fever, headache and vomiting. He is alert and can take tablets. A blood film shows Plasmodium falciparum with 1% parasitaemia and no features of severe malaria. He is treated with artemether-lumefantrine twice daily for three days, taken with fatty food.

How does artemether kill the parasites?

  1. A. Haem iron activates its endoperoxide bridge
  2. B. It inhibits dihydrofolate reductase
  3. C. It blocks haem polymerisation
  4. D. It kills liver hypnozoites
  5. E. It inhibits the mitochondrial electron chain
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Haem iron activates its endoperoxide bridge

Artemisinins (artesunate, artemether, dihydroartemisinin) contain an endoperoxide bridge. Inside the parasite, haem iron released from digested haemoglobin cleaves the bridge, generating free radicals that damage parasite proteins and membranes. They kill blood stages, including young ring forms, faster than any other antimalarial, but have very short half-lives, so they are combined with a longer-acting partner drug (lumefantrine, amodiaquine, piperaquine, mefloquine) in artemisinin-based combination therapy (ACT) to clear remaining parasites and protect against resistance. Lumefantrine absorption needs fat, so it is taken with food or milk. Partial artemisinin resistance has emerged in South-East Asia and East Africa.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. It inhibits dihydrofolate reductase: Inhibiting dihydrofolate reductase is the action of pyrimethamine and proguanil.
  • C. It blocks haem polymerisation: That is chloroquine's main action.
  • D. It kills liver hypnozoites: Killing liver hypnozoites is the action of primaquine or tafenoquine.
  • E. It inhibits the mitochondrial electron chain: Inhibiting the mitochondrial electron chain is the action of atovaquone.

Key point: Artemisinins are activated by haem iron to form free radicals; they are combined with a longer-acting partner drug (ACT).

Question 5 · Antiviral drugs · easy

A 62-year-old man from Chitral presents 36 hours after the onset of a painful vesicular rash in a band over the left chest wall. The vesicles are grouped on an erythematous base and do not cross the midline. He is otherwise well and his kidney function is normal. His doctor prescribes valaciclovir 1 g three times daily rather than oral aciclovir 800 mg five times daily, explaining that it produces better aciclovir levels with fewer doses.

Why does valaciclovir give higher plasma aciclovir levels than oral aciclovir?

  1. A. It is activated by viral thymidine kinase in the gut wall before absorption
  2. B. It is a valyl ester prodrug converted to aciclovir
  3. C. It is not renally excreted, so aciclovir accumulates
  4. D. It binds albumin and is released slowly into the circulation
  5. E. It inhibits intestinal enzymes that destroy aciclovir
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. It is a valyl ester prodrug converted to aciclovir

Aciclovir is a guanosine analogue. Viral thymidine kinase in infected cells phosphorylates it to the monophosphate, host kinases form the triphosphate, and aciclovir triphosphate inhibits viral DNA polymerase and terminates the chain. Oral bioavailability of aciclovir is only 15 to 30 percent, which is why five doses a day are needed. Valaciclovir is the L-valyl ester prodrug; it is transported by an intestinal peptide transporter and rapidly converted to aciclovir, giving three to five times higher exposure and allowing three-times-daily dosing for zoster. Antiviral treatment of herpes zoster should start within 72 hours of the rash to shorten disease and reduce pain. Adverse effects include nausea and headache; neurotoxicity (confusion, hallucinations) can occur with accumulation in renal impairment, so adjust the dose to eGFR.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. It is activated by viral thymidine kinase in the gut wall before absorption: Viral thymidine kinase works only inside infected cells; it does not act in the intestinal wall.
  • C. It is not renally excreted, so aciclovir accumulates: Aciclovir and its prodrug are cleared by the kidney, and doses must be reduced in renal impairment.
  • D. It binds albumin and is released slowly into the circulation: Protein binding of both drugs is low and does not explain the higher levels.
  • E. It inhibits intestinal enzymes that destroy aciclovir: Aciclovir is not broken down by intestinal enzymes; the issue is poor absorption.

Key point: Explain the pharmacokinetic advantage of valaciclovir over aciclovir.

Question 6 · Haematological drugs: anticoagulants, antiplatelets and haematinics · medium

A 56-year-old man in a district hospital in Balochistan has an anterior ST elevation myocardial infarction that began 2 hours ago. The nearest centre for primary angioplasty is 6 hours away. His blood pressure is 140/90 mmHg. While consenting him for thrombolysis, the doctor reviews his history for absolute contraindications.

Which history would be an absolute contraindication to thrombolysis?

  1. A. Stable angina for 5 years
  2. B. Brain haemorrhage 2 years ago
  3. C. Takes aspirin daily
  4. D. Type 2 diabetes with retinopathy
  5. E. Age over 55 years
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Brain haemorrhage 2 years ago

Thrombolytics (alteplase, tenecteplase, reteplase, streptokinase) convert plasminogen to plasmin, which breaks down fibrin in clots but can also cause bleeding anywhere. Absolute contraindications include any previous intracranial haemorrhage, ischaemic stroke in the past 3-6 months, known intracranial tumour or vascular malformation, active bleeding (excluding menses), suspected aortic dissection, and significant head or facial trauma in the past 3 months. Relative contraindications include uncontrolled hypertension (above 180/110 mmHg), recent major surgery, pregnancy, oral anticoagulation and non-compressible punctures. When primary PCI cannot be done within 120 minutes, thrombolysis within 12 hours of symptoms is recommended.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Stable angina for 5 years: Stable angina is not a contraindication to thrombolysis.
  • C. Takes aspirin daily: Taking aspirin daily does not contraindicate thrombolysis; aspirin is given with it.
  • D. Type 2 diabetes with retinopathy: Not an absolute contraindication.
  • E. Age over 55 years: Age over 55 is not a contraindication.

Key point: Previous intracranial haemorrhage is an absolute contraindication to thrombolysis.

Question 7 · CNS, psychiatric and anaesthetic drugs · medium

A 45-year-old man with alcohol dependence completed detoxification and was started on a tablet to help him stay abstinent. A week later at a wedding he drank one glass of whisky. Within 15 minutes he had flushing, throbbing headache, palpitations, nausea and vomiting, and felt faint with a blood pressure of 90/50 mmHg.

What is the mechanism of this reaction?

  1. A. Opioid receptors are blocked
  2. B. Acetaldehyde build-up
  3. C. Alcohol is metabolised faster
  4. D. Histamine release from an allergy
  5. E. GABA-A receptors are blocked
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Acetaldehyde build-up

Alcohol is oxidised to acetaldehyde by alcohol dehydrogenase, and acetaldehyde is then oxidised to acetate by aldehyde dehydrogenase (ALDH). Disulfiram irreversibly inhibits ALDH, so after even a small amount of alcohol acetaldehyde accumulates, causing flushing, headache, nausea, vomiting, palpitations, hypotension and, rarely, arrhythmias or collapse. The unpleasant reaction is meant to deter drinking, and it can occur for up to 2 weeks after the last dose. Patients must avoid alcohol in foods, mouthwashes and medicines. Similar reactions occur with metronidazole, some cephalosporins and sulfonylureas. Disulfiram is avoided in heart disease and psychosis.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Opioid receptors are blocked: That is naltrexone; no such reaction.
  • C. Alcohol is metabolised faster: Disulfiram does not speed alcohol metabolism; it blocks acetaldehyde breakdown.
  • D. Histamine release from an allergy: This is not allergy; acetaldehyde builds up.
  • E. GABA-A receptors are blocked: Disulfiram does not block GABA-A receptors.

Key point: Disulfiram inhibits aldehyde dehydrogenase; alcohol then causes acetaldehyde build-up with flushing, vomiting and hypotension.

Question 8 · Cardiovascular drugs · medium

A 44-year-old man with poorly controlled diabetes who drinks heavily has fasting triglycerides of 950 mg/dL and an LDL of 90 mg/dL. He had pancreatitis last year. Along with stopping alcohol and better glucose control, he is started on a drug that activates PPAR-alpha and increases lipoprotein lipase activity.

Which drug is this?

  1. A. Atorvastatin
  2. B. Ezetimibe
  3. C. Colestyramine
  4. D. Pioglitazone
  5. E. Fenofibrate
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Fenofibrate

Fibrates (fenofibrate, gemfibrozil, bezafibrate) activate the nuclear receptor PPAR-alpha, increasing lipoprotein lipase and fatty acid oxidation and lowering apoC-III, so triglycerides fall by 30-50% and HDL rises modestly. They are used for very high triglycerides (above about 500-1,000 mg/dL) to reduce the risk of pancreatitis, with omega-3 fatty acids, weight loss, alcohol avoidance and glucose control. Side effects: myopathy (especially gemfibrozil with statins, which raises statin levels), gallstones and a reversible rise in creatinine. Fenofibrate is the fibrate preferred with a statin.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Atorvastatin: Atorvastatin mainly lowers LDL and has less effect on very high triglycerides.
  • B. Ezetimibe: Ezetimibe lowers LDL, not triglycerides.
  • C. Colestyramine: Bile acid binders such as colestyramine can raise triglycerides.
  • D. Pioglitazone: A PPAR-gamma agonist for diabetes.

Key point: Fibrates activate PPAR-alpha and lipoprotein lipase, lowering very high triglycerides; gemfibrozil with statins raises myopathy risk.

Question 9 · Endocrine and reproductive drugs · medium

A 52-year-old man from Haripur with type 2 diabetes and obesity takes metformin 1 g twice daily and was started on semaglutide by injection three weeks ago. Since yesterday he has severe epigastric pain radiating to the back, worse after eating, with repeated vomiting. He drinks no alcohol and a recent ultrasound showed no gallstones. Pulse is 108/min, blood pressure is 114/70 mmHg and the upper abdomen is tender with guarding. Serum lipase is 1,250 U/L (13-60) and triglycerides are 160 mg/dL (below 150).

What is the most appropriate action regarding the semaglutide?

  1. A. Reduce the dose by half and continue
  2. B. Continue and add a proton pump inhibitor
  3. C. Switch to a DPP-4 inhibitor
  4. D. Stop it permanently and do not restart
  5. E. Hold it for a week and restart when the pain settles
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. Stop it permanently and do not restart

GLP-1 receptor agonists (semaglutide, liraglutide, dulaglutide, exenatide) mimic incretin: they increase glucose-dependent insulin secretion, suppress glucagon, slow gastric emptying and reduce appetite, giving weight loss and low hypoglycaemia risk. Several reduce cardiovascular events and are useful in obesity and established atherosclerosis. Common adverse effects are nausea, vomiting and diarrhoea, which are dose-related and reduced by slow titration. Acute pancreatitis is a rare but serious risk, as with DPP-4 inhibitors, so patients should be told to stop the drug and seek help for severe abdominal pain; once pancreatitis is confirmed, the drug is not restarted. They are also associated with gallstones and cholecystitis, and carry a boxed warning for medullary thyroid carcinoma, so are contraindicated with a personal or family history of it or of MEN2. Diabetic retinopathy may worsen with rapid control. Manage him with fluids, analgesia and supportive care.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Reduce the dose by half and continue: Continues exposure to the suspected cause of pancreatitis in this patient.
  • B. Continue and add a proton pump inhibitor: Does not treat pancreatitis and risks recurrent damage.
  • C. Switch to a DPP-4 inhibitor: Is also associated with pancreatitis, so the switch would not be safe here.
  • E. Hold it for a week and restart when the pain settles: Rechallenge after probable pancreatitis risks a more severe recurrence.

Key point: Stop GLP-1 agonists permanently after drug-associated pancreatitis.

Question 10 · General pharmacology and toxicology · medium

A 62-year-old man takes simvastatin 40 mg daily. He is prescribed clarithromycin for a chest infection. Five days later he has severe aching and weakness in his thighs and dark urine. His creatine kinase is 18,000 U/L (normal under 200) and creatinine is rising.

What caused this?

  1. A. Clarithromycin directly damaged muscle
  2. B. The infection caused viral myositis
  3. C. Clarithromycin induced statin metabolism
  4. D. Simvastatin blocked clarithromycin excretion
  5. E. Clarithromycin inhibited CYP3A4
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Clarithromycin inhibited CYP3A4

Simvastatin (and atorvastatin, lovastatin) are metabolised by CYP3A4. Strong CYP3A4 inhibitors raise their levels many times and cause myopathy and rhabdomyolysis (high CK, myoglobinuria, acute kidney injury). Important inhibitors: macrolides (clarithromycin, erythromycin, not azithromycin), azole antifungals, HIV protease inhibitors (ritonavir), diltiazem, verapamil, amiodarone, grapefruit juice and cimetidine. Important inducers: rifampicin, carbamazepine, phenytoin, phenobarbital, St John's wort, chronic alcohol and smoking (CYP1A2). Pravastatin and rosuvastatin are not mainly CYP3A4-metabolised. Statins should be paused during a short course of a strong inhibitor.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Clarithromycin directly damaged muscle: It does not cause myopathy itself.
  • B. The infection caused viral myositis: The timing and statin use point to an interaction.
  • C. Clarithromycin induced statin metabolism: Induction would lower levels.
  • D. Simvastatin blocked clarithromycin excretion: The reverse interaction is not the issue.

Key point: CYP3A4 inhibitors (macrolides, azoles, protease inhibitors) raise simvastatin levels and can cause rhabdomyolysis.
