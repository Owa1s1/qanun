Question 1 · Breast · medium

A 42-year-old woman from Mardan presents with a rapidly enlarging, painless mass in her left breast that she first noticed 6 months ago. Examination reveals a smooth, firm, bosselated 8 cm mass with visible, dilated superficial veins coursing over the overlying taut skin. Core needle biopsy suggests a fibroepithelial lesion with stromal hypercellularity and cellular atypia. Wide local excision with clear histological margins of at least 1 cm is performed.

What distinguishes a phyllodes tumour from a fibroadenoma?

  1. A. It spreads mainly to the axillary nodes
  2. B. It arises mainly after the menopause
  3. C. It is benign and does not recur after excision
  4. D. It arises from the lactiferous ducts
  5. E. Its stroma overgrows and it tends to recur locally
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Its stroma overgrows and it tends to recur locally

A phyllodes tumour (historically termed cystosarcoma phyllodes) is a rare fibroepithelial breast neoplasm that accounts for less than 1% of all breast tumours. While it shares some histological similarities with a fibroadenoma, it is distinguished by its hypercellular stromal proliferation arranged in a characteristic "leaf-like" (phyllodes) architectural pattern. Phyllodes tumours are graded histologically as benign, borderline, or malignant based on stromal cellularity, nuclear atypia, mitotic activity, and border characteristics. Unlike fibroadenomas, which have a very low recurrence risk following enucleation, phyllodes tumours have a high propensity for local recurrence if excised with narrow margins. Therefore, the surgical standard of care is wide local excision with clear histological margins of at least 1 cm. Axillary lymph node dissection is omitted because lymphatic metastasis is exceptionally rare.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. It spreads mainly to the axillary nodes: Phyllodes tumours rarely involve lymph nodes; malignant ones spread through the blood, to the lungs and bones, so axillary surgery is not needed.
  • B. It arises mainly after the menopause: Phyllodes tumours peak at 40-50 years, about 15 years later than fibroadenomas, but most arise before the menopause.
  • C. It is benign and does not recur after excision: Phyllodes tumours are graded benign, borderline or malignant, and even benign ones recur if excised with narrow margins.
  • D. It arises from the lactiferous ducts: Like fibroadenomas, phyllodes tumours arise from the terminal duct lobular unit, not the large ducts.

Key point: Distinguish phyllodes tumours from fibroadenomas by their hypercellular leaf-like stromal proliferation and high local recurrence risk, requiring wide local excision with 1 cm margins.

Question 2 · Cardiothoracic surgery · easy

A 45-year-old man is brought to a hospital in Zhob after a fall from a tractor, with right-sided chest pain and breathlessness. He is alert, with respiratory rate 28/min, oxygen saturation 90% on oxygen and absent breath sounds on the right. A chest radiograph shows a large right pneumothorax with a fractured rib. He is stable, and the surgeon prepares to insert an intercostal chest drain under local anaesthesia, aiming for the safe triangle.

Where should the drain be inserted?

  1. A. Second space, mid-clavicular line
  2. B. Fifth space, anterior mid-axillary
  3. C. Seventh space, posterior axillary line
  4. D. Eighth space, paravertebral area
  5. E. Third space, anterior axillary line
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Fifth space, anterior mid-axillary

The safe triangle for chest drain insertion is bounded by the anterior border of latissimus dorsi, the lateral border of pectoralis major and a line superior to the horizontal level of the nipple (the fifth intercostal space), with the apex below the axilla. Insert the drain just above the rib to avoid the neurovascular bundle running beneath the lower border. Use local anaesthetic, blunt dissection through the intercostal muscles and the pleura, finger sweep to check the space, and ultrasound guidance where available. Small-bore tubes (10-14 Fr) are used for pneumothorax, and larger ones for blood. Connect to an underwater seal, never clamp a bubbling drain, keep it below chest level and confirm position by radiograph.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Second space, mid-clavicular line: This site risks injury to the internal mammary vessels and is used for needle decompression or cosmetic reasons; it is not the usual site for a chest drain in an adult.
  • C. Seventh space, posterior axillary line: A low posterior site risks puncturing the diaphragm, liver or spleen and is uncomfortable for a supine patient.
  • D. Eighth space, paravertebral area: This site is near the diaphragm and abdominal organs and bears no relation to the safe triangle.
  • E. Third space, anterior axillary line: This is higher than the safe triangle and passes through thicker breast and muscle tissue, with a greater risk to the axillary structures.

Key point: Insert an intercostal drain through the safe triangle at the fifth space, just above the rib.

Question 3 · Colorectal surgery and the appendix · medium

A 70-year-old man from Peshawar with a known history of atrial fibrillation presents with sudden onset of agonising, diffuse abdominal pain. He appears profoundly distressed, yet upon examination, his abdomen is remarkably soft and only minimally tender, completely out of proportion to his screaming pain. He has no guarding or rigidity. His laboratory investigations show a white blood cell count of 18,000/µL and a serum lactate of 47 mg/dL.

What is the most likely pathological diagnosis driving this clinical presentation?

  1. A. Acute perforated appendicitis
  2. B. Perforated duodenal peptic ulcer
  3. C. Strangulated incisional hernia
  4. D. Superior mesenteric artery embolus
  5. E. Acute sigmoid diverticulitis
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. Superior mesenteric artery embolus

Acute mesenteric ischaemia is a surgical emergency characterised by the classic, striking clinical paradox of "pain out of proportion to physical signs". The patient has severe, agonising, diffuse abdominal pain, yet on palpation, the abdomen remains soft with minimal tenderness early on. This occurs because the ischaemia affects the visceral smooth muscle of the bowel wall, which somatic nerve endings do not palpably register until transmural infarction and peritonitis set in. This patient has atrial fibrillation, which is the major risk factor for a cardioembolic occlusion of the Superior Mesenteric Artery (SMA). The elevated white cell count and high serum lactate (47 mg/dL) reflect established tissue necrosis and anaerobic cellular metabolism. It requires urgent CT angiography and surgical revascularisation or resection.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Acute perforated appendicitis: Appendicitis presents with localised right lower quadrant peritonitis and tenderness, completely contrasting with diffuse pain out of proportion and a high lactate.
  • B. Perforated duodenal peptic ulcer: A perforated ulcer causes sudden severe pain, but it is accompanied by board-like abdominal rigidity and pneumoperitoneated free air, not a soft abdomen.
  • C. Strangulated incisional hernia: Strangulation causes localised tenderness over a specific hernia defect with overlying skin changes, rather than generalised diffuse ischaemic pain.
  • E. Acute sigmoid diverticulitis: Diverticulitis causes localised left lower quadrant tenderness and inflammation, whereas this patient has diffuse ischaemia with a high serum lactate.

Key point: Diagnose acute superior mesenteric artery occlusion by the classic triad of atrial fibrillation, pain out of proportion to physical signs, and an elevated serum lactate.

Question 4 · Endocrine surgery, head and neck · medium

A 42-year-old woman in Khairpur had a total thyroidectomy for a 2.2 cm papillary carcinoma with central node metastases, followed by radioiodine ablation. Six months later she feels well on levothyroxine 150 µg daily, with a healed scar and no palpable nodes. TSH is 0.3 mIU/L (0.4-4.0). Neck ultrasound is normal and anti-thyroglobulin antibodies are negative. The surgeon must choose a blood test for long-term surveillance of recurrence.

Which blood test is most useful for monitoring recurrence?

  1. A. Serum thyroglobulin
  2. B. Serum calcitonin
  3. C. Serum CA 19-9
  4. D. Serum thyroid peroxidase antibody
  5. E. Serum free T4 alone
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Serum thyroglobulin

Thyroglobulin is made only by thyroid follicular cells, normal or malignant, so after total thyroidectomy and radioiodine ablation it should fall to very low or undetectable levels. A rising level on TSH suppression or stimulation suggests residual or recurrent disease and prompts imaging. Always measure anti-thyroglobulin antibodies with it, because they interfere and can give a falsely low result, in which case their trend serves as the marker. Neck ultrasound is the main imaging test for nodal recurrence. Levothyroxine is adjusted to a risk-based TSH target: about 0.5-2 mIU/L in low-risk disease and lower, 0.1-0.5 mIU/L, in intermediate-risk disease, as here. Long-term follow-up is needed because late recurrence can occur.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Serum calcitonin: Is the marker for medullary carcinoma of C cells; her papillary cancer arises from follicular cells, which do not produce calcitonin.
  • C. Serum CA 19-9: Is a marker for pancreatobiliary cancers and has no role in thyroid cancer surveillance.
  • D. Serum thyroid peroxidase antibody: Indicates autoimmune thyroid disease and does not track tumour burden; her thyroid has been removed, so it adds nothing.
  • E. Serum free T4 alone: Checks the adequacy of levothyroxine replacement and cannot detect recurrence; her TSH already shows the dose is appropriate.

Key point: Use serum thyroglobulin with anti-thyroglobulin antibodies to monitor differentiated thyroid cancer after ablation.

Question 5 · Principles of surgery · medium

A 62-year-old man from Mardan presents to the emergency department with severe right lower limb pain over the past 12 hours. He has a history of poorly controlled hypertension. Temperature is 38.8°C, HR 122 bpm, and BP 85/50 mmHg. Examination of the leg reveals spreading dusky erythema, oedema, and purple haemorrhagic bullae. The leg is exquisitely tender to touch, extending proximally far beyond the visible skin changes.

What is the most appropriate next step in management?

  1. A. Intravenous vancomycin and clindamycin
  2. B. Magnetic resonance imaging of the leg
  3. C. Urgent extensive surgical debridement
  4. D. Incision and drainage of the bullae
  5. E. Application of topical silver sulfadiazine
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Urgent extensive surgical debridement

This patient has a necrotising soft tissue infection, clinically suggested by the rapid onset of pain out of proportion to skin changes, spreading dusky erythema, haemorrhagic bullae, and septic shock. Necrotising fasciitis is a surgical emergency. The infection tracks rapidly along deep fascial planes, causing widespread thrombosis of penetrating vessels and subsequent tissue necrosis. Because the tissue is ischaemic, systemic antibiotics cannot reach the infection site in adequate concentrations. Therefore, immediate and aggressive surgical debridement of all non-viable skin, subcutaneous tissue, and fascia until healthy bleeding tissue is encountered is the only life-saving intervention. Delaying surgery for imaging significantly increases mortality.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Intravenous vancomycin and clindamycin: Broad-spectrum antibiotics are adjunctive and must be given immediately, but they cannot penetrate dead fascial planes and are inadequate as standalone primary treatment.
  • B. Magnetic resonance imaging of the leg: Cross-sectional imaging provides excellent soft tissue detail but delays life-saving operative intervention in a patient exhibiting unmistakable clinical signs of deep fascial infection.
  • D. Incision and drainage of the bullae: Simple skin drainage is wholly inadequate because the destructive infectious process spreads rapidly along the deep fascial planes far beyond the superficial blisters.
  • E. Application of topical silver sulfadiazine: Topical antimicrobials are used for superficial thermal burns; they possess no efficacy against rapidly advancing, deep fascial infections driving profound systemic shock.

Key point: Identify that immediate and aggressive surgical debridement is the primary, life-saving treatment for necrotising fasciitis.

Question 6 · Hepatobiliary and pancreatic surgery, and the spleen · medium

A 42-year-old man from Abbottabad is recovering from an episode of severe acute gallstone pancreatitis. Six weeks ago, his acute symptoms resolved, and a cholecystectomy was performed successfully. However, over the last two weeks, he has developed a persistent, dull epigastric ache and early satiety. An ultrasound scan reveals a well-defined, unilocular fluid collection measuring 7 cm in maximum diameter within the body of the pancreas. The cyst wall is thick and mature. He has no fever, and his white blood cell count is normal.

What is the most appropriate management for this patient?

  1. A. Immediate emergency open surgical resection of the entire pancreas
  2. B. Percutaneous catheter drainage followed by permanent external fistulisation
  3. C. Immediate emergency diagnostic laparoscopy and washout
  4. D. Observation and supportive care for a further 12 weeks
  5. E. Endoscopic transmural drainage or surgical internal drainage (cystogastrostomy)
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Endoscopic transmural drainage or surgical internal drainage (cystogastrostomy)

A pancreatic pseudocyst is a collection of pancreatic juice enclosed by a wall of fibrous or granulation tissue that develops outside the pancreas, typically occurring 4 to 6 weeks following an attack of acute pancreatitis or trauma. Crucially, unlike true cysts, a pseudocyst lacks an epithelial lining (hence "pseudo"). Management depends heavily on symptoms and maturity. Asymptomatic pseudocysts <6 cm are managed conservatively, as up to 50% resolve spontaneously over 6 to 12 weeks. However, intervention is strictly required if the cyst is symptomatic (causing persistent pain, gastric outlet obstruction, or biliary obstruction), enlarging, or complicated (infection, haemorrhage), or if it persists beyond 6 weeks with a mature wall. Because external drainage causes a chronic pancreatic fistula, definitive management involves internal drainage (cystogastrostomy, cystojejunostomy) or endoscopic transluminal drainage.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Immediate emergency open surgical resection of the entire pancreas: Total pancreatectomy for a benign pseudocyst is catastrophically over-aggressive and induces lifelong brittle diabetes and exocrine failure.
  • B. Percutaneous catheter drainage followed by permanent external fistulisation: External drainage of a pancreatic pseudocyst frequently results in a chronic, high-output pancreatic cutaneous fistula that is exceptionally difficult to close.
  • C. Immediate emergency diagnostic laparoscopy and washout: Washing out the peritoneal cavity is indicated for acute free perforation or peritonitis, not for a walled-off, sterile fluid collection inside the pancreatic parenchyma.
  • D. Observation and supportive care for a further 12 weeks: A mature, symptomatic cyst larger than 6 cm that has persisted beyond 6 weeks is unlikely to resolve, and further observation risks infection, bleeding or rupture.

Key point: Manage symptomatic or persistent mature pancreatic pseudocysts (>6 weeks, >6 cm) with internal drainage, while observing asymptomatic small cysts.

Question 7 · Neurosurgery · medium

A 24-year-old man from Peshawar is managed in the neurosurgical ICU following a severe traumatic brain injury. Over the last hour, his nurse notes a sudden, alarming deterioration in his clinical status. His heart rate drops from 85 to 50 bpm (bradycardia), his systemic blood pressure rises from 120/80 to 190/100 mmHg (hypertension), and his respiratory pattern becomes irregular and deep (irregular respirations).

What do these vital sign changes represent?

  1. A. Compensated hypovolaemic shock
  2. B. Neurogenic shock from cervical cord injury
  3. C. Cushing reflex from raised intracranial pressure
  4. D. Autonomic dysreflexia from a full bladder
  5. E. Paroxysmal sympathetic hyperactivity
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Cushing reflex from raised intracranial pressure

Cushing's triad is a classic, ominous clinical sign representing severely raised intracranial pressure (ICP) leading to mechanical compression of the brainstem (vasomotor centre ischaemia). It consists of three cardinal features: 1. Hypertension (often with a widening pulse pressure). 2. Bradycardia. 3. Irregular respirations (caused by compression of the medullary respiratory centre). The physiological mechanism is a reflex response: as ICP approaches mean arterial pressure (MAP), cerebral perfusion drops. To maintain brain perfusion, the body mounts a massive systemic sympathetic surge, causing extreme hypertension. This hypertension activates the carotid baroreceptors, triggering a reflex parasympathetic response via the vagus nerve that causes profound bradycardia. The presence of Cushing's triad is a pre-terminal warning sign of impending uncal or tonsillar herniation, requiring immediate hyperventilation, osmotic therapy, or surgical decompression.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Compensated hypovolaemic shock: Hypovolaemic shock causes profound tachycardia, hypotension, and tachypnoea, exactly the reverse of bradycardia and hypertension seen here.
  • B. Neurogenic shock from cervical cord injury: Neurogenic shock causes hypotension and bradycardia with warm peripheries, lacking the extreme reflex systemic hypertension of raised ICP.
  • D. Autonomic dysreflexia from a full bladder: Autonomic dysreflexia occurs in patients with spinal cord injuries above T6, causing sudden hypertension and bradycardia, but lacks primary raised intracranial pressure.
  • E. Paroxysmal sympathetic hyperactivity: Sympathetic storms after brain injury cause hypertension with tachycardia, sweating and fever, not bradycardia.

Key point: Identify Cushing's triad (hypertension, bradycardia, irregular respirations) as a pre-terminal sign of severely raised intracranial pressure and brainstem compression.

Question 8 · Orthopaedics and fractures · easy

A 42-year-old man attends a hospital in Haripur after feeling a sudden sharp pain at the back of his left ankle, as if he had been kicked, while lunging for a shuttlecock. He heard a snap and now cannot push off on that foot or stand on tiptoe. The calf is bruised and swollen, and a 4 cm gap can be felt in the tendon above the heel. He is able to flex his toes weakly. He has no history of fluoroquinolone use.

Which examination finding most reliably confirms the diagnosis?

  1. A. Pain on resisted ankle dorsiflexion
  2. B. No plantar flexion on calf squeeze
  3. C. Positive talar tilt test
  4. D. Tenderness over the lateral malleolus
  5. E. Painful passive dorsiflexion with calf swelling
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. No plantar flexion on calf squeeze

Achilles tendon rupture occurs mostly in men aged 30-50 who play sport intermittently, typically with a sudden push-off or lunge, a snap and a sensation of being kicked. Risk factors are fluoroquinolone use, corticosteroids and tendinopathy. Examination shows a palpable gap 2-6 cm above the calcaneus, weak plantar flexion (toe flexors may still move the foot) and a positive Thompson (Simmonds) calf squeeze test, which shows no plantar flexion. Ultrasound confirms when there is doubt. Treatment is either early functional rehabilitation in a boot with the foot in equinus and progressive weight bearing, or surgical repair; modern trials show similar re-rupture rates with accelerated rehabilitation. Delayed diagnosis is common.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Pain on resisted ankle dorsiflexion: Suggests anterior ankle or tibialis anterior pathology and does not test the Achilles tendon.
  • C. Positive talar tilt test: Tests the lateral ankle ligaments after inversion injury; he has a posterior tendon gap with loss of push-off.
  • D. Tenderness over the lateral malleolus: Points to fibular fracture or lateral ligament sprain after inversion, not to a palpable gap above the heel.
  • E. Painful passive dorsiflexion with calf swelling: Is nonspecific and occurs in deep vein thrombosis and calf strain; it does not confirm a tendon rupture.

Key point: Confirm Achilles tendon rupture with the Thompson calf squeeze test.

Question 9 · Paediatric surgery · medium

A neonate born at 38 weeks to a mother with polyhydramnios is examined at 18 hours in a hospital in Hyderabad. He has upslanting eyes and a single palmar crease, and has had bilious vomiting since his first feed. The abdomen is scaphoid with upper abdominal fullness and he has passed a little meconium. A radiograph shows two gas bubbles in the upper abdomen with no gas distally. He is stable after fluid resuscitation and nasogastric decompression, and echocardiography shows no major cardiac defect.

What is the most appropriate definitive treatment?

  1. A. Duodenoduodenostomy
  2. B. Ladd procedure
  3. C. Ramstedt pyloromyotomy
  4. D. Loop colostomy
  5. E. Gastrostomy feeding only
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Duodenoduodenostomy

Duodenal atresia presents in neonates with bilious vomiting (obstruction is usually distal to the ampulla), a history of polyhydramnios and often features of Down syndrome (about a third), with associated cardiac defects, oesophageal atresia, anorectal malformations and annular pancreas. The abdominal radiograph shows the double bubble sign (distended stomach and proximal duodenum), with no distal gas in complete atresia. Management is decompression with a nasogastric tube, correction of fluids and electrolytes, echocardiography, and surgery within days: duodenoduodenostomy (side-to-side or diamond-shaped), by open or laparoscopic route. Outcomes are good, with survival over 90%. In the presence of distal gas, partial obstruction or malrotation must be considered.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Ladd procedure: Treats malrotation with volvulus, in which a double bubble is not seen and distal gas is usually present.
  • C. Ramstedt pyloromyotomy: Treats pyloric stenosis in weeks-old infants with non-bilious vomiting; this neonate has bilious vomiting from complete duodenal obstruction.
  • D. Loop colostomy: Treats anorectal malformations or distal obstruction; the problem is in the duodenum, which a colostomy does not bypass.
  • E. Gastrostomy feeding only: Does not relieve a complete duodenal obstruction, and feeds would not pass distally.

Key point: Treat duodenal atresia by duodenoduodenostomy after resuscitation.

Question 10 · Perioperative care and anaesthesia · medium

A 38-year-old woman in Rawalakot who presents a music programme on local radio and sings at functions is listed for total thyroidectomy for a large multinodular goitre. In clinic she has said several times that her voice is her livelihood. The registrar taking consent plans to discuss bleeding, wound infection and temporary hoarseness. He says the risk of permanent recurrent laryngeal nerve injury, about 1% in the unit, is too small to mention and may frighten her into refusing a needed operation. Her calcium is 9.3 mg/dL (8.5-10.5) and she has full capacity.

What is the most appropriate approach to her consent?

  1. A. Disclose only risks above about one per cent
  2. B. Ask her husband what she should be told
  3. C. Give a printed leaflet and take her signature
  4. D. Discuss the risk of permanent voice change
  5. E. Withhold the risk to avoid causing anxiety
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. Discuss the risk of permanent voice change

Valid consent requires capacity, voluntariness and adequate information. Since the Montgomery judgment (2015) in the UK, and in GMC guidance on decision making and consent (2020), doctors must disclose any material risk, defined as one a reasonable person in the patient's position would consider significant, or one the doctor knows or should know this particular patient would consider significant, together with reasonable alternatives, including doing nothing. A low percentage does not make a risk immaterial: permanent recurrent laryngeal nerve palsy after thyroidectomy is uncommon, but for a woman whose income depends on her voice it is central to her decision, and she has said so. Fear that she may refuse is not a reason to withhold information; therapeutic privilege is a narrow exception for serious harm. Consent should ideally be taken by the operating surgeon or someone able to perform the procedure, and documented.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Disclose only risks above about one per cent: Consent law and guidance reject a fixed percentage threshold; what matters is whether the risk is significant to this particular patient.
  • B. Ask her husband what she should be told: She is an adult with full capacity, so information is shared with her directly and family cannot decide what she hears.
  • C. Give a printed leaflet and take her signature: A leaflet supports but does not replace a dialogue tailored to her concerns, and a signature alone does not show informed consent.
  • E. Withhold the risk to avoid causing anxiety: Therapeutic privilege applies only where disclosure would cause serious harm, not simply because it might lead her to decline.

Key point: Apply the material-risk standard of informed consent to the individual patient's priorities.
