Question 1 · Caries management and direct restorations · easy

A 40-year-old man visits a private dental clinic in Quetta with sharp pain in the lower right premolars when drinking cold water for two weeks. The teeth are 44 and 45. Examination shows gingival recession with exposed dentine and no caries or crack.

What is the most appropriate first-line management?

  1. A. Composite restorations on both teeth
  2. B. A desensitising agent or fluoride varnish
  3. C. Root canal treatment of both teeth
  4. D. Extraction of both premolars
  5. E. Systemic antibiotics for five days
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. A desensitising agent or fluoride varnish

Dentine hypersensitivity is a short, sharp pain from exposed dentine in response to stimuli, with no other defect. First-line management is a desensitising agent such as potassium nitrate toothpaste or fluoride varnish, which occludes tubules or reduces nerve transmission. Restorations, root canal treatment or extraction are reserved for complicated cases. Reassurance and a soft brush are also important. In Pakistan, many patients use acidic soft drinks and should be counselled to reduce frequency.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Composite restorations on both teeth: Composite restorations are indicated for cervical defects or lesions, not for simple exposed dentine without tissue loss.
  • C. Root canal treatment of both teeth: Transient cold pain with no spontaneous pain does not indicate irreversible pulpitis or root canal treatment.
  • D. Extraction of both premolars: Extraction is for unrestorable teeth; these premolars are vital and can be treated conservatively.
  • E. Systemic antibiotics for five days: Antibiotics treat infection; dentine hypersensitivity is a hydrodynamic phenomenon, not an infection.

Key point: Choose first-line conservative management for dentine hypersensitivity.

Question 2 · Crowns and indirect restorations · easy

A 30-year-old female attends a dental teaching hospital in Hyderabad with a fractured upper left central incisor. She wants an all-ceramic crown that matches the adjacent tooth. Examination of 21 shows an uncomplicated crown fracture with sound root and periodontal health.

Which all-ceramic material is most appropriate for this anterior crown?

  1. A. Zirconia
  2. B. Lithium disilicate
  3. C. Feldspathic porcelain
  4. D. Leucite-reinforced porcelain
  5. E. Alumina
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Lithium disilicate

For an anterior all-ceramic crown, lithium disilicate offers the best combination of strength and translucency, allowing a natural match to the adjacent central incisor. Zirconia is stronger but more opaque, making shade matching harder in the aesthetic zone. Feldspathic porcelain lacks sufficient strength as a full crown core. Leucite-reinforced porcelain is better suited to veneers and inlays. Alumina is rarely used now. Shillingburg Fundamentals of Fixed Prosthodontics: chapter on all-ceramic crowns.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Zirconia: Zirconia is very strong but more opaque, making it harder to match a single central incisor for translucency.
  • C. Feldspathic porcelain: Feldspathic porcelain is too brittle to be used alone as a full crown and is mainly a veneering ceramic.
  • D. Leucite-reinforced porcelain: Leucite-reinforced porcelain is suitable for veneers and inlays but is less fracture resistant than lithium disilicate for a crown.
  • E. Alumina: Alumina is strong but less aesthetic and has largely been replaced by lithium disilicate and zirconia for crowns.

Key point: Choose lithium disilicate for an aesthetic all-ceramic anterior crown.

Question 3 · Endodontics and dental trauma · medium

A 42-year-old man attends a private clinic in Sukkur with a severe periapical infection in 36, lower left first molar. He has fever 38.2 degrees Celsius and malaise, with a fluctuant buccal swelling. Incision and drainage was performed under local anaesthesia, and the canal was opened to establish drainage.

Which antibiotic should be prescribed now?

  1. A. Metronidazole 400 mg every 8 hours for 5 days
  2. B. Clarithromycin 500 mg every 12 hours for 5 days
  3. C. Ciprofloxacin 500 mg every 12 hours for 5 days
  4. D. Amoxicillin 500 mg every 8 hours for 5 days
  5. E. No antibiotic; analgesics only
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. Amoxicillin 500 mg every 8 hours for 5 days

After incision and drainage of an acute apical abscess, systemic antibiotics are indicated when the patient has fever, malaise, or spreading infection. Amoxicillin 500 mg every 8 hours is the first-line choice for odontogenic infections because it covers the aerobic and anaerobic streptococci commonly involved. Metronidazole alone lacks aerobic coverage and is only used as an adjunct or in combination. Clarithromycin is reserved for penicillin-allergic patients. Ciprofloxacin is unreliable against oral anaerobes. Analgesics alone are insufficient once systemic signs are present. In Sukkur private practice, amoxicillin is widely available and inexpensive, making it the practical first-line antibiotic.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Metronidazole 400 mg every 8 hours for 5 days: Metronidazole alone does not cover aerobic streptococci and is not used as monotherapy for odontogenic abscesses.
  • B. Clarithromycin 500 mg every 12 hours for 5 days: Clarithromycin is reserved for patients with a true penicillin allergy; this patient has no allergy stated.
  • C. Ciprofloxacin 500 mg every 12 hours for 5 days: Ciprofloxacin has poor activity against anaerobic oral flora and is not first-line for dental infections.
  • E. No antibiotic; analgesics only: Fever and malaise indicate systemic involvement, so antibiotics are required in addition to drainage and analgesia.

Key point: Prescribe amoxicillin after drainage of an acute apical abscess with fever and malaise.

Question 4 · Caries management and direct restorations · medium

A 24-year-old woman attends a private clinic in Gilgit with a small carious lesion on the distal surface of 11 (upper right central incisor). The labial surface and labial enamel are sound, the tooth has a normal shade, and the contact with 12 is open enough to reach the lesion from the palatal side.

From which surface should the Class III cavity be prepared?

  1. A. From the labial surface
  2. B. From the incisal edge
  3. C. From the palatal (lingual) surface
  4. D. From the gingival margin
  5. E. Through the contact of the adjacent tooth
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. From the palatal (lingual) surface

A Class III cavity on an anterior tooth is prepared from the palatal or lingual surface when the labial enamel is sound and the lesion can be reached from behind, because this keeps the labial enamel, protects the appearance of the tooth and conserves tooth structure. The labial approach is used when the lesion extends labially, when the contact is tight on the lingual side, or when a previous labial restoration has to be replaced. The adjacent tooth must never be cut to gain access.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. From the labial surface: A labial approach removes sound labial enamel and spoils the appearance of the tooth.
  • B. From the incisal edge: Preparing through the incisal edge removes sound tooth structure and weakens the tooth.
  • D. From the gingival margin: A gingival approach would mean cutting into the supporting tissues and is not needed.
  • E. Through the contact of the adjacent tooth: Cutting into the sound adjacent tooth damages it and is never done.

Key point: Classify a cervical third lesion on an anterior tooth as G.V. Black Class V.

Question 5 · Crowns and indirect restorations · easy

A 40-year-old man attends a dental OPD in Quetta. Tooth 24 (upper left first premolar) is prepared for a full ceramic crown. The finish line is a chamfer at the gingival margin.

Which impression technique gives the most accurate record of the finish line?

  1. A. Alginate impression with a stock tray
  2. B. Putty-wash impression using addition silicone
  3. C. Irreversible hydrocolloid with a custom tray
  4. D. Polyether in a stock tray without adhesive
  5. E. Condensation silicone with a single mix
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Putty-wash impression using addition silicone

Definitive crown impressions require an elastomeric material with high detail reproduction and dimensional stability. Alginate and irreversible hydrocolloid lack the accuracy needed for fixed prosthodontics and distort before pouring. Condensation silicone shrinks by solvent evaporation. Polyether is accurate but needs tray adhesive in a stock tray to prevent separation. Addition silicone in a putty-wash technique records the chamfer finish line precisely and remains stable, making it the best choice here. A custom tray with uniform spacer thickness further improves accuracy, but the material and technique shown are the key determinants.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Alginate impression with a stock tray: Alginate lacks the detail reproduction and dimensional stability required for a definitive crown impression.
  • C. Irreversible hydrocolloid with a custom tray: Irreversible hydrocolloid tears easily and distorts on removal; it is unsuitable for fixed prosthodontics.
  • D. Polyether in a stock tray without adhesive: Polyether is accurate but a stock tray without adhesive may separate from the tray during removal.
  • E. Condensation silicone with a single mix: Condensation silicone shrinks by solvent evaporation and is less dimensionally stable than addition silicone.

Key point: The student will choose the most accurate impression technique for a subgingival ceramic crown finish line.

Question 6 · Endodontics and dental trauma · medium

A 40-year-old woman attends a government dental hospital in Quetta with severe pain in her upper right first molar, 16. Root canal treatment was started elsewhere and stopped because of a blockage. A radiograph shows a ledge in the mesiobuccal canal at mid-root, with the apical third unprepared. The tooth is tender to percussion but there is no swelling.

What is the most appropriate next step?

  1. A. Negotiate the ledge with precurved hand files
  2. B. Extract 16 and fabricate a removable partial denture
  3. C. Obturate the canal short of the ledge and review in six months
  4. D. Perform an apicectomy to bypass the ledge
  5. E. Place calcium hydroxide and wait for symptoms to settle
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Negotiate the ledge with precurved hand files

A ledge that stops short of the apex should first be managed by careful negotiation with precurved hand files under magnification. If the ledge is bypassed, cleaning and shaping can continue to working length and the canal can be obturated. Extraction is unnecessary when the tooth is restorable and the blockage is accessible. Obturating short of the ledge leaves infected tissue untouched, and immediate apicectomy is not the first step before a nonsurgical attempt. Calcium hydroxide medicament alone does not remove the ledge.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Extract 16 and fabricate a removable partial denture: Extraction is unnecessary when the ledge can often be bypassed and the tooth remains restorable.
  • C. Obturate the canal short of the ledge and review in six months: Obturating the canal short of the ledge and reviewing in six months leaves the apex untreated.
  • D. Perform an apicectomy to bypass the ledge: Surgery is not the first step before attempting nonsurgical negotiation of the ledge.
  • E. Place calcium hydroxide and wait for symptoms to settle: Placing calcium hydroxide will not negotiate the ledge or clean the unprepared apex.

Key point: Manage a canal ledge by attempting nonsurgical negotiation before considering extraction or surgery.

Question 7 · Caries management and direct restorations · easy

A 24-year-old female attends a general practice in Mardan for a routine examination. The dentist is choosing a chairside aid to examine the proximal surfaces of her upper posterior teeth, which are not easily visible with a mirror alone.

Which examination method is most useful for detecting proximal caries in this situation?

  1. A. Visual inspection alone
  2. B. Transillumination
  3. C. Percussion
  4. D. Palpation
  5. E. Mobility test
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Transillumination

Transillumination uses a strong light source passed through the crown; sound enamel transmits light while carious lesions, especially on proximal surfaces, appear as dark shadows. It is a simple chairside adjunct for anterior and posterior proximal surfaces when direct vision is limited. It does not replace radiographs for depth assessment but helps detect early proximal lesions. Other tests such as percussion, palpation and mobility assess periodontal or pulpal status, not caries.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Visual inspection alone: Proximal surfaces are not directly visible without separation or radiographs, so inspection alone misses early lesions.
  • C. Percussion: Percussion assesses pulp and periapical status, not enamel or dentine caries on proximal surfaces.
  • D. Palpation: Palpation checks soft tissues and periapical inflammation, not proximal tooth surface caries.
  • E. Mobility test: Mobility evaluates periodontal support and has no role in detecting proximal carious lesions.

Key point: Select transillumination as the most useful chairside aid for proximal caries detection.

Question 8 · Crowns and indirect restorations · easy

A 40-year-old man is referred from a general practice in Islamabad for crown lengthening on 46. The distal caries margin lies close to the alveolar bone. The referring dentist asks how much soft tissue attachment exists between the pocket base and the bone crest.

What structures together form the supracrestal tissue attachment?

  1. A. Junctional epithelium
  2. B. Connective tissue attachment
  3. C. Gingival sulcus and junctional epithelium
  4. D. Gingival sulcus and connective tissue attachment
  5. E. Junctional epithelium and connective tissue attachment
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Junctional epithelium and connective tissue attachment

The supracrestal tissue attachment, or biologic width, is the dimension of soft tissue between the alveolar bone crest and the base of the periodontal pocket. It consists of the junctional epithelium and the connective tissue attachment, together averaging about two millimetres. If a restoration margin invades this width, chronic inflammation, pocket deepening, and bone loss can result. Respecting biologic width is essential when placing crown margins and planning crown lengthening.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Junctional epithelium: Junctional epithelium alone is only about one millimetre and does not include the connective tissue attachment.
  • B. Connective tissue attachment: Connective tissue attachment alone is only about one millimetre and omits the epithelial attachment.
  • C. Gingival sulcus and junctional epithelium: The gingival sulcus is coronal to the attachment and is not part of the biologic width.
  • D. Gingival sulcus and connective tissue attachment: The sulcus is a clinical space, not a component of the supracrestal tissue attachment.

Key point: Define the supracrestal tissue attachment as the combined junctional epithelium and connective tissue attachment.

Question 9 · Endodontics and dental trauma · easy

A 45-year-old man visits a private dental clinic in Swat because his upper right central incisor (11) has turned grey after a fall two years ago. Tooth 11 responds to cold only after a delay of ten seconds. There is no swelling or sinus tract. A radiograph shows a widened periodontal ligament space and a partly calcified pulp chamber. The dentist plans root canal treatment followed by internal bleaching.

Which access cavity outline is most appropriate for this tooth?

  1. A. Circular, centred on the incisal edge
  2. B. Rectangular, wider mesiodistally
  3. C. Trapezoidal, wider at the incisal third
  4. D. Oval, longer incisocervically
  5. E. Rounded triangle, base towards the incisal edge
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Rounded triangle, base towards the incisal edge

The access cavity for a maxillary central incisor is a rounded triangle on the palatal surface, with its base towards the incisal edge and its apex towards the cingulum. This shape follows the pulp chamber, which is widest incisally under the mesial and distal pulp horns, and lets all pulp horn tissue be removed so that it cannot cause discolouration. Entry is made on the palatal surface just above the cingulum, not through the incisal edge, to keep incisal enamel and give straight-line access. Oval outlines suit mandibular incisors and canines. In a calcified chamber, ultrasonic tips help locate the orifice with less risk of perforation.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Circular, centred on the incisal edge: Entry through the incisal edge destroys incisal anatomy and risks labial perforation of 11.
  • B. Rectangular, wider mesiodistally: This removes too much labial and palatal tooth structure and does not follow the shape of the pulp chamber of 11.
  • C. Trapezoidal, wider at the incisal third: This is not a standard anterior access shape and unnecessarily removes incisal enamel of 11.
  • D. Oval, longer incisocervically: An oval outline suits mandibular incisors and canines; in 11 it would not reach the mesial and distal pulp horns under the incisal edge.

Key point: The student will identify the oval incisocervical access outline for a maxillary central incisor.

Question 10 · Caries management and direct restorations · easy

A 16-year-old male attends a government dental clinic in Peshawar for a routine check-up. He has no pain. On examination, the occlusal surface of the lower left first molar (36) shows a carious lesion confined to the central fossa and fissures. The proximal surfaces are intact.

Which G.V. Black class does this cavity belong to?

  1. A. Class I
  2. B. Class II
  3. C. Class III
  4. D. Class IV
  5. E. Class V
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Class I

G.V. Black Class I cavities are those arising in pits and fissures, including occlusal surfaces of molars and premolars, buccal or lingual pits, and lingual pits of anterior teeth. Because this lesion is confined to the occlusal central fossa and fissures of 36 with intact proximal surfaces, it is Class I. Classes II to V involve smooth surfaces or cervical areas and do not fit this description.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Class II: Class II involves proximal surfaces of posterior teeth; this lesion is occlusal, not proximal.
  • C. Class III: Class III involves proximal surfaces of anterior teeth without incisal angle involvement; wrong tooth and surface.
  • D. Class IV: Class IV involves proximal anterior teeth with incisal angle involvement; not applicable here.
  • E. Class V: Class V is in the gingival third of buccal or lingual surfaces; this is an occlusal pit-and-fissure lesion.

Key point: Classify an occlusal pit-and-fissure lesion as G.V. Black Class I.
