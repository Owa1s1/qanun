Question 1 · Bacteriology · medium

A 66-year-old retired teacher in Faisalabad has had fever and weight loss for 2 months. Echocardiography shows a vegetation on the aortic valve. Haemoglobin is 9.6 g/dL with an MCV of 72 fL, and ferritin is 8 ng/mL (24-336). Blood cultures grow gram-positive cocci in chains that are non-haemolytic, catalase negative, hydrolyse aesculin in the presence of bile, but do not grow in 6.5% salt. The isolate is identified as Streptococcus gallolyticus, and he starts intravenous penicillin.

Which further investigation is most important for him?

  1. A. Dental examination
  2. B. Renal ultrasound
  3. C. HIV test
  4. D. Bone marrow biopsy
  5. E. Colonoscopy
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Colonoscopy

Streptococcus gallolyticus (formerly S. bovis biotype I) is a group D, non-enterococcal streptococcus: it grows in bile and hydrolyses aesculin (bile-aesculin positive) but, unlike enterococci, does not grow in 6.5% salt. It lives in the bowel, and bacteraemia or endocarditis with it is strongly associated with colorectal adenomas and carcinoma; disrupted colonic mucosa probably lets it into the blood. Every patient with S. gallolyticus bacteraemia or endocarditis needs colonoscopy, here made more urgent by iron-deficiency anaemia. The association also extends to chronic liver disease. It is usually sensitive to penicillin.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Dental examination: A dental source fits viridans streptococci; his isolate grows in bile and is bile-aesculin positive, pointing to the bowel.
  • B. Renal ultrasound: Renal imaging would not explain iron deficiency with S. gallolyticus, an organism that signals a colonic lesion.
  • C. HIV test: HIV testing is reasonable in endocarditis generally, but it would miss the colonic tumour that this organism points to.
  • D. Bone marrow biopsy: Marrow biopsy is unnecessary when his ferritin of 8 ng/mL already shows iron deficiency and points to blood loss.

Key point: Streptococcus gallolyticus (S. bovis) bacteraemia or endocarditis is linked to colorectal cancer; colonoscopy is needed.

Question 2 · Mycology · easy

A 23-year-old man in Karachi has had several pale, slightly scaly patches on his chest, upper back and shoulders for 2 months, more obvious since the hot weather began and after he started playing cricket daily. They do not itch much and have no raised edge. Potassium hydroxide examination of scrapings shows short curved hyphae with clusters of round yeast cells.

Which organism causes this condition?

  1. A. Trichophyton rubrum
  2. B. Malassezia furfur
  3. C. Candida albicans
  4. D. Microsporum canis
  5. E. Sporothrix schenckii
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Malassezia furfur

Pityriasis versicolor is caused by Malassezia species (formerly Pityrosporum), lipid-dependent yeasts that are part of normal skin flora in sebum-rich areas. Heat, humidity, sweating, oily skin and immunosuppression allow overgrowth, so it is common in young adults in hot climates and after exercise. It produces well-defined macules on the trunk, upper arms and neck that are lighter than surrounding skin in dark-skinned people (the yeast makes azelaic acid, which inhibits melanin) and darker or pink in pale skin, with fine scale that is revealed by stretching or scraping. Potassium hydroxide microscopy shows the classic "spaghetti and meatballs" appearance of short hyphae with round yeasts; culture needs lipid supplementation. Treatment is topical ketoconazole or selenium sulphide shampoo applied to the whole area, with oral itraconazole for extensive or recurrent disease; pigment takes months to return to normal, which patients should be told. Malassezia also causes seborrhoeic dermatitis, dandruff and folliculitis.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Trichophyton rubrum: Trichophyton gives an itchy ring with a raised scaly edge; his patches are flat, pale and barely itchy.
  • C. Candida albicans: Candida affects moist folds and mucosa with soreness; his lesions are dry, scaly and on the upper trunk.
  • D. Microsporum canis: Microsporum comes from cats and dogs and attacks the scalp, not the chest and shoulders of a cricketer.
  • E. Sporothrix schenckii: Sporothrix follows a thorn injury with nodules up a limb, not pale scaly macules appearing in hot weather.

Key point: Malassezia causes pityriasis versicolor with hypopigmented scaly macules and a spaghetti-and-meatballs appearance on KOH microscopy.

Question 3 · General microbiology · easy

A 3-day-old baby in a neonatal unit in Lahore develops fever, poor feeding and a temperature of 38.6°C. A blood culture flags positive after 14 hours, and the Gram stain shows gram-positive cocci in pairs and small groups, so the arrangement is unclear. The laboratory wants one quick bench test to tell whether these are staphylococci or streptococci before further tests.

Which test should be done first?

  1. A. Coagulase test
  2. B. Optochin test
  3. C. Bacitracin test
  4. D. Catalase test
  5. E. Oxidase test
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. Catalase test

Gram-positive cocci are first split by the catalase test: a colony is mixed with 3% hydrogen peroxide, and bubbles of oxygen mean catalase is present. Staphylococci (and micrococci) are catalase positive; streptococci and enterococci are catalase negative. Colonies must not be taken from blood agar with red cells, which contain catalase and give a false positive. Staphylococci are then split by the coagulase test (S. aureus positive; S. epidermidis and S. saprophyticus negative, separated by novobiocin: epidermidis sensitive, saprophyticus resistant). Streptococci are split by haemolysis on blood agar: beta (complete; group A bacitracin sensitive, group B bacitracin resistant and CAMP positive), alpha (partial, green; pneumococcus optochin sensitive and bile soluble, viridans resistant) and gamma (none; enterococci grow in bile and 6.5% salt). Catalase-positive organisms are the main threat in chronic granulomatous disease.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Coagulase test: Coagulase separates S. aureus from other staphylococci, a step taken only after catalase has placed the organism in that group.
  • B. Optochin test: Optochin separates pneumococci from viridans streptococci and is useful only once the organism is known to be a streptococcus.
  • C. Bacitracin test: Bacitracin separates group A from other beta-haemolytic streptococci, again a later step.
  • E. Oxidase test: Oxidase testing sorts gram-negative rods and does not distinguish these gram-positive cocci.

Key point: The catalase test separates staphylococci (positive) from streptococci and enterococci (negative); coagulase then identifies S. aureus.

Question 4 · Parasitology · medium

A 37-year-old man in Lahore has noticed flat, white, mobile segments about 2 cm long in his underclothes and bedding for 2 months. He has mild abdominal discomfort and a good appetite but has lost 2 kg. He often eats grilled beef kebabs that are pink inside. Microscopy of a segment shows a gravid uterus with more than 15 lateral branches, and the stool contains spherical eggs with radial striations.

Which tapeworm is most likely?

  1. A. Taenia saginata
  2. B. Taenia solium
  3. C. Diphyllobothrium latum
  4. D. Echinococcus granulosus
  5. E. Hymenolepis nana
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Taenia saginata

Taenia saginata, the beef tapeworm, is acquired by eating undercooked beef containing cysticerci; the adult can reach several metres in the small intestine. Patients often present because they notice gravid proglottids, which are actively motile and crawl out of the anus, appearing in underclothes and bedding, with little else besides vague abdominal discomfort, appetite change and slight weight loss. The species is distinguished from Taenia solium by the proglottid (more than 15 uterine branches, versus fewer in solium) and the scolex (four suckers without hooks, versus a rostellum with hooks in solium); the eggs of both are identical, so species identification needs proglottids, the scolex or PCR. Importantly, T. saginata does not cause cysticercosis in humans, so it carries none of the risk of neurological disease seen with T. solium. Treatment of either adult tapeworm is a single dose of praziquantel (or niclosamide), with care in T. solium carriers who may harbour cysts. Diphyllobothrium latum, from raw freshwater fish, competes for vitamin B12 and causes megaloblastic anaemia.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Taenia solium: T. solium comes from pork, sheds less active segments and carries fewer uterine branches than the 15 seen here.
  • C. Diphyllobothrium latum: The fish tapeworm follows raw freshwater fish and causes B12 deficiency; he eats undercooked beef kebabs.
  • D. Echinococcus granulosus: Echinococcus adults live in dogs; in people it forms cysts in organs rather than shedding segments into underclothes.
  • E. Hymenolepis nana: Hymenolepis is only a few centimetres long and sheds no visible segments, unlike the 2 cm proglottids he has found.

Key point: Taenia saginata from undercooked beef sheds actively motile proglottids with many uterine branches and does not cause cysticercosis.

Question 5 · Virology · medium

A 34-year-old man in Lahore is screened before starting work in a hospital kitchen. He feels well, with normal liver enzymes and no history of jaundice. His results are: HBsAg positive, anti-HBc IgG positive, anti-HBc IgM negative, HBeAg negative, anti-HBe positive, and HBV DNA 900 IU/mL. Liver ultrasound is normal.

What do these results indicate?

  1. A. Acute hepatitis B infection
  2. B. Past infection with recovery
  3. C. Inactive chronic carriage
  4. D. Response to vaccination
  5. E. Active chronic hepatitis
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Inactive chronic carriage

Hepatitis B serology is read as a panel. HBsAg means the virus is present; it appears first and persists beyond 6 months in chronic infection. Anti-HBc IgM indicates recent (acute) infection and is the only marker during the window period when HBsAg has gone and anti-HBs has not yet appeared; anti-HBc IgG persists for life after any natural infection and is absent after vaccination, which produces anti-HBs alone. HBeAg reflects active viral replication and high infectivity; its loss with the appearance of anti-HBe usually means replication has fallen. This man has chronic infection in the inactive carrier phase: HBsAg positive beyond the acute stage, anti-HBc IgG positive, HBeAg negative with anti-HBe, low viral load and normal transaminases. Such patients need no antiviral treatment but must be followed, because reactivation can occur, especially with immunosuppression or chemotherapy, and they remain at some risk of cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Acute hepatitis B infection: Acute infection would show anti-HBc IgM, which is negative, and he has normal enzymes with no history of jaundice.
  • B. Past infection with recovery: Recovery would clear HBsAg and leave anti-HBs, whereas his HBsAg is positive with detectable DNA.
  • D. Response to vaccination: Vaccination gives anti-HBs alone, with no anti-HBc and no surface antigen.
  • E. Active chronic hepatitis: Active chronic hepatitis would show raised transaminases and a far higher viral load than 900 IU/mL.

Key point: HBsAg with anti-HBc IgG, anti-HBe, low HBV DNA and normal enzymes indicates inactive chronic hepatitis B carriage, needing follow-up not treatment.

Question 6 · Immunology · easy

A 6-year-old boy from Peshawar has had recurrent ear, sinus and chest infections since the age of 2. He has had no opportunistic infections and is growing normally. On examination his tonsils are absent and no lymph nodes can be felt. Serum IgG is 90 mg/dL (reference 630-1500), IgA under 7 mg/dL (reference 33-200) and IgM 12 mg/dL (reference 40-180). CD19+ B cells are below 1% of lymphocytes; T-cell numbers are normal.

What is the most likely diagnosis?

  1. A. Selective IgA deficiency
  2. B. Common variable immunodeficiency
  3. C. X-linked agammaglobulinaemia
  4. D. DiGeorge syndrome
  5. E. Severe combined immunodeficiency
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. X-linked agammaglobulinaemia

X-linked agammaglobulinaemia (Bruton’s) results from a BTK mutation blocking B-cell maturation. This leads to absent peripheral B-cells, pan-hypogammaglobulinaemia, and lack of secondary lymphoid tissue (tonsils/nodes). Patients suffer recurrent bacterial infections after maternal IgG wanes (~6 months), but T-cell immunity remains intact.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Selective IgA deficiency: Presents with normal IgG/IgM and selective low IgA; tonsils/lymph nodes are present.
  • B. Common variable immunodeficiency: Usually presents later in life; B-cell numbers are typically normal despite low antibodies.
  • D. DiGeorge syndrome: DiGeorge syndrome causes thymic hypoplasia with low T cells, low calcium and heart defects; this boy has normal T cells and absent B cells instead.
  • E. Severe combined immunodeficiency: Causes both T and B cell dysfunction with severe opportunistic infections and failure to thrive.

Key point: Absent tonsils + low B-cells + pan-hypogammaglobulinaemia = XLA.

Question 7 · Bacteriology · medium

A 39-year-old shopkeeper in Rawalpindi has had burning upper abdominal pain for 5 months, worse when hungry at night and eased by eating. He takes no painkillers. Endoscopy shows a 1 cm ulcer in the duodenal cap. Biopsy of the gastric antrum shows curved gram-negative rods on the surface of the mucus layer, with neutrophils in the epithelium. The organism grows in low oxygen and is strongly urease positive.

How does urease help this organism survive in the stomach?

  1. A. It lets it swim through mucus
  2. B. It blocks acid secretion
  3. C. It raises local pH
  4. D. It digests the mucus layer
  5. E. It stops antibody binding
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. It raises local pH

Helicobacter pylori is a curved, oxidase- and catalase-positive gram-negative rod that lives in gastric mucus and infects a large share of the population in Pakistan, usually from childhood. Its many urease molecules split urea into ammonia and carbon dioxide; the ammonia buffers gastric acid in a cloud around the organism, allowing it to survive long enough to reach the near-neutral layer next to the epithelium, where its flagella propel it. Urease is the basis of diagnostic tests: the urea breath test (labelled urea by mouth, labelled carbon dioxide in the breath) and the rapid urease test on biopsy. Other virulence factors are flagella, adhesins, mucinase, and the CagA and VacA proteins, which cause cell damage. Antral infection raises gastrin and acid, causing duodenal ulcers; corpus infection causes atrophy, hypochlorhydria, gastric ulcer and raises cancer risk (gastric adenocarcinoma and MALT lymphoma). Treatment is triple therapy with a proton pump inhibitor plus two antibiotics.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. It lets it swim through mucus: Flagella provide the movement through mucus; the enzyme asked about is the one that makes ammonia from urea.
  • B. It blocks acid secretion: Acid secretion is often increased in his antral infection, which is why he has a duodenal ulcer.
  • D. It digests the mucus layer: Mucus is digested by other enzymes; urease makes ammonia to neutralise acid around the organism.
  • E. It stops antibody binding: Urease does not block antibody; his biopsy shows the organism surviving in acid next to the epithelium.

Key point: H. pylori urease makes ammonia that neutralises stomach acid around the organism, and is the basis of urea breath and rapid urease tests.

Question 8 · Mycology · medium

A 38-year-old gardener in Islamabad pricked her right index finger on a rose thorn 4 weeks ago. A small painless nodule formed there, which has ulcerated, and three further nodules have appeared in a line up her forearm. She has no fever and feels well. Her HIV test is negative. Biopsy culture at 25°C grows a mould, and at 37°C the same isolate grows as cigar-shaped yeasts.

What is the most appropriate treatment?

  1. A. Topical antifungal cream
  2. B. Oral co-trimoxazole
  3. C. Oral itraconazole
  4. D. Intravenous amphotericin B
  5. E. Excision of each nodule
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Oral itraconazole

This is lymphocutaneous sporotrichosis caused by Sporothrix schenckii, a dimorphic fungus living on plants, thorns, hay and sphagnum moss; it is implanted by a prick or scratch, so gardeners, farmers and florists are affected, and the disease is sometimes called "rose gardener's disease". A painless papule or nodule appears at the site, often ulcerating, and further nodules develop in a chain along the draining lymphatics, the sporotrichoid pattern also seen with Mycobacterium marinum, nocardiosis, leishmaniasis and tularaemia. Tissue shows cigar-shaped yeasts, and culture is dimorphic, growing as a mould at 25°C and a yeast at 37°C. Treatment is oral itraconazole for 3-6 months; potassium iodide is a cheaper traditional alternative, and amphotericin B is used for pulmonary, osteoarticular or disseminated disease, which occurs mainly in alcohol dependence, diabetes and immunosuppression. Wearing gloves and shoes prevents implantation injuries.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Topical antifungal cream: Cream cannot reach the nodules in her subcutaneous tissue and lymphatics up the forearm.
  • B. Oral co-trimoxazole: Co-trimoxazole suits actinomycetoma and nocardiosis; her culture was dimorphic, with cigar-shaped yeasts at 37°C.
  • D. Intravenous amphotericin B: Amphotericin is kept for lung, bone or disseminated disease; she is well, with nodules confined to one arm.
  • E. Excision of each nodule: Excising each nodule leaves the organism in the lymphatics, and new nodules would keep appearing up her arm.

Key point: Sporotrichosis follows thorn implantation and spreads as nodules along lymphatics; treat with oral itraconazole for months.

Question 9 · General microbiology · medium

A 55-year-old man has been on a ventilator for 9 days in a Peshawar intensive care unit after a head injury. He develops fever of 38.9°C, thick yellow tracheal secretions and a new shadow in the right lower lobe; oxygen needs rise. Tracheal aspirate shows plump gram-negative coccobacilli. They form pale non-lactose-fermenting colonies, are oxidase negative and non-motile, and are resistant to meropenem, piperacillin-tazobactam, ceftazidime and ciprofloxacin, but sensitive to colistin.

Which organism is most likely?

  1. A. Pseudomonas aeruginosa
  2. B. Klebsiella pneumoniae
  3. C. Acinetobacter baumannii
  4. D. Streptococcus pneumoniae
  5. E. Haemophilus influenzae
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Acinetobacter baumannii

Ventilator-associated pneumonia that starts after 4-5 days in hospital is usually caused by hospital organisms, many of them multidrug resistant: Acinetobacter baumannii, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Klebsiella and other Enterobacterales, and MRSA. Early-onset pneumonia (first 4 days) more often involves community organisms such as S. pneumoniae, H. influenzae and meticillin-sensitive S. aureus. Acinetobacter baumannii is a non-fermenting, oxidase-negative, non-motile gram-negative coccobacillus that survives for weeks on dry surfaces, equipment and hands. Carbapenem-resistant strains are common in intensive care units in Pakistan, often leaving only colistin, tigecycline or newer agents. Other common healthcare-associated infections: catheter-associated urinary infection (E. coli, Klebsiella, Enterococcus, Candida), central line infection (coagulase-negative staphylococci, S. aureus, Candida), surgical site infection (S. aureus) and C. difficile diarrhoea. Prevention relies on ventilator care bundles (head of bed raised, daily sedation breaks, oral care), hand hygiene and environmental cleaning.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Pseudomonas aeruginosa: Pseudomonas is oxidase positive and motile with a grape-like smell; her isolate is oxidase negative and non-motile.
  • B. Klebsiella pneumoniae: Klebsiella ferments lactose, giving pink mucoid colonies; her aspirate grew pale non-fermenting colonies that were non-motile.
  • D. Streptococcus pneumoniae: Pneumococcus is a gram-positive diplococcus seen in early-onset pneumonia, not on day 9 of ventilation.
  • E. Haemophilus influenzae: Haemophilus causes early-onset pneumonia and needs X and V factors, and would not resist meropenem and amikacin.

Key point: Late-onset ventilator-associated pneumonia is caused by hospital organisms; Acinetobacter baumannii is an oxidase-negative, often carbapenem-resistant coccobacillus.

Question 10 · Parasitology · medium

A 36-year-old man in Hyderabad was treated with metronidazole for amoebic dysentery and his symptoms settled within a week. He has returned to work as a cook in a school canteen. He feels well, with a normal abdomen and formed stools. His employer asks whether he is safe to handle food again, 10 days after finishing his treatment.

What is still needed before he returns to food handling?

  1. A. A repeat course of metronidazole
  2. B. Serology to confirm cure
  3. C. Nothing further, as he feels well
  4. D. A luminal agent to clear cysts
  5. E. Colonoscopy to confirm healing
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. A luminal agent to clear cysts

Treatment of invasive amoebiasis has two stages, and missing the second is a common error. A tissue amoebicide such as metronidazole or tinidazole clears trophozoites from the bowel wall, liver and other tissues and relieves symptoms, but it penetrates the lumen poorly and leaves cysts behind in a substantial minority of patients. A luminal agent must follow: paromomycin, diloxanide furoate or iodoquinol for 7 to 10 days, which eradicates cyst carriage, prevents relapse and stops the patient infecting others. Asymptomatic cyst passers found on screening are treated with a luminal agent alone when the species is confirmed as Entamoeba histolytica, and this matters most in food handlers, nursery staff and people living in institutions. Follow-up stool examination or antigen testing after treatment is used to confirm clearance before returning to food handling. Serology stays positive for years and is useful for diagnosing liver abscess, not for proving cure.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. A repeat course of metronidazole: Repeating a tissue amoebicide would again miss the cysts remaining in his bowel lumen.
  • B. Serology to confirm cure: Antibody persists for years after infection and cannot show whether he still passes cysts.
  • C. Nothing further, as he feels well: Feeling well does not stop cyst excretion, and he prepares food for schoolchildren.
  • E. Colonoscopy to confirm healing: Healing of ulcers does not address the cysts he may still be shedding into the canteen.

Key point: After metronidazole for amoebiasis, give a luminal agent such as paromomycin to clear cysts, especially in food handlers.
