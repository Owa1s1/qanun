Question 1 · Behavioural sciences and medical ethics · medium

A 40-year-old man with long-standing schizophrenia, stable on treatment, has gallstones causing occasional pain. Surgery is advised, but he refuses because he is frightened of anaesthesia. He can describe the condition, the operation and its risks, and he explains that he would rather have occasional pain than risk dying under anaesthetic. A junior doctor says that because he has schizophrenia his refusal can be ignored.

What is the most appropriate next step?

  1. A. Treat him without consent
  2. B. Assess his capacity
  3. C. Ask his brother to consent
  4. D. Detain him under mental health law
  5. E. Discharge him without follow-up
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Assess his capacity

Capacity is assessed for a specific decision at a specific time; it is not removed by a diagnosis such as schizophrenia, dementia or learning disability. A person has capacity for a decision if they can understand the relevant information, retain it long enough, use or weigh it to reach a decision, and communicate that decision. Adults are presumed to have capacity unless shown otherwise, and they must be helped to decide (with simple explanations, time and support). Here, his reasons are coherent and based on a real fear, not a delusion, so if he meets these tests his refusal must be respected, while offering ongoing care and the chance to reconsider.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Treat him without consent: A diagnosis of schizophrenia does not remove capacity, and this man is stable and reasoning clearly about the operation.
  • C. Ask his brother to consent: His brother cannot consent for him unless he lacks capacity, and nothing so far shows that he does.
  • D. Detain him under mental health law: Mental health legislation allows treatment of mental disorder, not a gallbladder operation a capable man has refused.
  • E. Discharge him without follow-up: His gallstones still need review and pain relief, so discharging him without follow-up abandons a treatable problem.

Key point: Capacity is decision-specific; a psychiatric diagnosis does not by itself mean a person lacks capacity.

Question 2 · Behavioural sciences and medical ethics · medium

A house officer prescribes intravenous morphine 10 mg instead of 1 mg for a 4-year-old girl after surgery because of a decimal point error. She becomes drowsy with slow breathing, is treated with naloxone and recovers fully within a few hours. Her parents ask why she suddenly became so sleepy. The house officer is afraid of being punished.

What should be done?

  1. A. Say it was a reaction to the anaesthetic
  2. B. Say nothing as she has recovered
  3. C. Blame the nurse who gave the drug
  4. D. Tell the parents and apologise
  5. E. Change the notes to hide the error
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. Tell the parents and apologise

When a patient is harmed by an error, doctors have a duty of candour: to tell the patient (or parents) promptly and honestly what happened, apologise, explain what is being done to put it right and prevent recurrence, and offer support. An apology is not an admission of legal liability. The error should also be reported through the hospital's incident reporting system so that systems can be improved (for example, standard dosing charts, decimal-point rules and double-checking of opioid doses in children). Hiding errors, blaming others or altering records are serious professional misconduct. Senior colleagues should support the junior doctor in a learning, not punitive, culture.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Say it was a reaction to the anaesthetic: Blaming the anaesthetic is a lie, and her parents are entitled to know why their daughter stopped breathing properly.
  • B. Say nothing as she has recovered: She recovered, but a ten-fold morphine overdose that needed naloxone is exactly the kind of event parents must be told about.
  • C. Blame the nurse who gave the drug: The nurse gave what was prescribed; shifting blame is both untrue and unjust to a colleague.
  • E. Change the notes to hide the error: Altering the notes turns a reportable prescribing error into misconduct that could end a career.

Key point: When an error causes harm, tell the patient or family honestly, apologise, and report it so systems can improve.

Question 3 · Behavioural sciences and medical ethics · medium

An 84-year-old man with end-stage heart failure and kidney failure has been in hospital for 3 weeks, bedbound and deteriorating despite full treatment. The team believes that if his heart stops, CPR would not succeed and would only cause suffering. He has capacity and wants to talk about his care. His family want "everything done".

What is the most appropriate approach?

  1. A. Discuss a DNACPR decision with him
  2. B. Offer CPR because the family request it
  3. C. Decide quietly without telling him
  4. D. Stop all treatment, including comfort care
  5. E. Transfer him to intensive care
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Discuss a DNACPR decision with him

Cardiopulmonary resuscitation is a treatment like any other: if the clinical team judges that it would not succeed or would not provide any overall benefit, doctors are not obliged to offer it, even if the family requests it. However, the decision should usually be discussed with a capable patient, sensitively and honestly, as part of wider advance care planning, unless doing so would cause him serious harm. The family should be involved with his consent and helped to understand the reasons. A "do not attempt CPR" decision applies only to CPR: all other appropriate treatment, symptom control and comfort care continue. Decisions are documented and reviewed.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Offer CPR because the family request it: The family's wish cannot create a benefit that is not there; CPR that cannot restart his heart need not be offered.
  • C. Decide quietly without telling him: Deciding behind his back denies a man with capacity any say in what happens when his heart stops.
  • D. Stop all treatment, including comfort care: Comfort care continues whatever is decided about CPR; a DNACPR decision does not stop treating his symptoms.
  • E. Transfer him to intensive care: Intensive care cannot reverse end-stage heart and kidney failure and would move him away from his family to die.

Key point: A DNACPR decision applies only to CPR; it should be discussed honestly with a capable patient, and all other care continues.

Question 4 · Behavioural sciences and medical ethics · medium

A 60-year-old man has a 2 cm kidney stone. Two treatments are reasonable: shock-wave lithotripsy, which avoids an operation but may need repeating, and keyhole surgery, which is more likely to clear the stone in one go but carries slightly more risk. He works as a farmer and is worried about time off work. The urologist wants to handle the choice in the best way.

Which approach is best?

  1. A. The urologist decides
  2. B. Give him leaflets and let him decide
  3. C. Let his family decide
  4. D. Shared decision-making
  5. E. Choose the cheaper option automatically
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. Shared decision-making

Shared decision-making is a process in which the doctor and patient decide together: the doctor provides information about the options, their benefits, risks and uncertainties (often with decision aids), and the patient brings his values, preferences and circumstances, such as his work and fears. It is particularly suitable when more than one reasonable option exists. It differs from the paternalistic model (the doctor decides what is best), the informative or consumer model (the doctor provides facts and the patient decides alone) and the interpretive model. Shared decisions improve knowledge, satisfaction and adherence, and respect autonomy while supporting the patient.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. The urologist decides: Deciding alone leaves out the thing that matters most to him: how much time off the farm each option costs.
  • B. Give him leaflets and let him decide: Leaflets give him the facts but leave him to weigh repeat treatments against surgical risk without help.
  • C. Let his family decide: He has capacity and it is his kidney, his risk and his harvest; the family cannot weigh these for him.
  • E. Choose the cheaper option automatically: Cost may matter to him, but choosing on price alone ignores the repeat treatments and time off he is worried about.

Key point: When there are several reasonable options, shared decision-making combines medical evidence with the patient's values.

Question 5 · Behavioural sciences and medical ethics · medium

A 45-year-old man with diabetes and a high HbA1c says he wants to see his children grow up and walk his daughter down the aisle, but he continues to drink sugary drinks and does not take his tablets. The doctor, using motivational interviewing, gently highlights the difference between what he wants for his future and how his current behaviour affects it, without lecturing him.

Which motivational interviewing technique is the doctor using?

  1. A. Confronting and warning
  2. B. Developing discrepancy
  3. C. Rolling with resistance
  4. D. Giving advice directly
  5. E. Supporting self-efficacy
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Developing discrepancy

Motivational interviewing is a collaborative, patient-centred counselling style that strengthens a person's own motivation to change, especially when they are ambivalent. Its principles include expressing empathy through reflective listening, developing discrepancy (helping patients see the gap between their goals and values and their current behaviour), rolling with resistance (avoiding argument and confrontation, which increase resistance), and supporting self-efficacy (building confidence that change is possible). Techniques use open questions, affirmations, reflections and summaries (OARS) and draw out "change talk" from the patient. It is effective for smoking, alcohol, diet, exercise and adherence.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Confronting and warning: Confronting and warning is what previous lectures would do; motivational interviewing deliberately avoids it.
  • C. Rolling with resistance: Rolling with resistance means not arguing when he pushes back, but he is not resisting at this point.
  • D. Giving advice directly: Direct advice would be telling him to stop the sugary drinks, which is not what the doctor is doing.
  • E. Supporting self-efficacy: Supporting self-efficacy would mean building his confidence that he can change, which comes later.

Key point: In motivational interviewing, developing discrepancy helps patients see the gap between their goals and their behaviour.

Question 6 · Behavioural sciences and medical ethics · easy

A 62-year-old retired schoolteacher with diabetes has wet gangrene of the right foot. The surgeons advise a below-knee amputation and explain that without it he may die of sepsis. He listens carefully, repeats the risks back accurately, and says he would rather take that risk than lose his leg, because he values being whole more than living longer. His family urges the doctors to operate anyway.

Which ethical principle requires the doctors to respect his refusal?

  1. A. Beneficence
  2. B. Justice
  3. C. Autonomy
  4. D. Non-maleficence
  5. E. Paternalism
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Autonomy

The four principles of medical ethics are respect for autonomy (respecting the informed choices of capable individuals), beneficence (acting in the patient's best interests), non-maleficence (avoiding harm) and justice (fairness in the distribution of benefits, risks and resources). An adult with decision-making capacity has the right to refuse any treatment, even life-saving treatment, for reasons that others think unwise. The doctors should make sure he understands, explore his reasons, offer alternatives (such as antibiotics, wound care and palliative care), document the discussion and continue to care for him. Relatives cannot consent or refuse on behalf of a capable adult. Operating against his will would be assault.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Beneficence: Beneficence would push the team towards the amputation that saves his life, which is the course he is refusing, not the reason to accept his refusal.
  • B. Justice: Justice concerns fair sharing of theatre time and beds between patients; nothing here turns on competing claims to a scarce resource.
  • D. Non-maleficence: Non-maleficence is about not harming him; amputating against his will would itself be the harm, so this does not settle the question.
  • E. Paternalism: Paternalism would let the family and surgeons overrule his reasoned choice, which is the opposite of what respecting his refusal requires.

Key point: A capable adult may refuse even life-saving treatment; respecting this is the principle of autonomy.

Question 7 · Behavioural sciences and medical ethics · easy

A 30-year-old bank officer has had a runny nose, mild sore throat and dry cough for 3 days. He is well, with a normal temperature, clear chest and no tonsillar exudate. He insists on an antibiotic injection because he has an important meeting. The doctor explains that the illness is viral and declines, offering advice on symptom relief and when to return.

Which principle mainly supports the doctor's refusal?

  1. A. Non-maleficence
  2. B. Respect for autonomy
  3. C. Justice alone
  4. D. Confidentiality
  5. E. Veracity
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Non-maleficence

Non-maleficence means "first, do no harm". An unnecessary antibiotic offers no benefit for a viral cold but can cause side effects, allergic reactions (including anaphylaxis with injections), Clostridioides difficile infection and antibiotic resistance, which also harms others (a justice concern). Respect for autonomy gives patients the right to accept or refuse treatments offered, but not to demand treatments that are not clinically indicated. A good response explains the reasons kindly, addresses the patient's concerns (here, his meeting), offers symptom relief and safety-netting advice, and documents the discussion.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Respect for autonomy: Autonomy lets him refuse treatment, not demand an injection that cannot help; his insistence on it does not create a duty to give it.
  • C. Justice alone: Justice matters, since needless antibiotics drive resistance for everyone, but the first duty here is not to harm this man.
  • D. Confidentiality: Confidentiality is about keeping his information private; nothing in the consultation turns on who may be told what.
  • E. Veracity: Veracity requires the honest explanation the doctor has already given; it supports the conversation, not the decision to withhold the drug.

Key point: Doctors should not give treatments that offer no benefit and may cause harm; autonomy does not entitle patients to demand them.

Question 8 · Behavioural sciences and medical ethics · easy

A 30-year-old motorcyclist is brought to the emergency department in Lahore unconscious after a crash, with signs of a rapidly enlarging extradural haematoma on CT. He needs an urgent operation to save his life. He carries no identity documents, no relatives can be found, and nothing is known of his wishes. The neurosurgeon hesitates because there is no consent.

What is the most appropriate action?

  1. A. Wait until relatives are found
  2. B. Ask the police to consent
  3. C. Transfer him without treatment
  4. D. Operate in his best interests
  5. E. Wait for him to wake up
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. Operate in his best interests

When a patient lacks capacity because of unconsciousness and needs urgent treatment to save life or prevent serious harm, doctors may provide treatment that is immediately necessary in the patient's best interests without consent (the principle of necessity, sometimes called implied or presumed consent). Treatment should be limited to what is needed for the emergency; non-urgent decisions should wait until the patient can decide. If there is a known, valid refusal of the specific treatment (for example an advance refusal), it should be respected. The reasons and the urgency should be documented, and relatives informed when found.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Wait until relatives are found: The haematoma is enlarging now, so a search for relatives that may take hours will outlast the chance to save him.
  • B. Ask the police to consent: The police can confirm his identity but have no power to consent to an operation on his behalf.
  • C. Transfer him without treatment: Moving him elsewhere wastes the minutes that matter and offers nothing the neurosurgeon cannot already do.
  • E. Wait for him to wake up: He is unconscious from a haematoma that is compressing his brain; he will not wake without the operation.

Key point: In an emergency, an unconscious patient may be given necessary treatment without consent in his best interests.

Question 9 · Behavioural sciences and medical ethics · easy

A 45-year-old man is admitted for investigation of weight loss, and a biopsy shows tuberculosis of the intestine. Before the doctor has spoken to him, his brother and cousin stop the doctor in the corridor and ask what the biopsy showed. The patient is alert and has capacity. He has not said whom he wants to be told about his health.

What should the doctor do?

  1. A. Ask the patient first
  2. B. Tell them, since they are family
  3. C. Tell only the brother
  4. D. Tell them it is cancer to prepare them
  5. E. Refuse to speak to them at all
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Ask the patient first

Information about a patient's health is confidential and should not be disclosed to others, including relatives, without the patient's consent, unless there is a legal requirement or a strong public interest justification. Families in Pakistan are often closely involved in care, and many patients want them to be, but the decision belongs to the capable adult patient. The doctor should speak to the patient first, give him the result, and ask whom he would like to be informed and how much they should be told. The relatives can be told politely that the doctor will discuss the results with the patient and, with his agreement, with them.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Tell them, since they are family: Being family gives no automatic right to his results; the information is his until he says otherwise.
  • C. Tell only the brother: Telling the brother alone is still disclosure without permission, whatever the family relationship.
  • D. Tell them it is cancer to prepare them: Inventing a diagnosis of cancer is both a lie and a breach, and would reach him before the truth does.
  • E. Refuse to speak to them at all: Shutting the family out helps nobody; ask the patient first what may be shared, then talk to them within that.

Key point: Do not disclose a capable adult's diagnosis to relatives without his consent; ask the patient first.

Question 10 · Behavioural sciences and medical ethics · easy

A doctor needs to tell a 52-year-old woman that her breast biopsy shows invasive cancer. The ward round is busy, the patient is in a six-bed bay with visitors present, the doctor's phone keeps ringing, and the patient's husband is on his way. The doctor wants to follow a recognised structured approach to breaking bad news.

What should the doctor do first?

  1. A. Tell her at the bedside right away
  2. B. Ask a nurse to tell her
  3. C. Find a private room and time
  4. D. Give her a leaflet and leave
  5. E. Tell her husband first, alone
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Find a private room and time

Structured approaches such as SPIKES help doctors break bad news well. Setting: arrange a private, quiet room, enough time without interruptions (phone handed to a colleague), sit down, and invite the people the patient wants present. Perception: find out what the patient already knows or suspects. Invitation: ask how much information she wants. Knowledge: give a warning shot, then the news clearly in simple language, in small amounts, avoiding jargon. Emotions: allow silence, respond with empathy and acknowledge feelings. Strategy and summary: agree a plan, next steps and support, and check understanding. The setting is the first step.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Tell her at the bedside right away: Telling her in a six-bed bay with visitors present and the phone ringing gives her no privacy at the worst moment of her year.
  • B. Ask a nurse to tell her: Breaking news of cancer is the doctor's task, and delegating it to a nurse leaves her questions unanswered.
  • D. Give her a leaflet and leave: A leaflet cannot answer her questions or support her, and leaving her alone with the news is abandonment.
  • E. Tell her husband first, alone: She has capacity and decides who hears her result; telling her husband first takes that from her.

Key point: In breaking bad news (SPIKES), start by arranging a private setting with enough time and the people the patient wants present.
