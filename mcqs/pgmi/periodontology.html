Question 1 · The periodontium, classification and periodontal examination · medium

A 58-year-old man attends a dental hospital in Charsadda for periodontal assessment. Tooth 46 has 30 percent bone loss, a probing depth of 5 millimetres, grade 1 mobility and a through-and-through (class III) furcation. He does not smoke and his plaque control is good.

Which finding gives the poorest prognosis for tooth 46?

  1. A. Probing depth of 5 millimetres
  2. B. Grade 1 mobility
  3. C. His age of 58 years
  4. D. 30 percent bone loss
  5. E. Class III furcation involvement
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Class III furcation involvement

Prognosis weighs patient-related factors such as smoking and diabetes against tooth-related factors including furcation class, mobility, bone loss and pocket depth. A class III furcation is a through-and-through defect that makes plaque control and periodontal access extremely difficult, so it is a strong indicator of poor molar survival. Supportive therapy and strict plaque control can modify the outlook. Grade C periodontitis would further indicate a significant risk of rapid progression.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Probing depth of 5 millimetres: A 5 mm pocket responds well to scaling and root planing.
  • B. Grade 1 mobility: Slight mobility with 30 percent bone loss is common and does not by itself worsen the outlook much.
  • C. His age of 58 years: Age alone is a weak prognostic factor; attachment lost at an older age suggests slower disease.
  • D. 30 percent bone loss: Bone loss limited to the coronal third leaves good support.

Key point: Identify class III furcation involvement as the strongest poor prognostic factor for a molar.

Question 2 · Gingival and periodontal diseases in the clinic · easy

A 38-year-old man attends a dental clinic in Karachi with throbbing pain and a swelling in the lower right back tooth region for two days. He has poor oral hygiene and smokes fifteen cigarettes a day. Examination shows a tender swelling beside 46, the lower right first molar, with pus expressed from a deep pocket. Tooth 46 responds normally to cold testing and is not carious.

What is the most likely diagnosis?

  1. A. Periodontal abscess
  2. B. Periapical abscess
  3. C. Pericoronitis
  4. D. Gingival abscess
  5. E. Lateral periodontal cyst
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Periodontal abscess

A periodontal abscess is an acute localised collection of pus within the periodontium, usually arising from a deep periodontal pocket. The involved tooth is typically vital and responds normally to pulp testing, and there is no caries. Pus can be expressed from the pocket opening. This differs from a periapical abscess, which follows pulp necrosis, and from pericoronitis, which involves a partially erupted tooth. Initial management is drainage through the pocket and subgingival debridement, with antibiotics only if systemic signs are present.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Periapical abscess: A periapical abscess requires a non-vital or carious tooth with periapical radiolucency; 46 is vital and sound.
  • C. Pericoronitis: Pericoronitis affects the operculum around a partially erupted tooth, usually a lower third molar, not a pocket beside 46.
  • D. Gingival abscess: A gingival abscess is confined to the marginal gingiva without involvement of the periodontal pocket or bone.
  • E. Lateral periodontal cyst: A lateral periodontal cyst is usually symptomless and found on radiographs; it does not drain pus from a pocket.

Key point: Diagnose a periodontal abscess from pus in a pocket beside a vital non-carious tooth.

Question 3 · Causes and pathogenesis of periodontal disease · medium

A 40-year-old man from Charsadda visits a general dental practice with calculus on the lower front teeth and deep periodontal pockets. He smokes ten cigarettes daily and has not brushed regularly for years. Subgingival plaque is sampled from a 6 mm pocket around 44.

Which organisms predominate in subgingival plaque from this deep pocket?

  1. A. Gram-positive facultative cocci
  2. B. Gram-negative anaerobic rods and spirochetes
  3. C. Yeasts such as Candida
  4. D. Gram-positive rods such as Actinomyces
  5. E. Obligate aerobes
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Gram-negative anaerobic rods and spirochetes

Supragingival plaque is exposed to oxygen and saliva and is dominated by gram-positive aerobes and facultative anaerobes, especially streptococci and Actinomyces. Subgingival plaque lies in an anaerobic, crevicular fluid-rich environment and shifts to gram-negative anaerobic rods and spirochetes as periodontal disease progresses. In this 40-year-old smoker from Charsadda with a 6 mm pocket around 44, the subgingival flora would include Porphyromonas gingivalis, Tannerella forsythia and Treponema species. Cigarette smoking further alters the subgingival ecosystem and impairs host defence. Mechanical root debridement is required to remove this flora.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Gram-positive facultative cocci: Streptococci dominate supragingival plaque; the deep pocket favours anaerobes.
  • C. Yeasts such as Candida: Yeasts may be present, especially in the immunocompromised, but are not the main subgingival flora.
  • D. Gram-positive rods such as Actinomyces: Actinomyces are early colonisers and common in supragingival plaque and gingivitis, not the dominant flora of deep pockets.
  • E. Obligate aerobes: The deep pocket has very little oxygen; obligate aerobes fall, not rise, as pockets deepen.

Key point: Distinguish subgingival gram-negative anaerobic flora from supragingival gram-positive flora.

Question 4 · Periodontal treatment, surgery and implants · medium

A 17-year-old girl attends a private dental clinic in Hyderabad with fixed orthodontic brackets on her upper and lower arches. She has gingival inflammation around the brackets and difficulty cleaning beneath the archwire. She brushes twice daily but plaque accumulates around the brackets.

Which interdental cleaning aid is most effective for her?

  1. A. Dental floss
  2. B. Interdental brush
  3. C. Water flosser
  4. D. End-tuft brush
  5. E. Wooden interdental stick
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Interdental brush

Interdental brushes can pass beneath orthodontic archwires and clean the interproximal spaces and around brackets effectively, making them the preferred mechanical aid for fixed appliance patients. Floss threaders are an alternative but are more difficult to use. Water flossers and wooden sticks are adjuncts, and end-tuft brushes are for localized access. Fluoride toothpaste should be used to reduce decalcification risk.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Dental floss: Dental floss requires a threader under the archwire and is technique-sensitive for adolescents with fixed appliances.
  • C. Water flosser: A water flosser is a helpful adjunct but does not remove plaque around brackets as effectively as an interdental brush.
  • D. End-tuft brush: An end-tuft brush is useful for isolated areas but cannot clean the full interproximal space efficiently.
  • E. Wooden interdental stick: Wooden sticks remove food debris but do not disrupt biofilm around orthodontic brackets.

Key point: Recommend an interdental brush for plaque control around fixed orthodontic brackets.

Question 5 · The periodontium, classification and periodontal examination · easy

A 19-year-old man attends a private dental clinic in Swat with sore, bleeding gums for three weeks. He smokes five cigarettes daily. Examination shows generalized marginal gingival inflammation. The dentist explains that the gingiva is attached to the tooth by a specialized epithelium at the base of the sulcus.

Which feature is most characteristic of this epithelium?

  1. A. Keratinized stratified squamous epithelium with prominent rete ridges
  2. B. Non-keratinized stratified squamous epithelium with rete ridges and a thick granular layer
  3. C. Non-keratinized epithelium with rete ridges, lining the sulcus wall
  4. D. Parakeratinized epithelium with rete ridges, bound to the periosteum
  5. E. Non-keratinized cells attached to the tooth by hemidesmosomes and an internal basal lamina
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Non-keratinized cells attached to the tooth by hemidesmosomes and an internal basal lamina

The junctional epithelium is a non-keratinized stratified squamous epithelium that extends from the sulcus to the cementoenamel junction. Its basal cells form hemidesmosomes with the tooth and an internal basal lamina, providing the epithelial attachment. Unlike oral epithelium, it has no rete ridges and a minimal granular layer. Its high turnover and permeability make it a key site where plaque bacteria initiate gingival inflammation. In Pakistani dental practice, patients often present with bleeding gums after irregular brushing or tobacco use.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Keratinized stratified squamous epithelium with prominent rete ridges: This describes oral epithelium covering the external gingiva, not the attachment epithelium at the sulcus base.
  • B. Non-keratinized stratified squamous epithelium with rete ridges and a thick granular layer: Rete ridges and a granular layer are features of oral epithelium; the junctional epithelium lacks both.
  • C. Non-keratinized epithelium with rete ridges, lining the sulcus wall: This is sulcular epithelium, which lines the sulcus but is not attached to the tooth and has rete ridges.
  • D. Parakeratinized epithelium with rete ridges, bound to the periosteum: This is attached gingiva, the oral epithelium bound to bone; it does not attach to the tooth.

Key point: Identify the histological features of the junctional epithelium.

Question 6 · Gingival and periodontal diseases in the clinic · medium

A 45-year-old woman visits a dental teaching hospital in Quetta with recurrent swelling beside 36, the lower left first molar. She has a 6 mm pocket with bleeding on probing and subgingival calculus. The tooth is vital and responds normally to thermal testing.

What is the most likely predisposing cause of the abscess?

  1. A. Haematogenous spread from a distant infection
  2. B. Blockage of the periodontal pocket outlet by calculus or food
  3. C. Perforation of the gingiva by a toothbrush bristle
  4. D. Spread of infection from a periapical lesion through the periodontal ligament
  5. E. Allergic reaction to dental materials
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Blockage of the periodontal pocket outlet by calculus or food

A periodontal abscess develops when infection in a periodontal pocket cannot drain because the pocket outlet is obstructed by calculus, food debris or semilunar exostosis. The resulting pressure causes acute pain, swelling and pus formation. The tooth is usually vital. Treatment is drainage through the pocket and removal of the obstructing calculus or debris. Haematogenous spread, periapical spread and allergic reactions are not typical causes of a periodontal abscess in clinical practice.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Haematogenous spread from a distant infection: Periodontal abscesses arise locally from a blocked pocket, not from blood-borne spread of infection.
  • C. Perforation of the gingiva by a toothbrush bristle: A bristle injury may cause a minor gingival wound but not a deep periodontal abscess.
  • D. Spread of infection from a periapical lesion through the periodontal ligament: That pathway produces a combined endo-perio lesion, usually in a non-vital tooth, unlike this vital 36.
  • E. Allergic reaction to dental materials: Allergy causes mucosal inflammation or ulceration, not a localised pus-filled periodontal abscess.

Key point: Identify blockage of the periodontal pocket outlet as the predisposing cause of a periodontal abscess.

Question 7 · Causes and pathogenesis of periodontal disease · medium

A 40-year-old woman visits a dental OPD in D.I. Khan with a painful, rapidly progressive gum swelling around 36 (lower left first molar) for 3 days. She has type 1 diabetes, HbA1c 10.5% (4.0-5.6). She smokes 20 cigarettes daily. Examination shows 36 with a deep periodontal pocket, purulent discharge, vertical bone loss, and tenderness to percussion. No caries or pulp exposure is present.

What is the most likely diagnosis?

  1. A. Periodontal abscess
  2. B. Periapical abscess
  3. C. Pericoronitis
  4. D. Gingival cyst
  5. E. Periodontal cyst
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Periodontal abscess

A periodontal abscess is a localised purulent infection within a periodontal pocket, often associated with deep pockets, vertical bone loss, and systemic conditions such as diabetes. It presents with pain, swelling, and pus discharge. Unlike a periapical abscess, there is no caries or pulp involvement. Treatment involves drainage, deep debridement of the pocket, and control of diabetes. Smoking and poor glycaemic control increase the risk of severe or recurrent abscesses.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Periapical abscess: No carious lesion or pulp exposure is described to suggest an endodontic origin.
  • C. Pericoronitis: Pericoronitis affects a partially erupted tooth, usually a lower third molar, not 36 with a pocket.
  • D. Gingival cyst: A gingival cyst is a painless developmental swelling, not an acute purulent lesion.
  • E. Periodontal cyst: A periodontal cyst is a slow-growing radiolucency, not an acute painful swelling with pus.

Key point: Identify a periodontal abscess from a deep pocket, pus, and vertical bone loss.

Question 8 · Periodontal treatment, surgery and implants · medium

A 55-year-old man undergoes modified Widman flap surgery at a dental teaching hospital in Peshawar for a 7 mm pocket on 46 (lower right first molar). Four weeks after surgery, the site is clinically healthy with no bleeding on probing. The periodontist explains the type of healing that has occurred.

What is the primary healing outcome after modified Widman flap surgery?

  1. A. New cementum and periodontal ligament
  2. B. Junctional epithelium at its original level
  3. C. Gingival connective tissue attachment only
  4. D. Regeneration of alveolar bone
  5. E. Long junctional epithelium
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Long junctional epithelium

Modified Widman flap surgery eliminates pockets by providing access for thorough root debridement. The wound usually heals by repair, forming a long junctional epithelium that extends apically toward the alveolar bone, rather than by true regeneration of cementum, periodontal ligament, and bone. Regeneration requires additional techniques such as guided tissue regeneration, bone grafts, or enamel matrix derivative. The result is a shallow, maintainable sulcus.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. New cementum and periodontal ligament: True regeneration with new cementum and periodontal ligament requires guided tissue regeneration or grafts, not a flap alone.
  • B. Junctional epithelium at its original level: This would imply no apical migration of the epithelium, which is not the usual result after pocket surgery.
  • C. Gingival connective tissue attachment only: Connective tissue attachment alone without junctional epithelium is not the standard healing pattern.
  • D. Regeneration of alveolar bone: Bone regeneration requires additional materials such as grafts or membranes.

Key point: State that flap surgery heals mainly by long junctional epithelium, not true regeneration.

Question 9 · The periodontium, classification and periodontal examination · easy

A 25-year-old woman attends a general dental practice in Karachi for a routine check-up. She has no pain or bleeding, and her gingiva is healthy.

Which of the following is a characteristic clinical feature of healthy gingiva?

  1. A. Coral pink color with an orange-peel texture
  2. B. Bluish-red color and spontaneous bleeding
  3. C. Smooth, shiny surface without stippling
  4. D. Rolling, folded margin covering the clinical crown
  5. E. Ulcerated papillae with purulent discharge
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Coral pink color with an orange-peel texture

Healthy gingiva is coral pink, firm, and resilient, with a knife-edge margin following the scalloped contour of the teeth. The attached gingiva often shows stippling, resembling an orange peel, due to the connective tissue papillae beneath the epithelium. The gingival sulcus is shallow, usually 1 to 3 mm, and there is no bleeding on probing. Stippling may be less visible in patients with high melanin pigmentation, which is common in many Pakistani populations, but firmness and absence of bleeding remain the key signs.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Bluish-red color and spontaneous bleeding: These are signs of chronic gingival inflammation, not a healthy periodontium.
  • C. Smooth, shiny surface without stippling: A smooth surface suggests edema or inflammation; healthy attached gingiva is usually stippled.
  • D. Rolling, folded margin covering the clinical crown: A rolling margin that covers the clinical crown is not a feature of healthy gingival anatomy.
  • E. Ulcerated papillae with purulent discharge: Ulcerated papillae with purulent discharge indicate a periodontal abscess or necrotizing periodontal disease.

Key point: Recognize clinical features of healthy gingiva.

Question 10 · Gingival and periodontal diseases in the clinic · easy

A 20-year-old male presents at a dental teaching hospital in Peshawar with bleeding gums while brushing for three months. He brushes once daily with a medium-bristle brush. Extraoral examination is normal. Intraoral examination shows red, swollen marginal gingiva around all anterior teeth. Probing depths are 3 mm with bleeding on probing. There is no clinical attachment loss or alveolar bone loss on radiographs.

What is the most likely diagnosis?

  1. A. Periodontitis stage I grade A
  2. B. Plaque-induced gingivitis
  3. C. Necrotising gingivitis
  4. D. Hereditary gingival fibromatosis
  5. E. Leukaemic gingival enlargement
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Plaque-induced gingivitis

Plaque-induced gingivitis is inflammation confined to the gingiva without loss of clinical attachment or alveolar bone. It presents as redness, swelling and bleeding on probing, often related to poor oral hygiene. It is reversible with mechanical plaque control, including toothbrushing and interdental cleaning. Periodontitis is distinguished by attachment loss and bone destruction, which are absent here. Necrotising gingivitis causes pain and papillary necrosis. Fibromatosis and leukaemic infiltration produce enlargement without the inflammatory bleeding pattern described. These findings fit reversible plaque-induced gingivitis.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Periodontitis stage I grade A: Periodontitis stage I requires clinical attachment loss and radiographic bone loss, which are absent.
  • C. Necrotising gingivitis: Necrotising gingivitis presents with pain, necrosis and punched-out interdental papillae, none of which are seen here.
  • D. Hereditary gingival fibromatosis: Hereditary gingival fibromatosis causes firm, painless, slowly progressive enlargement, not acute bleeding inflammation.
  • E. Leukaemic gingival enlargement: Leukaemic gingival enlargement is usually associated with pallor, petechiae or systemic illness, absent.

Key point: Identify plaque-induced gingivitis by bleeding on probing without attachment loss.
