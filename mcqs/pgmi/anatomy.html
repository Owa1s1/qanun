Question 1 · Abdomen and pelvis · easy

A 50-year-old man has a stone-filled upper pole of the left kidney. During an open flank approach to the upper pole of the left kidney, the surgeon removes part of the 12th rib. The patient is breathless after the operation, and the chest X-ray shows air in the pleural cavity on that side. No lung injury is found.

Why can the pleura be injured in this approach?

  1. A. The pleura covers the front of the kidney
  2. B. The costodiaphragmatic recess extends behind the upper part of the kidney
  3. C. The lung lies directly on the kidney
  4. D. The kidney lies inside the thorax
  5. E. The phrenic nerve passes behind the kidney on its way down to the diaphragm
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. The costodiaphragmatic recess extends behind the upper part of the kidney

The upper part of each kidney lies on the diaphragm, which separates it from the costodiaphragmatic recess of the pleura. The recess extends down to about the 12th rib at the back (and the 11th and 12th ribs lie behind the upper kidney). Removing or cutting near the 12th rib in a flank incision can open the pleura, causing a pneumothorax. The lower part of the kidney lies on psoas, quadratus lumborum and transversus abdominis, with the subcostal, iliohypogastric and ilioinguinal nerves behind it. The right kidney lies a little lower than the left because of the liver.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. The pleura covers the front of the kidney: The front of the kidney is related to abdominal organs.
  • C. The lung lies directly on the kidney: The lung does not reach this low behind the kidney.
  • D. The kidney lies inside the thorax: The kidney is retroperitoneal in the abdomen.
  • E. The phrenic nerve passes behind the kidney on its way down to the diaphragm: The phrenic nerve lies on the diaphragm above.

Key point: The pleural costodiaphragmatic recess extends behind the upper kidney to the 12th rib, so flank surgery can cause a pneumothorax.

Question 2 · General anatomy, embryology and histology · easy

A pathologist in Peshawar examines a placenta from a miscarriage at ten weeks. The chorionic villi look like swollen grapes with fluid inside. There is no embryo and no amniotic sac. Genetic testing shows all forty-six chromosomes come from the father.

How would a partial mole differ from this one in its origin?

  1. A. It also has a paternal-only set of chromosomes, but only 23 of them
  2. B. It is triploid, with two paternal sets, and fetal tissue
  3. C. It has 46 chromosomes, all from the mother, with no fetal tissue
  4. D. It has a normal 46 chromosomes and arises from an infection of the villi
  5. E. It has 92 chromosomes and always contains a normal fetus
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. It is triploid, with two paternal sets, and fetal tissue

A complete mole, as in this case, forms when a sperm fertilises an ovum whose nucleus is absent or lost and then duplicates its own chromosomes, giving 46 chromosomes that are all paternal, usually 46,XX. There is no embryo, all the villi are swollen, trophoblast proliferation is marked and hCG is very high. A partial mole is triploid, usually 69,XXY, from fertilisation of a normal ovum by two sperm: one maternal and two paternal sets, with fetal parts, a mixture of normal and swollen villi, and lower hCG. Both need removal and then follow-up with serial hCG, because trophoblast can persist or become choriocarcinoma; the risk is much higher after a complete mole.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. It also has a paternal-only set of chromosomes, but only 23 of them: A single paternal set alone cannot develop; the complete mole described here carries a duplicated paternal set.
  • C. It has 46 chromosomes, all from the mother, with no fetal tissue: An ovum that divides with only maternal chromosomes gives an ovarian teratoma, not a mole.
  • D. It has a normal 46 chromosomes and arises from an infection of the villi: Molar change is a genetic accident of fertilisation, not an infection.
  • E. It has 92 chromosomes and always contains a normal fetus: A tetraploid partial mole is described but is rare, and a partial mole never contains a normal, viable fetus.

Key point: Contrast the diandric diploid origin of a complete mole with the triploid origin of a partial mole.

Question 3 · Head and neck · easy

A 50-year-old woman has a pleomorphic adenoma removed from her left parotid gland, but the tumour was wrapped around the facial nerve trunk. After the operation she cannot close her left eye, raise her eyebrow or smile on that side, and her forehead is smooth. But her taste on the front of the tongue and tear production are normal, and loud sounds are not uncomfortable.

Why are taste and tears spared?

  1. A. Taste and tear fibres leave the nerve before it reaches the parotid
  2. B. Taste and tears are carried by the trigeminal nerve
  3. C. The parotid gland itself controls taste and tear secretion
  4. D. The surgeon cut the sensory root of the facial nerve
  5. E. Tears are controlled by the oculomotor nerve, not the facial nerve
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Taste and tear fibres leave the nerve before it reaches the parotid

The facial nerve gives off the greater petrosal nerve (tears and nasal glands) at the geniculate ganglion, the nerve to stapedius, and the chorda tympani (taste to the anterior two-thirds of the tongue, submandibular and sublingual glands) inside the facial canal, before leaving the skull at the stylomastoid foramen. In the parotid gland it divides into its five motor branches (temporal, zygomatic, buccal, marginal mandibular and cervical), which pass between the superficial and deep lobes. Damage in the parotid paralyses the muscles of expression but spares taste and tears.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Taste and tears are carried by the trigeminal nerve: The trigeminal carries these fibres only after they join it from the facial nerve.
  • C. The parotid gland itself controls taste and tear secretion: The parotid gland has no part in taste.
  • D. The surgeon cut the sensory root of the facial nerve: The facial nerve branches in the parotid are motor.
  • E. Tears are controlled by the oculomotor nerve, not the facial nerve: The oculomotor nerve controls the pupil and lens, not the lacrimal gland.

Key point: In the parotid, the facial nerve carries only motor fibres; taste and tears branch off earlier.

Question 4 · Lower limb · easy

A 25-year-old man from a village near Multan has had back pain, evening fever and weight loss for three months. He now has a soft, painless swelling in his groin below the inguinal ligament and keeps his hip slightly flexed. Extending the hip is painful. X-ray shows collapse of the L1 vertebra with loss of the disc space.

Why does pus from his spine appear in the groin?

  1. A. It spreads along the femoral sheath from the abdomen
  2. B. It spreads through the inguinal canal
  3. C. It drains through the obturator canal
  4. D. It spreads through the greater sciatic foramen
  5. E. It tracks down within the psoas sheath
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. It tracks down within the psoas sheath

Psoas major arises from the bodies and transverse processes of T12 to L5 and passes under the inguinal ligament to insert, with iliacus, into the lesser trochanter. It is enclosed in a fascial sheath. Pus from tuberculosis of the spine (Pott disease), still common in Pakistan, can track down inside the sheath and appear as a cold abscess in the groin below the inguinal ligament, lateral to the femoral vessels. The patient holds the hip flexed, and extension stretches the inflamed muscle (psoas sign).

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. It spreads along the femoral sheath from the abdomen: The femoral sheath contains the femoral vessels, not psoas.
  • B. It spreads through the inguinal canal: The inguinal canal is above the inguinal ligament.
  • C. It drains through the obturator canal: The obturator canal opens on the medial thigh.
  • D. It spreads through the greater sciatic foramen: This would bring it to the buttock.

Key point: A psoas abscess tracks down the psoas sheath from the spine to the groin below the inguinal ligament.

Question 5 · Neuroanatomy · easy

A patient is in a coma after a head injury. When the doctor turns the head quickly to the left, both eyes move to the right, keeping their gaze fixed.

What does this tell the doctor?

  1. A. The brainstem pathways are working
  2. B. The patient is brain dead
  3. C. The occipital cortex is working normally
  4. D. The optic nerves are damaged on both sides
  5. E. The cerebellum has herniated
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. The brainstem pathways are working

The oculocephalic (doll's eye) reflex tests the vestibulo-ocular pathway. Turning the head stimulates the semicircular canals, and the signal passes through the vestibular nuclei in the medulla and pons, the MLF, and the abducens and oculomotor nuclei to move the eyes the opposite way. Intact eye movements show that a large part of the brainstem is working, so the coma is likely due to damage above it. If the eyes move with the head, the brainstem is badly damaged. The reflex must not be tested when a neck injury has not been ruled out.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. The patient is brain dead: In brain death the eyes move with the head.
  • C. The occipital cortex is working normally: This reflex does not need the visual cortex.
  • D. The optic nerves are damaged on both sides: The reflex does not test the optic nerves.
  • E. The cerebellum has herniated: This does not explain the finding.

Key point: An intact doll's eye reflex shows the brainstem vestibulo-ocular pathways are working.

Question 6 · Thorax and mediastinum · easy

A 50-year-old man in Lahore with cirrhosis from hepatitis C vomits a large amount of fresh blood and becomes faint. He has ascites, spider naevi and a large spleen, and his platelets are 60. Endoscopy shows bleeding varices in the lower oesophagus, just above the gastro-oesophageal junction, which are banded.

Which veins connect at this site?

  1. A. Superior and inferior rectal veins
  2. B. Paraumbilical and superficial epigastric veins
  3. C. Left gastric and azygos tributaries
  4. D. Splenic vein and left renal vein
  5. E. Right gastric vein and the inferior vena cava
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Left gastric and azygos tributaries

The lower oesophagus drains in two directions: down through the left gastric vein into the portal vein, and up through oesophageal veins into the azygos and hemiazygos veins and so to the superior vena cava. In portal hypertension, blood is diverted through these submucosal connections, which dilate into varices that can bleed massively. Other portosystemic sites are the anorectal junction, the umbilicus (paraumbilical veins), and the retroperitoneum.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Superior and inferior rectal veins: These form the anorectal anastomosis.
  • B. Paraumbilical and superficial epigastric veins: These form caput medusae at the umbilicus.
  • D. Splenic vein and left renal vein: This is a retroperitoneal route, not the cause of oesophageal varices.
  • E. Right gastric vein and the inferior vena cava: The right gastric vein does not connect directly with the IVC.

Key point: Oesophageal varices form where the left gastric vein (portal) meets oesophageal tributaries of the azygos (systemic).

Question 7 · Upper limb · medium

A 25-year-old man punches through a glass window and has a deep cut across the front of his wrist. Afterwards he cannot feel any of his fingertips on the palmar side, the small muscles of his hand are paralysed, and he cannot spread his fingers or oppose his thumb. His long finger flexors still work, and he can bend all his fingertips.

How will his hand look after some weeks?

  1. A. Clawing of the ring and little fingers, with a normal thumb
  2. B. Wrist drop with fingers that cannot extend
  3. C. Clawing of all four fingers, with a flat thenar eminence
  4. D. Index finger held straight while others flex
  5. E. A normal hand at rest, with numbness alone
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Clawing of all four fingers, with a flat thenar eminence

At the wrist, the median nerve supplies the thenar muscles and the lateral two lumbricals, and the ulnar nerve supplies the hypothenar muscles, interossei, adductor pollicis and the medial two lumbricals. Cutting both at the wrist paralyses all the intrinsic muscles, while the long flexors and extensors (supplied in the forearm) still work. Without lumbricals and interossei, the extensors hyperextend the knuckles and the long flexors bend the finger joints, so all four fingers claw ("intrinsic minus" hand). Thenar wasting and loss of opposition give a flat, "ape-like" thumb. Wounds here also often cut the flexor tendons and radial or ulnar arteries.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Clawing of the ring and little fingers, with a normal thumb: This follows an isolated ulnar injury.
  • B. Wrist drop with fingers that cannot extend: This follows a radial nerve injury.
  • D. Index finger held straight while others flex: This follows a high median nerve injury.
  • E. A normal hand at rest, with numbness alone: The paralysed small muscles change its shape.

Key point: Combined median and ulnar injury at the wrist causes clawing of all four fingers and loss of thenar function.

Question 8 · Abdomen and pelvis · medium

A 20-year-old man has had lower abdominal pain for a day with loose stools and frequent passing of urine, with a fever. His right iliac fossa is only mildly tender, but rectal examination is tender on the right. Flexing his right hip and rotating it inwards causes pain in the lower abdomen. His urine shows a few white cells but no bacteria.

Where is the inflamed appendix most likely lying?

  1. A. Behind the caecum, against psoas major on the posterior wall
  2. B. In front of the terminal ileum, beneath the anterior abdominal wall
  3. C. Below the liver, near the gallbladder and the duodenum
  4. D. Behind the ileum, deep in the right upper quadrant of the mesentery
  5. E. Over the pelvic brim, against rectum, bladder and obturator internus
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Over the pelvic brim, against rectum, bladder and obturator internus

The base of the appendix is constant (where the taeniae coli meet), but its tip can lie retrocaecal (commonest), pelvic, subcaecal, or pre- or post-ileal. A pelvic appendix lies against the rectum, bladder and obturator internus, so it may cause diarrhoea, urinary frequency, suprapubic pain and tenderness on rectal examination, with little anterior abdominal tenderness. Stretching obturator internus by flexing and medially rotating the hip causes pain (obturator sign). A retrocaecal appendix irritates psoas, so hip extension hurts (psoas sign). Atypical positions delay diagnosis and increase perforation.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Behind the caecum, against psoas major on the posterior wall: This causes flank pain and a psoas sign.
  • B. In front of the terminal ileum, beneath the anterior abdominal wall: This causes typical right iliac fossa tenderness.
  • C. Below the liver, near the gallbladder and the duodenum: This causes right upper abdominal pain.
  • D. Behind the ileum, deep in the right upper quadrant of the mesentery: This would not irritate the rectum or bladder.

Key point: A pelvic appendix causes diarrhoea, frequency, rectal tenderness and a positive obturator sign.

Question 9 · General anatomy, embryology and histology · medium

A 45-year-old woman has had painful erosions in her mouth for 2 months, so that eating is hard, and now fragile, flaccid blisters on her skin that rupture easily, leaving raw areas. Rubbing normal skin makes the top layer slide off. Biopsy shows a split within the epidermis, just above the basal layer, with rounded, separated keratinocytes.

Which cell junction is attacked by autoantibodies in this condition?

  1. A. Tight junctions (zonula occludens)
  2. B. Hemidesmosomes
  3. C. Gap junctions
  4. D. Adherens junctions (zonula adherens)
  5. E. Desmosomes (macula adherens)
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Desmosomes (macula adherens)

Desmosomes are spot-like junctions that join neighbouring epithelial cells, anchored to intermediate (keratin) filaments; their adhesion proteins are cadherins called desmogleins and desmocollins. In pemphigus vulgaris, IgG autoantibodies against desmoglein 3 (and often 1) separate keratinocytes from one another just above the basal layer (acantholysis), giving fragile blisters, mouth erosions and a positive Nikolsky sign. In bullous pemphigoid, antibodies against hemidesmosome proteins split the epidermis from the dermis, giving tense blisters, usually in older people.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Tight junctions (zonula occludens): These seal the spaces between epithelial cells.
  • B. Hemidesmosomes: These attach the basal cells to the basement membrane and are attacked in bullous pemphigoid.
  • C. Gap junctions: These allow small molecules and ions to pass between cells.
  • D. Adherens junctions (zonula adherens): These link actin filaments of neighbouring cells; they are not the main target.

Key point: Pemphigus vulgaris targets desmosomes (desmoglein); bullous pemphigoid targets hemidesmosomes.

Question 10 · Head and neck · easy

A surgeon must tie the artery that causes severe bleeding from the back of the nose. He reaches the fossa behind the maxilla where this artery divides.

Which artery is this, and through which opening does it enter the nose?

  1. A. The anterior ethmoidal artery, through the cribriform plate
  2. B. The facial artery, through the nostril
  3. C. The greater palatine artery, through the incisive canal
  4. D. The sphenopalatine artery, through the sphenopalatine foramen
  5. E. The ascending pharyngeal artery, through the choana
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. The sphenopalatine artery, through the sphenopalatine foramen

The pterygopalatine fossa is a small space behind the maxilla. It contains the third part of the maxillary artery, the maxillary nerve and the pterygopalatine ganglion. The maxillary artery ends as the sphenopalatine artery, which enters the nose through the sphenopalatine foramen to supply most of the back of the nasal cavity. Severe posterior nosebleeds that do not stop with packing are treated by tying or burning this artery endoscopically.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. The anterior ethmoidal artery, through the cribriform plate: This supplies the upper front of the nose.
  • B. The facial artery, through the nostril: The facial artery supplies the nose only from the front.
  • C. The greater palatine artery, through the incisive canal: This reaches the septum from below only.
  • E. The ascending pharyngeal artery, through the choana: This supplies the pharynx.

Key point: The sphenopalatine artery, from the maxillary artery in the pterygopalatine fossa, is the main source of posterior nosebleeds.
