Question 1 · General properties of dental materials · easy

A 58-year-old woman in Mardan returns to the prosthodontics clinic two weeks after receiving a removable partial denture. The wrought gold clasp arm on the lower right second premolar (45) is now permanently bent open and no longer engages the 0.5 mm undercut, even though the clasp has not fractured.

Which stress threshold was exceeded when the clasp was pulled over the tooth bulge?

  1. A. Fracture strength
  2. B. Modulus of resilience
  3. C. Flexural modulus
  4. D. Poisson ratio
  5. E. Proportional limit
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Proportional limit

The proportional limit is the greatest stress a material can sustain without deviating from Hooke law (linear proportionality between stress and strain). For practical clinical purposes it coincides with the elastic limit and closely approximates the yield strength. When a removable partial denture clasp is deflected into an undercut deeper than its design limit, the induced stress exceeds the proportional limit, causing permanent (plastic) deformation. The clasp stays open and loses retention.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Fracture strength: Represents the stress at which the clasp breaks into two pieces, whereas this patient has an intact but permanently bent clasp.
  • B. Modulus of resilience: Represents energy per unit volume rather than a stress threshold, although it ends at the proportional limit.
  • C. Flexural modulus: Describes the slope of the stress-strain curve in bending, not the stress threshold where permanent set begins.
  • D. Poisson ratio: Measures the ratio of lateral contraction to axial extension within the elastic range, unrelated to permanent bending.

Key point: Relate permanent distortion of a denture clasp without fracture to exceeding the proportional limit.

Question 2 · Impression, model, wax and casting materials · easy

A 59-year-old man attends a dental hospital in Peshawar for a partial denture to replace his missing lower right first molar (46) and second molar (47). Intraoral examination shows that the remaining lower right second premolar (45) and lower left canine (33) are tilted lingually, creating 1.2 mm bony and dental undercuts. If a rigid impression material is used, it will either lock onto the teeth or fracture upon removal.

Which category of impression materials is indicated to record these teeth and soft-tissue undercuts accurately?

  1. A. Thermoplastic inelastic materials such as impression compound
  2. B. Chemosetting inelastic materials such as zinc oxide eugenol paste
  3. C. Aqueous or non-aqueous elastic impression materials
  4. D. Rigid gypsum impression plaster (Type I)
  5. E. Thermoplastic corrective impression waxes
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Aqueous or non-aqueous elastic impression materials

Dental impression materials are classified by their mechanical behaviour after setting into inelastic (rigid) and elastic materials, and by their setting mechanism into reversible (physical/thermal) and irreversible (chemical) systems. Inelastic materials—impression plaster, impression compound, zinc oxide eugenol (ZOE) paste and impression waxes—exhibit negligible elastic strain before fracturing or permanently deforming, restricting their use to edentulous mouths without severe bony undercuts. Elastic impression materials—comprising aqueous hydrocolloids (agar and alginate) and non-aqueous elastomers (polysulphide, condensation silicone, addition silicone and polyether)—can be stretched or compressed around coronal and alveolar undercuts and spring back elastically upon removal.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Thermoplastic inelastic materials such as impression compound: Impression compound sets into a rigid, brittle mass at 37 °C that distorts or fractures when pulled over tooth undercuts.
  • B. Chemosetting inelastic materials such as zinc oxide eugenol paste: Zinc oxide eugenol paste is rigid when set and is restricted to edentulous arches with no significant bony undercuts.
  • D. Rigid gypsum impression plaster (Type I): Impression plaster is intentionally brittle so it fractures into pieces when undercuts exist, making it unsuitable for dentate arches.
  • E. Thermoplastic corrective impression waxes: Impression waxes flow permanently at mouth temperature and cannot recover elastically after passing over an undercut.

Key point: Classify dental impression materials into elastic and inelastic groups and select elastic materials when tooth or tissue undercuts are present.

Question 3 · Metals, polymers, ceramics and implant materials · easy

A dental metallurgy professor in Peshawar writes on the lecture whiteboard the current ISO 22674 / ADA classification for fixed prosthodontic casting alloys. He notes that alloys are categorized by their noble metal content (gold, platinum group metals including palladium, rhodium, ruthenium, iridium and osmium) and their intended clinical uses (Type 1 for inlays up to Type 5 for long-span bridges and removable partial denture frameworks).

What minimum noble metal content defines a "high noble" casting alloy?

  1. A. At least 90% noble metal
  2. B. At least 25%, with no gold minimum
  3. C. At least 75% gold
  4. D. At least 40% palladium
  5. E. 60% noble, including 40% gold
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. 60% noble, including 40% gold

Dental casting alloys are classified by the American Dental Association (ADA) and ISO 22674 into three compositional categories based on noble metal content, and five clinical application types (Types 1 to 5): 1) **High Noble Alloys**: Must contain at least **60 wt% noble metal**, of which at least **40 wt% must be gold (Au)**. Examples include traditional Type III and IV gold-silver-copper-platinum/palladium alloys. 2) **Noble Alloys**: Must contain at least **25 wt% noble metal**, with **no minimum gold requirement**. These are typically silver-palladium or palladium-silver alloys. 3) **Base Metal Alloys**: Contain less than 25 wt% noble metal. They rely on passivating chromium oxide films for corrosion resistance and include nickel-chromium (Ni-Cr) and cobalt-chromium (Co-Cr) alloys. ISO 22674 further classifies alloys by clinical utility: Type 1 (low stress, inlays), Type 2 (moderate stress, single crowns), Type 3 (high stress, multi-unit fixed partial dentures), Type 4 (high stress and thin sections, long-span bridges and removable partial denture frameworks), and Type 5 (implant superstructures and thin partial denture frames).

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. At least 90% noble metal: The threshold is 60%, not 90%.
  • B. At least 25%, with no gold minimum: This is the definition of a noble alloy.
  • C. At least 75% gold: Older gold-alloy grading used carat, but high noble is defined as 60% noble with 40% gold.
  • D. At least 40% palladium: The definition depends on total noble content and gold, not palladium alone.

Key point: State the ADA/ISO composition criteria for high noble (≥60% noble, ≥40% Au) versus noble (≥25% noble) casting alloys.

Question 4 · Direct restorative materials, cements and adhesives · easy

During an operative dentistry seminar in Peshawar, a 2nd-year BDS student examines the metallurgical composition of a conventional dental amalgam alloy powder containing 68 wt% silver, 26 wt% tin, 5 wt% copper and 1 wt% zinc. Each metal is added in a specific proportion to control setting expansion, reaction rate, strength, and oxygen scavenging during alloy manufacture.

Which alloying element primarily increases setting expansion and 24-hour compressive strength, whereas which element reduces setting expansion and slows the setting reaction?

  1. A. Zinc increases setting expansion under dry conditions; copper slows the reaction and reduces strength
  2. B. Tin increases setting expansion and strength; zinc reduces setting expansion and slows the reaction
  3. C. Silver increases setting expansion and strength; tin reduces setting expansion and slows the reaction
  4. D. Copper causes severe setting contraction; silver acts only as an oxygen scavenger during alloy melting
  5. E. Mercury increases the elastic modulus; tin eliminates the gamma-2 phase during trituration
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Silver increases setting expansion and strength; tin reduces setting expansion and slows the reaction

In dental amalgam alloy powders, each constituent metal plays a defined metallurgical role: 1) Silver (Ag, 40–70 wt%) is the major element; it whitens the alloy, increases setting expansion, increases compressive strength, decreases creep, and increases resistance to tarnish. 2) Tin (Sn, 25–29 wt%) has a high solubility and affinity for liquid mercury, making amalgamation easier at room temperature, slowing the setting reaction to provide working time, and reducing setting expansion (or causing contraction), though in low-copper alloys it forms the weak, corrosion-prone gamma-2 (Sn7-8Hg) phase. 3) Copper (Cu, <6 wt% in low-copper and 12–30 wt% in high-copper alloys) increases strength and hardness and eliminates the gamma-2 phase. 4) Zinc (Zn, 0–2 wt%) acts as a deoxidizer (oxygen scavenger) during the melting and atomization of the alloy ingot by preferentially oxidizing to ZnO so silver, tin and copper are not oxidized.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Zinc increases setting expansion under dry conditions; copper slows the reaction and reduces strength: Zinc only causes abnormal delayed expansion if contaminated with moisture; copper increases strength and hardness by eliminating the gamma-2 phase.
  • B. Tin increases setting expansion and strength; zinc reduces setting expansion and slows the reaction: Tin actually causes contraction and reduces strength/hardness by forming the weak gamma-2 phase, while zinc is a deoxidizer (scavenger) during melting.
  • D. Copper causes severe setting contraction; silver acts only as an oxygen scavenger during alloy melting: Zinc (not silver) acts as the oxygen scavenger during alloy fusion, and silver increases rather than decreases setting expansion.
  • E. Mercury increases the elastic modulus; tin eliminates the gamma-2 phase during trituration: Excess mercury weakens the amalgam and lowers elastic modulus, and copper (not tin) eliminates the gamma-2 phase.

Key point: Identify the specific metallurgical functions of silver, tin, copper and zinc in dental amalgam alloy powders.

Question 5 · General properties of dental materials · easy

A postgraduate student in Quetta is measuring the surface hardness of consecutive 50 µm layers of carious dentine and brittle cervical enamel on an extracted lower right first molar (46). To avoid cracking the brittle enamel and to place closely spaced indentations across thin zones, he chooses an asymmetric rhombic-based pyramidal diamond indenter.

Which hardness test is the student performing?

  1. A. Brinell test
  2. B. Rockwell test
  3. C. Vickers test
  4. D. Knoop test
  5. E. Shore A durometer test
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. Knoop test

The Knoop microhardness test uses a diamond indenter ground to an elongated rhombic-based pyramid that produces a diamond-shaped impression with a long-to-short diagonal ratio of about 7.11:1. Only the longest diagonal is measured optically, and elastic recovery occurs mainly across the shorter diagonal. Because the indentation is very shallow and narrow in one dimension, the Knoop test can measure brittle materials like enamel (around 340 KHN) and ceramics without radial cracking, and allows closely spaced profiling across thin cement-dentine interfaces.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Brinell test: Uses a 1.6 mm steel ball that leaves a large circular indentation and shatters brittle tooth enamel or dental ceramics.
  • B. Rockwell test: Measures the direct depth of penetration of a ball or cone under major load and is used for bulk polymers or alloys, not thin enamel zones.
  • C. Vickers test: Uses a symmetric square-based diamond pyramid where both diagonals are equal, requiring more vertical spacing between adjacent indentations.
  • E. Shore A durometer test: Measures elastic indentation resistance of soft rubbers, maxillofacial silicones and elastomeric impressions, not mineralised tooth tissues.

Key point: Select the Knoop microhardness test for evaluating thin layers and brittle dental substrates such as enamel.

Question 6 · Impression, model, wax and casting materials · easy

In a prosthodontics clinic in Mardan, a 3rd-year BDS student is border moulding an acrylic special tray for an edentulous 64-year-old man using low-fusing Type I green stick compound. He softens the stick over a flame, tempers it in warm water, moulds the labial sulcus, and chills it before removal. Because of a property inherent to the resin-wax-filler mixture, he must hold the tray in the mouth for sufficient time for the entire thickness to cool.

Why does impression compound cool slowly from its outer surface to its core and require thorough chilling before removal?

  1. A. It sets by a slow exothermic acid-base chelation reaction
  2. B. It has very low thermal conductivity and thermal diffusivity
  3. C. It absorbs large amounts of salivary water to reach its glass transition temperature
  4. D. Its talc filler particles undergo an allotropic crystal inversion at 37 °C
  5. E. It releases volatile ethyl alcohol that must evaporate before hardening
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. It has very low thermal conductivity and thermal diffusivity

Impression compound is a thermoplastic, reversible, inelastic impression material composed of natural or synthetic resins (~40%, such as shellac or rosin), waxes (~7%, such as beeswax), a plasticizer (stearic acid ~3% to improve flow and plasticity), and an inert inorganic filler (~50%, such as talc or French chalk to reduce thermal contraction and control viscosity). It is supplied as Type I (lower-fusing impression/border-moulding compound, softening at ~45 °C) and Type II (higher-fusing tray compound). Because its organic resin-wax matrix has very low thermal conductivity and diffusivity, heat flows out of the core very slowly; if the tray is removed before the inner core cools below mouth temperature, the soft interior flows and distorts the impression.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. It sets by a slow exothermic acid-base chelation reaction: Impression compound is a thermoplastic physical mixture of resins, waxes, stearic acid and talc filler that sets reversibly by cooling, not by chemical chelation.
  • C. It absorbs large amounts of salivary water to reach its glass transition temperature: Impression compound is hydrophobic and does not rely on water absorption to harden.
  • D. Its talc filler particles undergo an allotropic crystal inversion at 37 °C: Talc (magnesium silicate) is an inert inorganic filler that reduces thermal contraction and improves plasticity without undergoing crystal inversion at 37 °C.
  • E. It releases volatile ethyl alcohol that must evaporate before hardening: Ethyl alcohol release occurs in condensation silicones, not thermoplastic impression compound.

Key point: Explain how the low thermal conductivity of impression compound causes differential cooling between its surface and core, requiring complete chilling before removal.

Question 7 · Metals, polymers, ceramics and implant materials · easy

A prosthodontist in Lahore is designing a light, rigid removable partial denture framework for a 52-year-old edentulous patient. He selects a cobalt-chromium (Co-Cr) base metal alloy because of its high elastic modulus (~210–220 GPa), high yield strength, low density, and excellent corrosion resistance in saliva.

What gives cobalt-chromium and nickel-chromium alloys their corrosion resistance?

  1. A. A molybdenum carbide layer
  2. B. Their high cobalt content
  3. C. Chromium oxide film
  4. D. Their beryllium content
  5. E. Their carbon content
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Chromium oxide film

Base metal casting alloys—specifically **cobalt-chromium (Co-Cr)** and **nickel-chromium (Ni-Cr)** alloys—are the workhorses of removable partial denture frameworks and porcelain-fused-to-metal (PFM) crown/bridge substructures. 1) **Corrosion Resistance via Passivation**: Both alloy classes contain **16 to 27 wt% chromium (Cr)**. When exposed to oxygen, chromium spontaneously forms an adherent, continuous, sub-nanometre passive surface film of **chromium oxide (Cr2O3)** that isolates the underlying metal from saliva and self-heals instantaneously if scratched. 2) **Physical Properties**: They possess roughly twice the elastic modulus (~180–220 GPa) and significantly higher yield strength than Type IV gold alloys, allowing thinner, lighter, more rigid frameworks. 3) **Beryllium Hazard**: Historically, many Ni-Cr base metal alloys (especially those designed for porcelain bonding) incorporated **0.8 to 2.0 wt% beryllium (Be)** to lower the melting range by ~100 °C, improve castability, and thin out oxide layers. However, grinding or sandblasting beryllium-containing Ni-Cr castings generates hazardous inhalable micro-dust and toxic fumes that cause **chronic berylliosis** (a debilitating granulomatous pulmonary disease resembling sarcoidosis), leading dental manufacturers to shift exclusively to **beryllium-free Ni-Cr and Co-Cr formulations**.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. A molybdenum carbide layer: Molybdenum refines grain and strengthens; it is not the passivating agent.
  • B. Their high cobalt content: Cobalt adds strength and stiffness; chromium gives the corrosion resistance.
  • D. Their beryllium content: Beryllium improved castability of some Ni-Cr alloys but is toxic and does not give corrosion resistance.
  • E. Their carbon content: Carbon forms carbides that strengthen the alloy; excess carbon reduces corrosion resistance.

Key point: State the passivating role of chromium (Cr2O3 film) in Co-Cr and Ni-Cr alloys and explain the occupational carcinogenicity and pulmonary hazard of beryllium (berylliosis).

Question 8 · Direct restorative materials, cements and adhesives · easy

A 56-year-old man in Bannu presents with a 12-year-old conventional low-copper (<5 wt% Cu) amalgam restoration on his lower left first molar (36). The restoration shows severe marginal ditching, blackened corrosion products penetrating the dentinal tubules, and low bulk hardness. Metallographic analysis confirms that an interconnected network of a specific tin-mercury phase has corroded electrochemically in saliva to release free mercury back into the alloy.

Which phase in set low-copper dental amalgam is the softest, weakest in tension, highest in creep, and most electronegative (most prone to corrosion)?

  1. A. Gamma (γ, Ag3Sn)
  2. B. Gamma-1 (γ1, Ag2Hg3)
  3. C. Gamma-2 (γ2, Sn7-8Hg)
  4. D. Eta-prime (η′, Cu6Sn5)
  5. E. Epsilon (ε, Cu3Sn)
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Gamma-2 (γ2, Sn7-8Hg)

In conventional low-copper dental amalgams (<6 wt% copper), the setting reaction produces both a silver-mercury matrix (γ1, Ag2Hg3) and a tin-mercury phase (γ2, Sn7-8Hg) that constitutes ~10–13 vol% of the set restoration: Ag3Sn (γ) + Hg → Ag2Hg3 (γ1) + Sn7-8Hg (γ2) + unreacted Ag3Sn (γ). The gamma-2 (Sn7-8Hg) phase is the metallurgical "Achilles heel" of low-copper amalgam: 1) it is by far the softest and mechanically weakest phase (tensile strength only ~20 MPa compared to ~170 MPa for γ and ~30 MPa for γ1, with Knoop hardness ~15 KHN vs ~120 KHN for γ1); 2) it forms a continuous, interconnected network along grain boundaries that allows grain-boundary sliding and high viscoelastic creep; and 3) it is the most electronegative (anodic) phase in saliva, undergoing rapid electrochemical corrosion (Sn7-8Hg + O2 + H2O + Cl⁻ → SnO / Sn4(OH)6Cl2 + free Hg).

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Gamma (γ, Ag3Sn): Gamma is the unreacted original alloy core—the strongest, hardest and most creep-resistant phase in set amalgam.
  • B. Gamma-1 (γ1, Ag2Hg3): Gamma-1 is the continuous silver-mercury matrix, which is intermediate in hardness and far more corrosion-resistant than the tin-mercury phase.
  • D. Eta-prime (η′, Cu6Sn5): Eta-prime is the copper-tin reaction product formed in high-copper amalgams that replaces gamma-2, reduces creep, and resists corrosion much better than gamma-2.
  • E. Epsilon (ε, Cu3Sn): Epsilon is a hard, strong copper-tin intermetallic compound present in unconsumed high-copper alloy particles.

Key point: Identify the gamma-2 (Sn7-8Hg) phase as the weakest, softest, highest-creep and most corrosion-susceptible phase in low-copper dental amalgam.

Question 9 · General properties of dental materials · easy

In a paediatric dentistry clinic in Charsadda, a house officer dispenses a topical acidulated phosphate fluoride gel into a foam tray for a 9-year-old child. The gel sits firmly in the inverted tray without dripping onto the bench, but as soon as the tray is pressed over the teeth and agitated slightly, the gel thins out and flows into the interproximal embrasures.

Which rheological property allows the gel to become less viscous when agitated and recover its thickness at rest?

  1. A. Dilatancy
  2. B. Newtonian flow
  3. C. Thixotropy
  4. D. Rheopexy
  5. E. Poisson effect
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Thixotropy

Thixotropy is a time-dependent shear-thinning rheological property: when a gel or paste is stirred, vibrated or sheared, weak secondary bonds (such as hydrogen bonds or van der Waals forces) between particles break down, lowering its viscosity so it flows easily. When the shearing force ceases, the structural network gradually reforms (displaying a hysteresis loop on a shear stress versus shear rate graph). Topical fluoride gels, prophy pastes, resin cements and dental plaster mixes are formulated to be thixotropic so they do not drip off trays or instruments, yet flow readily into pits, fissures and margins under placement pressure or vibration.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Dilatancy: Describes shear-thickening fluids (such as wet sand or heavily filled slurries) whose viscosity increases when sheared rapidly.
  • B. Newtonian flow: Describes fluids like water or pure glycerine whose viscosity stays constant regardless of shear rate or agitation time.
  • D. Rheopexy: Is the rare opposite of thixotropy, where gentle rhythmic shearing causes a liquid to thicken and gel more rapidly over time.
  • E. Poisson effect: Describes lateral elastic contraction of a solid under axial tension, unrelated to liquid or gel viscosity.

Key point: Define thixotropy and identify its clinical advantage in topical fluoride gels and dental pastes.

Question 10 · Impression, model, wax and casting materials · easy

During an undergraduate dental materials practical in Kohat, a demonstrator explains that when soluble sodium alginate powder and calcium sulphate dihydrate reactor are mixed with water alone, insoluble calcium alginate gel precipitates almost instantaneously while the operator is still spatulating in the rubber bowl, leaving zero working time to load the tray.

Which ingredient is added by manufacturers to react preferentially with calcium ions and provide a controlled working time?

  1. A. Diatomaceous earth (siliceous frustules)
  2. B. Potassium titanium fluoride
  3. C. Trisodium phosphate
  4. D. Magnesium oxide
  5. E. Glycol polymer coating
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Trisodium phosphate

Alginate (irreversible hydrocolloid) sets by the cross-linking of soluble linear sodium or potassium alginate chains by divalent calcium (Ca²⁺) ions released from slightly soluble calcium sulphate dihydrate (CaSO4·2H2O, ~12–16%). Because this ionic replacement is extremely rapid, a retarder—typically trisodium phosphate (Na3PO4) or tetrasodium pyrophosphate (~2 wt%)—is essential. Calcium ions have a higher chemical affinity for phosphate ions than for the carboxylate groups of sodium alginate; therefore, throughout the mixing and tray-loading period, all released Ca²⁺ ions are precipitated as insoluble calcium phosphate: 2Na3PO4 + 3CaSO4 → Ca3(PO4)2↓ + 3Na2SO4. Only after the trisodium phosphate is completely exhausted do Ca²⁺ ions begin cross-linking the alginate chains into an elastic calcium alginate gel.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Diatomaceous earth (siliceous frustules): Acts as an inert filler (~60–70%) that increases the strength, stiffness and smooth texture of the set alginate gel rather than retarding the reaction.
  • B. Potassium titanium fluoride: Acts as a gypsum hardener (~2–3%) to ensure the surface of the stone cast sets hard against the hydrocolloid, not as a setting retarder.
  • D. Magnesium oxide: Acts as a pH modifier and scavenger alongside zinc oxide rather than the primary calcium-sequestering retarder.
  • E. Glycol polymer coating: Is added to render the fine silica powder dustless so technicians do not inhale respirable cristobalite/silica dust when fluffing the tin.

Key point: Explain how trisodium phosphate provides working time in alginate impression materials by preferentially reacting with calcium ions.
