Question 1 · Communication skills (PGMI) · medium

During a consultation, you inform a 30-year-old man that he requires lifelong dialysis. He looks away, clenches his fists, and says, "This is entirely unfair. My life is completely ruined now." Using the NURSE framework for empathy,

What is the most appropriate response?

  1. A. "Many people live long, productive lives while on dialysis."
  2. B. "Let me explain the exact schedule you will need to follow for your treatment."
  3. C. "Why do you feel your life is completely ruined by this?"
  4. D. "I can see this feels incredibly unfair and devastating to you right now."
  5. E. "You should focus on the fact that we have a treatment available."
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. "I can see this feels incredibly unfair and devastating to you right now."

When responding to intense patient emotions, the NURSE framework (Name, Understand, Respect, Support, Explore) is highly effective. The patient is expressing anger and despair. The most appropriate immediate response is to explicitly Name the emotion or Understand/validate it (e.g., "I can see this feels incredibly unfair..."). This validates their experience without trying to prematurely fix it. Offering statistics, toxic positivity ("focus on the treatment"), or shifting immediately to clinical logistics blocks the patient's emotional expression and damages the therapeutic alliance.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. "Many people live long, productive lives while on dialysis.": Deflects the patient's acute distress with premature reassurance instead of validating his immediate feelings.
  • B. "Let me explain the exact schedule you will need to follow for your treatment.": Ignores the emotional outburst entirely and attempts to solve the problem cognitively, blocking empathy.
  • C. "Why do you feel your life is completely ruined by this?": Asking "why" can come across as challenging or analytical, rather than supportive and empathetic.
  • E. "You should focus on the fact that we have a treatment available.": Highly dismissive and invalidating; it tells the patient how they ought to feel rather than accepting their distress.

Key point: Utilise the NURSE framework by explicitly naming and validating a patient's emotions before offering clinical reassurance or plans.

Question 2 · Communication skills (PGMI) · medium

You are discharging a 5-year-old boy from the paediatric ward after observing him for a mild head injury. He is currently asymptomatic and acting normally. You have explained the signs of concussion to his mother.

What is the most critical component to include in your final discharge communication?

  1. A. A prescription for prophylactic anti-emetics in case he feels nauseated at home
  2. B. Strict instructions to keep the child awake for the next 24 hours
  3. C. A scheduled outpatient appointment for an MRI scan next month
  4. D. A written letter to his school excusing him from exams for the rest of the year
  5. E. Clear safety-netting advice on when to return to the emergency department
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Clear safety-netting advice on when to return to the emergency department

Discharging a patient safely, particularly after conditions that can unpredictably deteriorate (like a head injury or early infection), requires robust "safety netting." Safety netting involves communicating clear, specific, and actionable instructions to the patient or parent about what to look out for (red flag symptoms, such as repeated vomiting or altered consciousness) and exactly what to do if they occur (e.g., "return to the emergency department immediately"). It empowers the patient and provides a critical safety buffer for diagnostic uncertainty.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. A prescription for prophylactic anti-emetics in case he feels nauseated at home: Masks potential red-flag symptoms (vomiting) of rising intracranial pressure, which is clinically dangerous.
  • B. Strict instructions to keep the child awake for the next 24 hours: Outdated and incorrect advice; normal sleep should be allowed, with periodic checking.
  • C. A scheduled outpatient appointment for an MRI scan next month: Unnecessary investigation for a mild, asymptomatic head injury, wasting resources.
  • D. A written letter to his school excusing him from exams for the rest of the year: An extreme and unnecessary social intervention for a mild, resolved head injury.

Key point: Incorporate clear safety netting instructions, detailing specific red flags and actions to take, when discharging patients.

Question 3 · Communication skills (PGMI) · medium

A 25-year-old woman is admitted to the medical ward after intentionally taking an overdose of paracetamol following a relationship breakdown. She is medically stable but refuses the antidote (N-acetylcysteine), stating, "I want to die, please let me go."

What is the most appropriate action?

  1. A. Assess her capacity; if impaired by acute depression, administer the antidote
  2. B. Respect her refusal, as she is a conscious adult making a clear choice
  3. C. Discharge her immediately as you cannot force treatment on a conscious patient
  4. D. Ask her parents to sign the consent form for the antidote instead
  5. E. Wait for a court order before administering any medication against her will
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Assess her capacity; if impaired by acute depression, administer the antidote

An acute suicide attempt is a medical emergency often driven by severe, temporary psychiatric illness (like acute depression or a situational crisis). This acute state impairs a patient's capacity because they cannot rationally weigh the value of their own life against the consequences of their action. If a patient who has taken a deliberate overdose refuses life-saving treatment, the doctor must assess capacity. In almost all such cases, capacity is deemed impaired, and the doctor has a strict duty to provide life-saving treatment (like NAC) in the patient's best interests.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Respect her refusal, as she is a conscious adult making a clear choice: Her choice is driven by acute psychiatric illness (suicidality), which impairs her capacity to weigh the value of her own life.
  • C. Discharge her immediately as you cannot force treatment on a conscious patient: Discharging a suicidal patient who has taken a lethal overdose is gross medical negligence.
  • D. Ask her parents to sign the consent form for the antidote instead: Adults do not require parental consent; the legal justification for treatment here is her lack of capacity, not proxy consent.
  • E. Wait for a court order before administering any medication against her will: Delays critical, time-sensitive life-saving medical treatment (NAC must be given quickly to prevent liver failure).

Key point: Administer life-saving treatment in the best interests of a patient who has taken a deliberate overdose and lacks capacity due to acute suicidality.

Question 4 · Communication skills (PGMI) · medium

A 65-year-old man in the General Medicine ward at a Peshawar teaching hospital has just been diagnosed with advanced hepatocellular carcinoma. His son is waiting outside. You have arranged a quiet private room, and he says he would like to talk to you alone first.

According to the SPIKES protocol, what is the most appropriate next step?

  1. A. Ask the patient what he currently understands about his illness
  2. B. Call the son inside and inform them both simultaneously
  3. C. Explain the ultrasound and biopsy results using simple Urdu
  4. D. Advise the patient that palliative care is the remaining option
  5. E. Tell the son first and let him decide what his father is told
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Ask the patient what he currently understands about his illness

The SPIKES protocol is a structured framework for breaking bad news. After setting up the interview (S), the next step is assessing the patient’s Perception (P) of their condition. This involves asking open-ended questions like, "What have you been told so far about your illness?" to gauge their understanding and expectations. This must precede obtaining an Invitation (I) to share information, giving Knowledge (K), addressing Emotions (E), and formulating a Strategy (S). Assuming what the patient knows or forcing information before assessing their readiness can cause unnecessary distress and breakdown in communication.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Call the son inside and inform them both simultaneously: Bypasses the patient’s autonomy regarding who they explicitly want present during the consultation.
  • C. Explain the ultrasound and biopsy results using simple Urdu: Premature delivery of clinical information before assessing the patient’s readiness to hear it.
  • D. Advise the patient that palliative care is the remaining option: Too early in the protocol; delivering bad news requires establishing readiness and knowledge first.
  • E. Tell the son first and let him decide what his father is told: The patient has capacity and the right to his own information; letting the son decide removes his autonomy.

Key point: Assess the patient’s current perception of their illness before delivering new information when breaking bad news.

Question 5 · Communication skills (PGMI) · medium

A 28-year-old man is brought to the General Surgery emergency after an FAI (firearm injury). The ward is severely crowded. His brother aggressively confronts the doctor on duty, shouting that the patient is being ignored while waiting for a CT scan.

What is the most appropriate immediate response?

  1. A. Warn the brother that hospital security will be called immediately
  2. B. Loudly assert that all patients are triaged based on urgency
  3. C. Calmly acknowledge his frustration and explain the delay
  4. D. Walk away and wait for the brother to calm down entirely
  5. E. Immediately order the CT scan to appease the angry relative
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Calmly acknowledge his frustration and explain the delay

Dealing with angry relatives in a crowded emergency department requires rapid de-escalation skills. The most effective initial approach is to remain calm, acknowledge their distress (empathy), and actively listen to their concerns. Relatives in these situations are often driven by fear and a sense of helplessness. Explaining the triage process or the reason for a delay validates their concern while setting realistic expectations. Responding with aggression, threats of security (unless there is imminent physical danger), or capitulating to demands compromises both safety and clinical protocols.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Warn the brother that hospital security will be called immediately: Escalates the situation unnecessarily and lacks an immediate de-escalation effort for the distressed relative.
  • B. Loudly assert that all patients are triaged based on urgency: Adopting a defensive posture and raising your voice will likely provoke further aggression.
  • D. Walk away and wait for the brother to calm down entirely: Walking away is unprofessional and ignores the relative’s immediate concerns about the patient’s care.
  • E. Immediately order the CT scan to appease the angry relative: Compromises clinical triage protocols and prioritises based on intimidation rather than medical urgency.

Key point: Use active listening and empathetic acknowledgement to de-escalate confrontations with aggressive relatives in clinical settings.

Question 6 · Communication skills (PGMI) · medium

A 40-year-old man from a remote area presents to the outpatient clinic. He speaks only Pashto, while the attending doctor only speaks Urdu and English. The patient is accompanied by his 12-year-old son, who speaks both Pashto and Urdu.

What is the most appropriate approach to communication?

  1. A. Use the 12-year-old son to translate the medical history
  2. B. Attempt to communicate using basic hand gestures and simple Urdu
  3. C. Reschedule the appointment until an adult family member can attend
  4. D. Request a Pashto-speaking hospital staff member to interpret
  5. E. Use a medical translation app on a smartphone for the consultation
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. Request a Pashto-speaking hospital staff member to interpret

When facing a language barrier, utilising a formal interpreter or a bilingual staff member is the standard of care to ensure accurate communication and valid informed consent. Using family members, especially minors, as interpreters is strongly discouraged. It places an unfair emotional burden on the child, risks inaccurate translation of complex medical terms, and compromises patient confidentiality (the patient may withhold sensitive information from their child). While adult family members are sometimes used in resource-limited settings out of necessity, a trained staff member remains the most appropriate choice.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Use the 12-year-old son to translate the medical history: It is highly inappropriate to use a minor for medical translation due to the emotional burden.
  • B. Attempt to communicate using basic hand gestures and simple Urdu: Relies on unsafe clinical practice with a high risk of critical diagnostic miscommunication.
  • C. Reschedule the appointment until an adult family member can attend: Unnecessarily delays the patient’s access to medical care when hospital resources are available.
  • E. Use a medical translation app on a smartphone for the consultation: Automated translation applications are currently too unreliable for capturing complex medical nuances safely.

Key point: Utilise professional interpreters or bilingual staff rather than family members, particularly minors, to bridge language barriers.

Question 7 · Communication skills (PGMI) · medium

You are a house officer in General Medicine handing over a deteriorating patient to the night registrar over the telephone. Using the SBAR tool, you state: "I am calling about Mr. Khan in bed 4 who is highly breathless."

What is the most appropriate next piece of information to provide?

  1. A. His current respiratory rate, oxygen saturation, and pulse
  2. B. A request for the registrar to come and review the patient immediately
  3. C. A detailed history of his presenting complaint from three days ago
  4. D. His diagnosis of COPD and admission three days ago
  5. E. His complete medication list since admission
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. His diagnosis of COPD and admission three days ago

The SBAR (Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation) tool is the standard framework for clinical handover and escalating deteriorating patients. The stem provides the Situation ("calling about Mr. Khan... highly breathless"). The immediate next step is the Background, which should concisely provide the clinical context, such as the primary diagnosis and reason for admission. This is followed by the Assessment (current vital signs and clinical findings) and concludes with the Recommendation (what you need the senior to do).

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. His current respiratory rate, oxygen saturation, and pulse: This represents the Assessment (A) step, which should logically follow the Background (B).
  • B. A request for the registrar to come and review the patient immediately: This is the Recommendation (R) step, which should conclude the handover, not follow the initial statement.
  • C. A detailed history of his presenting complaint from three days ago: SBAR requires concise background information; a full, detailed history is inappropriate for an urgent handover.
  • E. His complete medication list since admission: Excessive detail for the immediate background step during an urgent call regarding acute deterioration.

Key point: Provide concise clinical context (Background) immediately after stating the acute problem (Situation) when using the SBAR handover tool.

Question 8 · Communication skills (PGMI) · medium

While covering a district headquarters hospital emergency at 2 AM, you assess a 45-year-old woman with a rigid abdomen and signs of peritonitis. You need to call the on-call surgical consultant for advice.

What is the most appropriate initial action before making the call?

  1. A. Administer intravenous morphine to relieve her severe pain
  2. B. Order and wait for the results of an erect chest X-ray
  3. C. Gather her vital signs and latest basic laboratory results
  4. D. Ensure the patient’s family has consented to emergency surgery
  5. E. Prepare the operating theatre for an immediate laparotomy
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Gather her vital signs and latest basic laboratory results

When seeking telephone advice or escalating a critically ill patient to a senior colleague, the referring doctor must be fully prepared. This means having the patient’s vital signs, early warning scores, relevant examination findings, and basic immediate investigations readily available before making the call. Calling a senior without basic objective data wastes time and prevents the senior from making safe clinical decisions remotely. Using a structured communication tool like SBAR is heavily dependent on having the Assessment data ready.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Administer intravenous morphine to relieve her severe pain: Analgesia is important but shouldn’t delay the urgent surgical consultation or mask signs before discussion.
  • B. Order and wait for the results of an erect chest X-ray: Waiting for imaging delays critical surgical consultation for a patient with obvious clinical peritonitis.
  • D. Ensure the patient’s family has consented to emergency surgery: Consent for surgery cannot be obtained until the surgical consultant has confirmed the need and plan.
  • E. Prepare the operating theatre for an immediate laparotomy: Premature allocation of resources before the surgical consultant has actually accepted and reviewed the case.

Key point: Gather all current vital signs and essential clinical data before calling a senior colleague for urgent telephone advice.

Question 9 · Communication skills (PGMI) · medium

A 30-year-old man diagnosed with pulmonary tuberculosis tells you he has stopped his anti-TB medications (ATT) because a local hakeem gave him a herbal syrup that "cures the root cause without heating the liver."

What is the most appropriate initial response?

  1. A. Explain that stopping ATT causes drug-resistant TB, and restart it today
  2. B. Agree he may keep taking the syrup if he restarts ATT
  3. C. Arrange directly observed treatment at the local DOTS centre
  4. D. Warn him that the hakeem's syrup may itself damage his liver
  5. E. Explore his understanding of TB and why he prefers the syrup
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Explore his understanding of TB and why he prefers the syrup

Patients in Pakistan frequently utilise complementary and alternative medicine (CAM), including Unani (hakeems) and homeopathic treatments, often alongside or instead of allopathic medicine. When a patient stops essential treatment for CAM, a confrontational approach typically destroys the therapeutic relationship. The most appropriate initial step is to explore the patient’s health beliefs, understand their fears (e.g., side effects like "heating the liver," a common cultural concept regarding hepatotoxicity), and gently provide education about the necessity of ATT, negotiating a safe path forward.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Explain that stopping ATT causes drug-resistant TB, and restart it today: Accurate, but lecturing before you know his beliefs usually makes him defensive; education comes after exploring his concerns.
  • B. Agree he may keep taking the syrup if he restarts ATT: Negotiating the syrup comes later, if at all; first you need to know why he stopped ATT, or he may stop it again.
  • C. Arrange directly observed treatment at the local DOTS centre: DOTS is a sound later step for adherence, but arranging it before understanding why he stopped treatment skips the initial conversation.
  • D. Warn him that the hakeem's syrup may itself damage his liver: A warning without first hearing his reasons is likely to be dismissed, and it does not address his fear of liver damage from ATT.

Key point: Explore a patient’s health beliefs and fears non-judgmentally when they abandon allopathic treatment for alternative remedies.

Question 10 · Communication skills (PGMI) · medium

A 45-year-old man is admitted with severe pneumonia. His HIV test returns positive. Later, his wife approaches you in the corridor and says, "Please tell me the truth, does my husband have a serious infection? I have a right to know." The patient has not yet been informed of the result.

What is the most appropriate response?

  1. A. Acknowledge her worry; explain you must speak to him first
  2. B. Inform her of the diagnosis since she is his spouse and at risk
  3. C. Tell her that he only has pneumonia and nothing else to worry about
  4. D. Explain that hospital policy forbids talking to relatives entirely
  5. E. Advise her to ask her husband directly when he wakes up
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Acknowledge her worry; explain you must speak to him first

Confidentiality is a cornerstone of medical ethics. A patient’s medical information, especially sensitive diagnoses like HIV, cannot be disclosed to anyone—including a spouse—without the patient’s explicit consent. While the spouse is potentially at risk, the first step must always be to inform the patient of their diagnosis, assess their capacity, and encourage them to disclose the information to their partner (or consent to the doctor doing so). Disclosing before the patient knows violates their autonomy and right to privacy.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Inform her of the diagnosis since she is his spouse and at risk: Breaches patient confidentiality; the patient must be informed first and asked for consent to share.
  • C. Tell her that he only has pneumonia and nothing else to worry about: Lying to the family is unethical and damages trust when the truth inevitably emerges.
  • D. Explain that hospital policy forbids talking to relatives entirely: Unnecessarily rigid and dismissive; communication is permitted with consent or in specific emergencies.
  • E. Advise her to ask her husband directly when he wakes up: Deflects responsibility inappropriately and places the burden entirely on the uninformed patient.

Key point: Maintain patient confidentiality with family members by insisting that test results must be discussed with the patient first.
