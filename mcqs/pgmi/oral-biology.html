Question 1 · Embryology of the face, mouth and teeth · easy

A 32-year-old man presents to a dental clinic in Mardan with inability to close his right eye and drooping of the right corner of his mouth 2 days after an ear infection. His ability to clench his posterior teeth tightly and move his mandible from side to side against resistance remains completely intact.

The muscles whose function remains intact in this patient develop from which embryonic structure?

  1. A. First pharyngeal arch mesoderm
  2. B. Second pharyngeal arch mesoderm
  3. C. Third pharyngeal arch mesoderm
  4. D. Fourth pharyngeal arch mesoderm
  5. E. Occipital somitic myotomes
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. First pharyngeal arch mesoderm

Each pharyngeal arch carries its own cranial nerve component that innervates the striated muscles derived from that arch. The first pharyngeal arch mesoderm differentiates into the four principal muscles of mastication (masseter, temporalis, medial pterygoid, and lateral pterygoid), along with the mylohyoid, anterior belly of the digastric, tensor tympani, and tensor veli palatini. Because these muscles originate in the first arch, they receive motor innervation from the mandibular division of the trigeminal nerve (cranial nerve V3). The second pharyngeal arch mesoderm forms the muscles of facial expression (including orbicularis oculi and orbicularis oris), stapedius, stylohyoid, and posterior belly of the digastric, all supplied by the facial nerve (cranial nerve VII). Consequently, a facial nerve lesion spares mastication while paralysing facial expression.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Second pharyngeal arch mesoderm: Second arch mesoderm gives rise to the muscles of facial expression innervated by the facial nerve, which are paralysed in this patient with inability to close his eye.
  • C. Third pharyngeal arch mesoderm: Third arch mesoderm forms only the stylopharyngeus muscle innervated by the glossopharyngeal nerve, which elevates the pharynx during swallowing rather than clenching the teeth.
  • D. Fourth pharyngeal arch mesoderm: Fourth arch mesoderm develops into the cricothyroid, levator veli palatini, and pharyngeal constrictors, which participate in phonation and swallowing rather than mandibular clenching.
  • E. Occipital somitic myotomes: Occipital somites form the intrinsic and extrinsic muscles of the tongue innervated by the hypoglossal nerve, not the muscles responsible for elevating and grinding the mandible.

Key point: Distinguish the pharyngeal arch origins and motor innervation of the muscles of mastication from the muscles of facial expression.

Question 2 · Oral histology: enamel, dentine, pulp and periodontium · easy

During an operative dentistry seminar at a dental college in Peshawar, a lecturer explains that bonding composite resin to uncut mature dental enamel relies on acid etching of a tissue that is vastly more mineralised than dentine, cementum, or bone when compared by weight and by volume.

What is the approximate composition of mature enamel by weight?

  1. A. 70% mineral, 20% organic, 10% water
  2. B. 96% mineral, 1% organic, 3% water
  3. C. 65% mineral, 23% collagen, 12% water
  4. D. 80% mineral, 15% amelogenin, 5% water
  5. E. 50% mineral, 30% organic, 20% water
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. 96% mineral, 1% organic, 3% water

Mature human dental enamel is the hardest and most densely mineralised biological tissue in the human body. By weight, fully formed enamel consists of approximately 96% inorganic mineral (predominantly carbonated calcium-deficient hydroxyapatite crystals, Ca10(PO4)6(OH)2, which are hexagonal in cross-section and roughly ten times larger in width and thickness than the crystals of bone, dentine, or cementum), 1% organic material, and 3% water (total 4% organic material and water by weight). Because hydroxyapatite has a much higher specific gravity (~3.1 g/cm3) than organic proteins and water (~1.0-1.3 g/cm3), expressing composition by volume yields approximately 86% to 90% inorganic mineral, 1% to 2% organic matrix, and 8% to 12% water filling the fine intercrystalline pores. Unlike dentine, cementum, and bone—whose organic matrices are 90% type I collagen—mature enamel contains NO collagen; its sparse remaining organic matrix consists of low-molecular-weight non-amelogenins (tuftelin, ameloblastin, enamelin peptides) and lipids.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. 70% mineral, 20% organic, 10% water: These percentages describe the composition of mature human dentine rather than mature dental enamel, which has far less organic material.
  • C. 65% mineral, 23% collagen, 12% water: These values correspond to cellular cementum and bundle bone, both of which contain abundant type I collagen that is completely absent from mature enamel.
  • D. 80% mineral, 15% amelogenin, 5% water: Amelogenins constitute 90% of the immature secretory enamel matrix but are degraded and removed during maturation, leaving less than 1% organic content by weight.
  • E. 50% mineral, 30% organic, 20% water: These values describe partially mineralised early secretory enamel before maturation-stage protein removal and crystal growth in thickness.

Key point: State the percentage composition of mature dental enamel by weight (96% inorganic, 1% organic, 3% water) and by volume, and contrast its non-collagenous organic matrix with collagenous dentine and bone.

Question 3 · Orofacial anatomy for dentistry · easy

During surgical removal of a deeply fractured distal root of the lower left second molar (37) at a hospital in Swat, the oral surgeon accidentally perforates the thin lingual cortical plate 4 mm below the root apex. The displaced root fragment enters a potential space lying immediately inferior to the posterior attachment of the muscle that forms the floor of the mouth.

Which bony landmark on the medial surface of the mandibular body separates the space entered by this root from the oral cavity proper?

  1. A. External oblique ridge
  2. B. Inferior genial tubercle
  3. C. Mandibular lingula
  4. D. Pterygomandibular raphe
  5. E. Mylohyoid line
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Mylohyoid line

The internal (lingual) surface of the body of the mandible is divided into two distinct regions by an oblique ridge of bone—the mylohyoid line (internal oblique ridge)—which gives origin to the mylohyoid muscle forming the muscular diaphragm of the floor of the mouth. The mylohyoid line begins below the genial tubercles near the symphysis and ascends posteriorly to end behind the third molar socket. Consequently, the shallow depression above the anterior part of the line (the sublingual fossa, lodging the sublingual gland) lies within the sublingual space of the oral cavity, and the root apices of the incisors, canine, premolars, and usually the first molar lie superior to the mylohyoid attachment. Conversely, the root apices of the mandibular second and third molars lie inferior to the posterior part of the mylohyoid line adjacent to the submandibular fossa; therefore, periapical infections or displaced root tips of 37 and 38 pass directly into the submandibular space.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. External oblique ridge: The external oblique ridge lies on the lateral (buccal) surface of the mandible continuing from the anterior border of the ramus and does not bound the lingual spaces.
  • B. Inferior genial tubercle: The inferior genial tubercle is a midline spine on the inner surface of the symphysis menti where the geniohyoid muscle originates, far anterior to the second molar.
  • C. Mandibular lingula: The lingula is a sharp bony tongue on the medial surface of the ramus guarding the mandibular foramen, located well superior and posterior to the molar root apices.
  • D. Pterygomandibular raphe: The pterygomandibular raphe is a tendinous band extending from the pterygoid hamulus to the posterior end of the mylohyoid line, not a bony ridge separating the lingual fossae.

Key point: Explain how the oblique course of the mylohyoid line on the medial surface of the mandible determines whether molar root apices communicate with the sublingual or submandibular space.

Question 4 · Tooth morphology and occlusion · easy

A 9-year-old boy from Peshawar is referred by an overseas dentist with a mixed-dentition chart that records caries in two teeth: Tooth #14 (Universal system) and Tooth #K (Universal system). The receiving dental house officer must convert both designations into the FDI two-digit system and the Zsigmondy-Palmer quadrant notation used on hospital charts.

Which tooth is Universal #14?

  1. A. Upper right first premolar (FDI 14)
  2. B. Lower left second premolar (FDI 35)
  3. C. Upper left first molar (FDI 26)
  4. D. Upper right central incisor (FDI 11)
  5. E. Primary upper left first molar (FDI 64)
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Upper left first molar (FDI 26)

Three major tooth-numbering systems are tested in dental examinations worldwide: (1) The FDI (Federation Dentaire Internationale / ISO 3950) two-digit system—standard in Pakistan and internationally—assigns a first digit for the quadrant (clockwise from the dentist view: 1 = permanent upper right, 2 = permanent upper left, 3 = permanent lower left, 4 = permanent lower right; and for deciduous teeth: 5 = primary upper right, 6 = primary upper left, 7 = primary lower left, 8 = primary lower right) and a second digit for the tooth position from the midline (1 to 8 for permanent teeth: central incisor to third molar; 1 to 5 for primary teeth: central incisor to second molar). (2) The Zsigmondy-Palmer notation uses an L-shaped quadrant bracket matching the patient quadrant view with numbers 1 to 8 from the midline for permanent teeth and capital letters A to E from the midline for primary teeth. (3) The Universal (ADA) system counts sequentially clockwise around the arches starting at the maxillary right posterior end: for permanent teeth, #1 is the upper right third molar (FDI 18), #3 is the upper right first molar (16), #6 is the upper right canine (13), #8 is the upper right central incisor (11), #9 is the upper left central incisor (21), #14 is the upper left first molar (26), #16 is the upper left third molar (28), dropping down to #17 for the lower left third molar (38), #19 for the lower left first molar (36), #24/#25 for the lower central incisors (31/41), #30 for the lower right first molar (46), and #32 for the lower right third molar (48). For primary teeth, Universal letters A to T follow the exact same clockwise path: A is the primary upper right second molar (55), E/F are the primary upper central incisors (51/61), J is the primary upper left second molar (65), K is the primary lower left second molar (75), O/P are the primary lower central incisors (71/81), and T is the primary lower right second molar (85).

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Upper right first premolar (FDI 14): Universal #14 counts clockwise from the upper right third molar (#1), so it lies on the upper left; the FDI and Universal numbers only look alike here.
  • B. Lower left second premolar (FDI 35): Lower teeth are Universal #17 to #32.
  • D. Upper right central incisor (FDI 11): The upper right central incisor is Universal #8.
  • E. Primary upper left first molar (FDI 64): Primary teeth carry letters (A to T) in the Universal system, not numbers.

Key point: Convert accurately between the FDI two-digit system (quadrants 1-4 permanent, 5-8 primary), Palmer notation (1-8 and A-E), and the Universal numbering system (1-32 clockwise permanent, A-T clockwise primary).

Question 5 · Oral mucosa, saliva, oral physiology and eruption · easy

During an introductory lecture on oral biology in Kohat, a professor explains that the mucous membrane lining the human oral cavity is divided functionally into three regional categories according to mechanical demands during chewing, speech, and gustation, and that these three categories occupy roughly 60%, 25%, and 15% of the total oral mucosal surface area.

Which functional type of oral mucosa covers the largest area (about 60%) of the mouth?

  1. A. Masticatory mucosa
  2. B. Lining mucosa
  3. C. Specialised mucosa
  4. D. Orthokeratinised mucosa
  5. E. Transitional mucosa
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Lining mucosa

Although the oral cavity is lined continuously by stratified squamous epithelium resting on a connective tissue lamina propria, its regional structure adapts dramatically to local mechanical and sensory functions, dividing oral mucosa into three main categories: (1) Lining mucosa (~60% of total oral mucosal surface area) covers mobile, distensible regions that must stretch during mastication, speech, and swallowing—specifically the labial and buccal mucosa (inner lips and cheeks), vestibular fornix and alveolar mucosa, floor of the mouth (sublingual sulcus), ventral (inferior) surface of the tongue, and the soft palate. Lining mucosa is covered by non-keratinised stratified squamous epithelium, has short broad rete ridges, contains elastic fibres in its lamina propria (for elastic recoil preventing cheek biting), and usually rests on a loose submucosa over mobile muscle. (2) Masticatory mucosa (~25% of surface area) covers regions subjected directly to heavy compressive and abrasive friction from the food bolus—specifically the gingiva (free and attached) and the hard palate. It is covered by keratinised (orthokeratinised or parakeratinised) stratified squamous epithelium, has tall narrow interdigitating rete ridges and connective tissue papillae, lacks elastic fibres in its dense inelastic lamina propria, and is either firmly bound directly to bone as a mucoperiosteum (attached gingiva, midline palatine raphe) or anchored via tough fibrous bands spanning a dense submucosa (lateral hard palate). (3) Specialised mucosa (~15% of surface area) covers the dorsum of the tongue, combining a predominantly masticatory-type papillary surface with sensory taste buds.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Masticatory mucosa: Masticatory mucosa (gingiva and hard palate) covers about 25%.
  • C. Specialised mucosa: Specialised mucosa is limited to the dorsum of the tongue, about 15%.
  • D. Orthokeratinised mucosa: This describes a type of keratinisation, not a functional type of mucosa.
  • E. Transitional mucosa: The vermilion is a transition zone, not one of the three functional types.

Key point: Classify the oral mucosa into lining (~60%), masticatory (~25%), and specialised (~15%) types, and list the anatomical sites and key histological features of each.

Question 6 · Embryology of the face, mouth and teeth · easy

A histological study at a dental college in Hyderabad examines developing tooth germs from an experimental model in which cranial neural crest cell migration into the first pharyngeal arch was selectively impaired, while oral ectoderm remained undamaged.

Which dental tissue arises directly from the undamaged embryonic layer rather than cranial neural crest cells?

  1. A. Dentine
  2. B. Dental pulp
  3. C. Cementum
  4. D. Enamel
  5. E. Periodontal ligament
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. Enamel

Cranial neural crest cells arise from the neuroectoderm at the lateral crests of the neural folds in the hindbrain and midbrain regions. Unlike trunk mesenchyme, most connective and skeletal tissues of the facial and oral region derive from these migrated neural crest cells, which are therefore termed ectomesenchyme. In the developing tooth, cranial neural crest cells populate the dental papilla (giving rise to odontoblasts, dentine, and dental pulp) and the dental follicle or sac (giving rise to cementoblasts/cementum, periodontal ligament fibroblasts, and alveolar bone proper). Only the enamel organ originates from the surface oral ectoderm of the stomodeum, which proliferates to form the dental lamina and differentiates into ameloblasts that secrete enamel. Nonetheless, reciprocal induction between oral ectoderm and neural crest ectomesenchyme is essential for normal morphogenesis.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Dentine: Dentine is secreted by odontoblasts that differentiate directly from the dental papilla, which is derived from cranial neural crest ectomesenchyme rather than oral ectoderm.
  • B. Dental pulp: The connective tissue of the dental pulp develops from the ectomesenchymal dental papilla originating from migrating cranial neural crest cells.
  • C. Cementum: Cementoblasts differentiate from the dental follicle (sac), an ectomesenchymal structure populated by cranial neural crest cells rather than oral ectoderm.
  • E. Periodontal ligament: Fibroblasts and osteoblasts of the periodontal ligament and alveolar bone proper originate from the neural crest-derived ectomesenchyme of the dental follicle.

Key point: Distinguish the oral ectodermal origin of dental enamel from the cranial neural crest (ectomesenchymal) origin of dentine, pulp, cementum, and periodontal tissues.

Question 7 · Oral histology: enamel, dentine, pulp and periodontium · easy

A 9-year-old girl in Abbottabad undergoes surgical exposure of an unerupted upper left central incisor (21). During flap elevation, the surgeon carefully preserves the thin, stratified epithelial covering enveloping the fully mineralised enamel crown so that it can fuse with the oral epithelium and form the initial dentogingival junction.

What is this epithelial covering called?

  1. A. Dental lamina
  2. B. Hertwig epithelial root sheath
  3. C. Reduced enamel epithelium
  4. D. Rests of Malassez
  5. E. Vestibular lamina
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Reduced enamel epithelium

Once the maturation stage of amelogenesis is finished and the enamel has reached its final 96% mineralisation, the ameloblasts lose their ruffle-ended/smooth-ended border, shorten to cuboidal/low columnar cells, and secrete a basal lamina-like structure (the primary enamel cuticle) onto the enamel surface to which they attach via hemidesmosomes. Together with the condensed remnants of the stratum intermedium, stellate reticulum, and outer enamel epithelium, these post-maturation ameloblasts form a stratified epithelial layer covering the entire crown called the reduced enamel epithelium (REE). During this protective stage of the ameloblast life cycle, the REE performs three vital functions: (1) it physically separates the mature enamel surface from the vascular neural crest ectomesenchyme of the surrounding dental follicle—if the REE is damaged or breaks down prematurely, follicular cells contact the enamel and either deposit afibrillar coronal cementum onto the crown or differentiate into clastic cells causing pre-eruptive coronal resorption and bony ankylosis; (2) it secretes proteases and signalling molecules that assist connective tissue breakdown along the eruption pathway; and (3) as the tooth pierces the oral mucosa, the REE fuses with the oral epithelium without bleeding and transforms directly into the initial (primary) junctional epithelium of the dentogingival junction.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Dental lamina: The dental lamina starts tooth development and breaks up early; it does not cover the finished crown.
  • B. Hertwig epithelial root sheath: The root sheath shapes the root; it does not cover the crown.
  • D. Rests of Malassez: Rests of Malassez are remnants of the root sheath in the periodontal ligament.
  • E. Vestibular lamina: The vestibular lamina splits to form the vestibule between the lips and the alveolar process.

Key point: State the origin and three clinical functions of the reduced enamel epithelium during the protective and eruptive stages of the ameloblast life cycle.

Question 8 · Orofacial anatomy for dentistry · easy

A 19-year-old dental student in Gilgit reviews a coronal CT scan of the nasal cavity and paranasal sinuses with an oral radiologist. The scan demonstrates a blocked ostium in the hiatus semilunaris causing fluid accumulation in the left maxillary sinus, while tears drain freely into the space beneath the lowest nasal concha.

Into which nasal spaces do the nasolacrimal duct and the maxillary sinus ostium respectively open?

  1. A. Inferior nasal meatus and superior nasal meatus
  2. B. Middle nasal meatus and spheno-ethmoidal recess
  3. C. Middle nasal meatus and inferior nasal meatus
  4. D. Spheno-ethmoidal recess and middle nasal meatus
  5. E. Inferior nasal meatus and middle nasal meatus
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Inferior nasal meatus and middle nasal meatus

The lateral wall of the nasal cavity features three scroll-like projections—the superior, middle, and inferior nasal conchae (turbinates)—which overhang three corresponding passages called the superior, middle, and inferior nasal meatuses. Above the superior concha lies the spheno-ethmoidal recess, which receives the opening of the sphenoid sinus. The superior nasal meatus receives the openings of the posterior ethmoidal air cells. The middle nasal meatus contains a rounded prominence (the ethmoidal bulla, formed by middle ethmoidal cells) and a curved groove below it called the hiatus semilunaris; into this middle meatus drain the frontal sinus, anterior and middle ethmoidal air cells, and the maxillary sinus. The inferior nasal meatus, situated beneath the inferior concha, receives only one structure: the inferior opening of the nasolacrimal duct, guarded by a mucosal fold (Hasner valve) about 2 cm behind the nostril.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Inferior nasal meatus and superior nasal meatus: Although the nasolacrimal duct opens into the inferior meatus, the superior nasal meatus receives only the posterior ethmoidal air cells, not the maxillary sinus.
  • B. Middle nasal meatus and spheno-ethmoidal recess: The spheno-ethmoidal recess lies above the superior concha and receives the opening of the sphenoid sinus rather than either the nasolacrimal duct or maxillary sinus.
  • C. Middle nasal meatus and inferior nasal meatus: This reverses the two openings: the nasolacrimal duct drains below the inferior concha, whereas the maxillary sinus drains into the middle meatus.
  • D. Spheno-ethmoidal recess and middle nasal meatus: While the maxillary sinus opens into the middle meatus, the nasolacrimal duct opens into the inferior meatus rather than the spheno-ethmoidal recess.

Key point: Identify the specific nasal meatuses that receive the nasolacrimal duct (inferior meatus) and the maxillary, frontal, and anterior/middle ethmoidal sinuses (middle meatus).

Question 9 · Tooth morphology and occlusion · easy

A mother brings her healthy 15-month-old daughter to a paediatric dental clinic in Swat for a routine check-up. Intraoral inspection shows that all eight primary incisors (lower and upper central and lateral incisors) and four posterior primary molars have erupted, while the four primary canines sit in the gum pads still unerupted.

What is the usual order of eruption of the primary teeth?

  1. A. A, B, D, C, E
  2. B. A, B, C, D, E
  3. C. B, A, D, C, E
  4. D. A, B, E, D, C
  5. E. A, D, B, C, E
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. A, B, D, C, E

According to Wheeler standard chronology of the human primary (deciduous) dentition (Logan and Kronfeld, modified by Lunt and Law), three developmental milestones govern all 20 primary teeth: (1) Initial Calcification (Mineralisation) in Utero: Every single primary tooth begins calcifying during the second trimester of pregnancy between 13 and 19 weeks (4 to 5 months) in utero—specifically in the order Central incisor (~14 weeks in utero), First molar (~15.5 weeks), Lateral incisor (~16 weeks), Canine (~17 weeks), and Second molar (~18-19 weeks; sequence A-D-B-C-E). At birth, none of the primary crowns are completely calcified (all finish crown completion between 1.5 and 11 months after birth, which is why all 20 primary teeth record a neonatal line!). (2) Clinical Eruption Sequence and Age Ranges: Primary teeth erupt between 6 and 30 (up to 33) months of age in the classic order A - B - D - C - E (note that the first primary molar [D] erupts BEFORE the primary canine [C]): (a) Mandibular central incisor (81, 71): 6 to 10 months (first tooth in the mouth); Maxillary central incisor (51, 61): 8 to 12 months. (b) Maxillary lateral incisor (52, 62): 9 to 13 months; Mandibular lateral incisor (82, 72): 10 to 16 months (the only pair where the maxillary tooth typically precedes the mandibular tooth!). (c) First primary molar (54, 64, 84, 74): 13 to 19 months (maxillary 13-19 months, mandibular 14-18 months)—explaining why a 15-month-old child has erupted first molars with an empty canine space ahead of them! (d) Primary canine (53, 63, 83, 73): 16 to 23 months (maxillary 16-22 months, mandibular 17-23 months). (e) Second primary molar (55, 65, 85, 75): 23 to 33 months (mandibular 23-31 months, maxillary 25-33 months). (3) Root Completion: In the primary dentition, root completion occurs approximately 1 to 1.5 years after eruption, so all 20 primary roots are fully formed by 1.5 to 3 years of age.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. A, B, C, D, E: The primary first molar erupts before the canine.
  • C. B, A, D, C, E: Central incisors erupt before lateral incisors.
  • D. A, B, E, D, C: The second molar is the last primary tooth to erupt.
  • E. A, D, B, C, E: The lateral incisors erupt before the first molars.

Key point: State the intrauterine calcification timeline (13-19 weeks in utero, sequence A-D-B-C-E), eruption sequence and age ranges (A-B-D-C-E from 6 to 33 months, noting D before C and upper B before lower B), and root completion window (1.5-3 years) of the primary dentition.

Question 10 · Oral mucosa, saliva, oral physiology and eruption · easy

A 19-year-old woman in Muzaffarabad presents to a dental clinic worried about a V-shaped row of 10 large, flat-topped circular projections at the back of her tongue that she noticed in the mirror, along with smooth depapillated red patches surrounded by yellowish-white serpentine borders on the anterior two-thirds of her tongue that change location every few days.

Which papillae are lost in these migrating red patches?

  1. A. Circumvallate papillae
  2. B. Fungiform papillae
  3. C. Foliate papillae
  4. D. Filiform papillae
  5. E. All four types equally
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. Filiform papillae

The specialised mucosa covering the anterior two-thirds (body/oral part) of the dorsum of the tongue features four distinct types of lingual papillae (whereas the posterior one-third behind the V-shaped sulcus terminalis has no true papillae, exhibiting instead low nodular elevations of submucosal lymphoid follicles that form the lingual tonsil): (1) Filiform papillae are by far the most numerous, covering the entire anterior dorsum in velvet-like rows parallel to the sulcus terminalis. They are slender, cone-shaped projections with a connective tissue core capped by pointed, heavily keratinised (parakeratinised/orthokeratinised) stratified squamous epithelium that points backward toward the pharynx. Crucially, filiform papillae contain NO taste buds; they provide an abrasive friction surface for compressing food against the hard palate and receive general tactile innervation from the lingual nerve (V3). Focal transient desquamation of filiform papillae produces the smooth red patches with raised white borders seen in erythema migrans (geographic tongue), while diffuse atrophy causes smooth beefy-red glossitis in iron, folate, or vitamin B12 deficiency. (2) Fungiform papillae are fewer, larger, smooth round mushroom-shaped projections scattered singly among the filiform papillae (most dense at the tip and lateral margins); they have a thin non-keratinised epithelium over a richly vascular core (appearing bright red) and contain 1 to 3 taste buds on their superior dome surface, innervated for taste by the chorda tympani (CN VII). (3) Foliate papillae consist of 4 to 11 parallel vertical folds along the posterolateral margin of the tongue just in front of the palatoglossal arch, bearing taste buds in their lateral cleft walls. (4) Circumvallate (vallate) papillae are the largest (1-2 mm wide), numbering 8 to 12 in a V-shaped line immediately ANTERIOR to the sulcus terminalis. Unlike other papillae, they do not project above the tongue surface but are surrounded by a deep circular trench (moat); their superior surface is lightly keratinised, while the non-keratinised lateral walls of the trench contain hundreds of taste buds flushed clean of tastants (and supplied with lingual lipase) by the ducts of pure serous salivary glands of von Ebner in the underlying muscle. Because the third-arch mucosa migrates slightly forward during development, the circumvallate papillae are innervated for both taste and general sensation by the glossopharyngeal nerve (CN IX).

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Circumvallate papillae: Circumvallate papillae lie in a V in front of the sulcus terminalis and are not lost in geographic tongue.
  • B. Fungiform papillae: Fungiform papillae remain as red dots; the patches are red because filiform papillae are lost.
  • C. Foliate papillae: Foliate papillae lie on the posterolateral border and are not the papillae lost here.
  • E. All four types equally: Geographic tongue is a selective loss of filiform papillae.

Key point: Compare the four types of lingual papillae (filiform, fungiform, foliate, circumvallate) in anatomy, keratinisation, presence/location of taste buds, associated von Ebner serous glands, and cranial nerve supply (V3, VII, IX).
