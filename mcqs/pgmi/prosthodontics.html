Question 1 · Complete dentures · easy

A 62-year-old man attends a dental hospital in Mardan for maxillomandibular jaw relation registration. Sitting upright with his head unsupported and looking straight ahead, he moistens his lips, swallows, and relaxes his jaw. Using a Willis gauge from the base of the nose to the underside of the chin, the clinician measures a vertical distance of 68 mm across three consistent readings. The clinician now trims the wax occlusal rims to establish the occlusal vertical dimension.

What should the nose-chin distance be with the rims in contact?

  1. A. 61 mm (7 mm less than rest position)
  2. B. 65 mm (3 mm less than the rest position)
  3. C. 68 mm (equal to the rest position)
  4. D. 71 mm (3 mm greater than rest position)
  5. E. 74 mm (6 mm greater than rest position)
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. 65 mm (3 mm less than the rest position)

According to GPT-9, the rest vertical dimension (RVD) is the distance between two selected anatomic or marked points (commonly subnasale and gnathion using a Willis gauge, or two skin dots on the nose tip and chin measured with dividers) when the mandible is in the physiologic rest position. The occlusal vertical dimension (OVD) is the distance between those same two points when the occluding members (teeth or wax rims) are in contact. The difference between the two (RVD minus OVD) is the interocclusal rest space, clinically termed the freeway space. In most patients, a physiological freeway space of 2 to 4 mm is required at the premolar region so the elevator muscles can relax between swallowing and chewing cycles. Therefore, with an RVD of 68 mm, the OVD must be set at 64 to 66 mm (65 mm).

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. 61 mm (7 mm less than rest position): Subtracting 7 mm creates an excessive freeway space that leaves the lower face overclosed, reduces masticatory efficiency, and predisposes to angular cheilitis.
  • C. 68 mm (equal to the rest position): Making the occlusal vertical dimension equal to the rest vertical dimension leaves zero freeway space, causing constant muscle fatigue, tooth clicking, and generalized ridge pain.
  • D. 71 mm (3 mm greater than rest position): Setting the occlusal vertical dimension 3 mm greater than the rest vertical dimension encroaches on the physiological rest position and causes severe elevator muscle spasm.
  • E. 74 mm (6 mm greater than rest position): Increasing the vertical dimension 6 mm beyond rest position prevents lip closure, stretches the facial soft tissues, and accelerates residual ridge resorption.

Key point: Calculate the occlusal vertical dimension from the rest vertical dimension by subtracting a physiological freeway space of 2 to 4 mm.

Question 2 · Bridges and occlusion · medium

A 30-year-old schoolteacher in Hyderabad requires a three-unit metal-ceramic fixed partial denture replacing her missing maxillary right first premolar (14). When she smiles and speaks, the cervical third of the premolar region and the adjacent gingival margin are completely exposed. The edentulous ridge has normal buccolingual width and height. She asks for a lifelike tooth profile that hides any gap at the gumline while still allowing her to clean under the bridge daily with dental floss.

Which pontic design best meets aesthetics and hygiene here?

  1. A. All-metal sanitary pontic with 3 mm ridge clearance
  2. B. Conical egg-shaped pontic contacting only the ridge apex
  3. C. Modified ridge-lap pontic with labial-only ridge contact
  4. D. Saddle ridge-lap pontic wrapping over both cortical plates
  5. E. Deeply recessed ovate pontic requiring alveolar bone trephination
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Modified ridge-lap pontic with labial-only ridge contact

The modified ridge-lap pontic is the design of choice for most replacements in the aesthetic zone (maxillary and mandibular incisors, canines, and premolars) when the edentulous ridge is already healed. It combines the facial aesthetic emergence of a ridge-lap with the hygienic convex lingual contour of a sanitary pontic. Specifically, tissue contact is strictly restricted to a T-shaped or crescent zone on the labial aspect of the residual ridge, never extending lingually past the midline of the ridge crest. The tissue-facing surface must remain mesiodistally convex and free of any buccolingual hollow on the palatal side, enabling dental floss threaded through the proximal embrasures to sweep smoothly across the entire gingival interface from lingual to facial without snagging.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. All-metal sanitary pontic with 3 mm ridge clearance: Leaving a 3 mm open space above the gingiva in a patient with a high smile line creates a dark, unsightly gap in the visible maxillary premolar area.
  • B. Conical egg-shaped pontic contacting only the ridge apex: A conical pontic tapers to a single point at the ridge crest, leaving wide open facial and triangular gingival embrasures that appear unnatural in the maxillary aesthetic zone.
  • D. Saddle ridge-lap pontic wrapping over both cortical plates: Although a full saddle looks lifelike labially, its deeply concave tissue surface across both ridge slopes makes flossing impossible and causes chronic mucosal inflammation.
  • E. Deeply recessed ovate pontic requiring alveolar bone trephination: Creating a surgical bone depression for an ovate pontic in a healed ridge is unnecessarily invasive when a modified ridge-lap provides excellent aesthetics and cleanability.

Key point: Describe the contour and clinical indication of the modified ridge-lap pontic in visible edentulous areas.

Question 3 · Implants, maxillofacial prosthodontics and gerodontology · medium

A 49-year-old woman in a tertiary hospital in Peshawar is scheduled for a partial maxillectomy tomorrow morning to remove an adenoid cystic carcinoma of the minor salivary glands. The prosthodontist fabricates a clear heat-cured acrylic plate on a pre-operative modified cast to be wired or screwed in place in the operating theatre.

What is this first prosthesis for, and what replaces it?

  1. A. Definitive cobalt-chromium obturator worn permanently from the day of surgical resection without relining
  2. B. A surgical obturator that supports the pack at surgery and is replaced at 7 to 10 days by an interim obturator
  3. C. Interim hollow-bulb obturator inserted six months after surgery once scar contracture has ceased
  4. D. Palatal lift appliance designed to elevate an anatomically intact but neurogenically paralysed soft palate
  5. E. Radiation carrier stent containing radioactive iridium-192 seeds for immediate intracavitary brachytherapy
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. A surgical obturator that supports the pack at surgery and is replaced at 7 to 10 days by an interim obturator

Prosthodontic rehabilitation of maxillectomy defects proceeds in three sequential phases. First, an immediate surgical obturator (usually clear heat-cured polymethyl methacrylate without artificial posterior teeth over the defect to avoid occlusal trauma to the wound) is inserted in the operating theatre to restore palatal contour, support the split-thickness skin graft and Whitehead's varnish gauze pack, allow immediate oral feeding without a nasogastric tube, enable intelligible speech and reduce psychological distress. Second, at 7 to 10 days post-operatively when the surgical pack is removed, the prosthesis is converted into (or replaced by) an interim obturator that is periodically relined with tissue conditioners or resilient liners over the next 3 to 6 months as the cavity contracts. Third, once healing and radiation changes stabilize, a definitive cast-framework hollow-bulb obturator is fabricated.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Definitive cobalt-chromium obturator worn permanently from the day of surgical resection without relining: Definitive cast metal obturators cannot be constructed before surgery because the defect undergoes rapid dimensional contraction for 3 to 6 months.
  • C. Interim hollow-bulb obturator inserted six months after surgery once scar contracture has ceased: A prosthesis placed at operation is a surgical obturator rather than an interim device, and waiting six months leaves the patient with an open oronasal fistula and nasogastric tube dependence.
  • D. Palatal lift appliance designed to elevate an anatomically intact but neurogenically paralysed soft palate: Palatal lift prostheses treat palatopharyngeal incompetence when the soft palate is anatomically intact but neurogenically paralysed, not a surgical hard palate resection.
  • E. Radiation carrier stent containing radioactive iridium-192 seeds for immediate intracavitary brachytherapy: A clear plate inserted during tumour resection serves as a barrier and surgical pack stent rather than an afterloading brachytherapy carrier.

Key point: Outline the three phases of maxillectomy obturator rehabilitation: immediate surgical obturator at surgery, interim obturator at 7-10 days, and definitive obturator at 3-6 months.

Question 4 · Removable partial dentures · medium

A 62-year-old retired teacher in Mansehra presents with soreness and rapid sinking of the posterior saddles of a mandibular Kennedy Class I cobalt-chromium partial denture fitted two months ago. Examination shows the acrylic resin denture bases are narrow, covering only the sharp knife-edge crest of the posterior mandibular ridge and terminating 8 mm anterior to the retromolar pads.

How far should the lower distal extension bases extend?

  1. A. Medially across the mylohyoid ridge into the deep sublingual gland space and posteriorly onto the pterygomandibular raphe
  2. B. Anteriorly over the labial gingival margins of the lower incisors and laterally into the masseteric notch
  3. C. Exclusively onto the sharp alveolar crest while trimming the buccal flange short of the mucobuccal fold by 6 mm
  4. D. Laterally onto the cortical buccal shelves and posteriorly over at least two-thirds of the retromolar pads
  5. E. Superiorly over the occlusal surfaces of the natural premolars and lingually onto the palatoglossal arch
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. Laterally onto the cortical buccal shelves and posteriorly over at least two-thirds of the retromolar pads

To minimise vertical tissueward movement and reduce torque on the terminal abutment teeth, a mandibular distal extension denture base must follow the snowshoe principle by covering the maximum anatomical area permitted by the surrounding musculature. In the mandible, the crest of the residual ridge often resorbs into sharp, cancellous bone covered by thin or movable mucosa, making it unsuitable as a primary stress-bearing zone. Instead, the primary stress-bearing area is the buccal shelf—bounded medially by the crest of the residual ridge, laterally by the external oblique ridge, anteriorly by the buccal frenum, and posteriorly by the retromolar pad. Because the buccal shelf consists of dense cortical bone arranged horizontally at right angles to vertical occlusal forces, extending the base laterally onto the buccal shelf and posteriorly over at least half to two-thirds of the retromolar pad is essential.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Medially across the mylohyoid ridge into the deep sublingual gland space and posteriorly onto the pterygomandibular raphe: Extending rigid acrylic below an undercut mylohyoid ridge causes severe mucosal ulceration, and covering the pterygomandibular raphe dislodges the denture when the mouth opens wide.
  • B. Anteriorly over the labial gingival margins of the lower incisors and laterally into the masseteric notch: Covering the labial gingiva causes periodontal inflammation, and overextending into the masseteric region leads to displacement when the masseter contracts.
  • C. Exclusively onto the sharp alveolar crest while trimming the buccal flange short of the mucobuccal fold by 6 mm: The resorbed mandibular alveolar crest consists of porous cancellous bone with thin mucosa and is not a primary stress-bearing area.
  • E. Superiorly over the occlusal surfaces of the natural premolars and lingually onto the palatoglossal arch: Covering the palatoglossal arch triggers gagging and painful ulceration during swallowing rather than supporting the posterior saddle.

Key point: Identify the mandibular buccal shelf and retromolar pad as essential anatomical extensions for supporting a mandibular distal extension partial denture base.

Question 5 · Complete dentures · medium

A 60-year-old edentulous man attends a hospital dental clinic in Swabi for the wax trial insertion of his complete dentures. On the articulator, the maxillary and mandibular teeth intercuspate tightly in Class I occlusion. However, when the prosthodontist guides his mandible into centric relation in the mouth, the right premolars strike first on their cusp tips, and as he closes firmly, the mandible slides 2.5 mm anteriorly and to the left before reaching maximum intercuspation.

What is the most appropriate clinical step to correct this error before processing the dentures?

  1. A. Grind the right premolar cusp tips intraorally with a stone bur until the remaining wax teeth appear to touch evenly
  2. B. Ask the patient to bite forcefully in his habitual forward position for five minutes to soften the mounting wax and shift the teeth
  3. C. Make a new passive centric relation record at the correct vertical dimension and remount the mandibular cast before resetting
  4. D. Process the dentures into heat-cured polymethyl methacrylate as they are and perform an intraoral selective grind at insertion
  5. E. Repeat the maxillary face-bow transfer using a kinematic hinge-axis locator while keeping the mandibular cast on its original mounting
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Make a new passive centric relation record at the correct vertical dimension and remount the mandibular cast before resetting

A primary objective of the wax trial denture stage (try-in) is verifying that maximum intercuspation on the articulator coincides with the patient centric relation at the planned occlusal vertical dimension. Because wax bases can shift and patients may inadvertently protrude during the initial jaw relation appointment, discrepancies must be caught before processing. If guiding the mandible into centric relation reveals a premature cuspal contact followed by an anterior or lateral slide into maximum intercuspation, the original centric relation mounting of the mandibular cast is wrong. Never process the dentures hoping to grind out a mounting error later. Instead, remove the interfering posterior teeth (or place softened Aluwax or elastomer over the posterior teeth without perforation), make a new passive centric relation record in the mouth, remount the mandibular cast on the articulator, and reset the teeth.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Grind the right premolar cusp tips intraorally with a stone bur until the remaining wax teeth appear to touch evenly: Attempting to eliminate a 2.5 mm anterolateral centric discrepancy by grinding plastic denture teeth in soft wax destroys tooth anatomy and fails to correct the false cast mounting.
  • B. Ask the patient to bite forcefully in his habitual forward position for five minutes to soften the mounting wax and shift the teeth: Warming wax in the mouth by forceful biting compresses the mucosa unevenly, loosens the prosthetic teeth in the wax rim, and leaves the casts mounted in the wrong position.
  • D. Process the dentures into heat-cured polymethyl methacrylate as they are and perform an intraoral selective grind at insertion: Processing a gross 2.5 mm horizontal jaw relation error into cured acrylic ruins the prosthesis because selective grinding corrects only minor processing errors up to 1 mm.
  • E. Repeat the maxillary face-bow transfer using a kinematic hinge-axis locator while keeping the mandibular cast on its original mounting: A 2.5 mm lateral slide in centric closure is caused by an inaccurate centric relation record of the mandible, not by a minor discrepancy in the maxillary face-bow transfer.

Key point: Manage a centric relation discrepancy discovered at the wax try-in stage by recording a new centric relation index and remounting the mandibular cast.

Question 6 · Bridges and occlusion · easy

A 26-year-old woman attends a dental teaching hospital in Peshawar requesting fixed replacement of her missing maxillary right lateral incisor (12), which was extracted 1 year ago after trauma. Clinical and radiographic examination shows an intact, unrestored maxillary right central incisor (11) and a robust, vital maxillary right canine (13) with a long conical root and favourable palatal clearance in intercuspal position and lateral excursions. To avoid preparing the central incisor and prevent differential rotational torque across two abutments, the prosthodontist plans a prosthesis supported solely by the canine.

Which bridge design carries the pontic from a retainer at one end only?

  1. A. Compound hybrid bridge combining rigid and movable units
  2. B. Cantilever bridge retained on the canine alone
  3. C. Fixed-fixed conventional bridge with bilateral full crowns
  4. D. Fixed-movable bridge with a precision intracoronal attachment
  5. E. Spring cantilever bridge with a long palatal bar
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Cantilever bridge retained on the canine alone

A cantilever fixed dental prosthesis supports a pontic using one or more retainers at one end of the edentulous space while the opposite end remains unattached. Geometrically, it acts as a Class I lever when the pontic is loaded occlusally, generating tipping and rotational forces on the abutment tooth. Consequently, cantilever bridges must be restricted to replacing a single tooth where occlusal forces on the pontic can be minimised. Replacing a missing maxillary lateral incisor off the adjacent maxillary canine is the classic clinical indication: the canine has a long root and dense bony support, and the lateral incisor pontic can be kept free of heavy contact during mandibular excursions. Involving the central incisor as a second abutment is unnecessary and risks debonding due to differential mobility.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Compound hybrid bridge combining rigid and movable units: A compound bridge joins two or more distinct prosthetic designs across multiple abutments, whereas this patient needs a single-tooth replacement anchored solely to the maxillary canine.
  • C. Fixed-fixed conventional bridge with bilateral full crowns: A fixed-fixed design requires rigid preparation of both the central incisor and the canine, which was specifically avoided here to preserve the intact central incisor.
  • D. Fixed-movable bridge with a precision intracoronal attachment: A fixed-movable design still requires preparation of a minor abutment on the mesial aspect of the span to house the mortise or tenon receptor.
  • E. Spring cantilever bridge with a long palatal bar: A spring cantilever uses a flexible palatal connector bar from a distant posterior molar rather than direct support from the immediately adjacent maxillary canine.

Key point: Identify the design and primary clinical indication of a cantilever fixed dental prosthesis for replacing a maxillary lateral incisor.

Question 7 · Implants, maxillofacial prosthodontics and gerodontology · medium

A 52-year-old man in a cancer hospital in Quetta is about to start 66 Gy of unilateral external-beam radiotherapy for a well-lateralized T2N0 squamous cell carcinoma of the right buccal mucosa. He has multiple amalgam and gold-alloy crowns on the ipsilateral posterior teeth, and the radiation oncologist requests an intraoral acrylic stent.

How does the radiation stent protect healthy tissue?

  1. A. It chelates free radicals in saliva by releasing calcium and phosphate ions during each linear accelerator treatment fraction
  2. B. It places 3 to 5 mm of PMMA over the metal crowns to absorb backscattered electrons and moves the tongue out of the field
  3. C. It increases the local tissue radiation dose around the metallic crowns by 40% to sterilize subgingival periodontal pockets
  4. D. It permanently blocks 100% of megavoltage photon beams using a 1 mm layer of clear autopolymerizing acrylic resin
  5. E. It stimulates parasympathetic secretomotor fibres in the parotid gland to double salivary flow during irradiation
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. It places 3 to 5 mm of PMMA over the metal crowns to absorb backscattered electrons and moves the tongue out of the field

Custom intraoral radiation stents fabricated before head and neck radiotherapy significantly reduce acute radiation mucositis, xerostomia and osteoradionecrosis. When megavoltage radiation strikes high-atomic-number dental restorations (such as gold crowns, amalgam fillings or titanium implants), it generates intense local secondary electron backscatter over a short distance (1 to 3 mm), causing severe focal ulceration and necrosis of the adjacent buccal mucosa or lateral tongue. Interposing 3 to 5 mm of tissue-equivalent, low-atomic-number polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) absorbs this electron backscatter before it reaches the mucosa. In addition, positioning stents reproducibly depress or deviate the mobile tongue, elevate the maxilla away from the mandible by opening the bite, and can incorporate low-fusing lead-bismuth alloy (Cerrobend) lined with acrylic to shield uninvolved contralateral salivary glands.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. It chelates free radicals in saliva by releasing calcium and phosphate ions during each linear accelerator treatment fraction: Polymethyl methacrylate is an inert polymer that does not release calcium or phosphate ions or chemically scavenge ionizing radiation free radicals.
  • C. It increases the local tissue radiation dose around the metallic crowns by 40% to sterilize subgingival periodontal pockets: Dose enhancement at the metal-mucosa interface is the harmful scatter effect caused by high-atomic-number metals, which the acrylic stent is specifically designed to prevent.
  • D. It permanently blocks 100% of megavoltage photon beams using a 1 mm layer of clear autopolymerizing acrylic resin: Megavoltage photons penetrate several centimetres of tissue and cannot be blocked by 1 mm of acrylic; true beam blocking requires thick Cerrobend (low-fusing lead alloy) shields.
  • E. It stimulates parasympathetic secretomotor fibres in the parotid gland to double salivary flow during irradiation: An intraoral stent acts mechanically by spatial displacement and scatter attenuation rather than neurogenically stimulating salivary acini.

Key point: Explain how an intraoral acrylic radiation stent attenuates secondary electron backscatter from metal restorations and displaces healthy tissues out of the radiation field.

Question 8 · Removable partial dentures · medium

A dental clinician in Quetta completes a border-moulded functional impression of the bilateral posterior ridges using custom acrylic trays attached to a verified cobalt-chromium framework for a mandibular Kennedy Class I patient. The framework with its completed functional impression is disinfected and sent to the dental laboratory along with the original stone master cast.

Which laboratory sequence correctly produces the altered master cast from this impression?

  1. A. Pour stone into the saddle impressions alone
  2. B. Remove the functional impression and reline the old cast with wax
  3. C. Cut off the stone ridges, seat the framework, box and pour
  4. D. Duplicate the impression and cast a second framework
  5. E. Press the impression onto the uncut ridges
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Cut off the stone ridges, seat the framework, box and pour

The laboratory phase of the altered cast (Applegate-Leupold corrected cast) technique replaces the anatomically recorded edentulous ridge portions of the original master cast with functionally recorded ridge contours while preserving the exact relationship to the natural teeth. First, the technician uses a plaster saw to section and remove the distal extension edentulous ridge segments from the original stone master cast just distal to the terminal abutment teeth, cutting mechanical retention grooves into the cut stone surfaces. Next, the cobalt-chromium framework carrying the functional impression is seated onto the remaining stone teeth; the technician verifies that every occlusal rest and indirect retainer is completely seated and lutes the framework to the stone teeth with sticky wax. Finally, the impression is boxed with wax, and a contrasting colour of dental stone is poured to form the corrected composite cast.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Pour stone into the saddle impressions alone: Pouring only the saddle impressions produces isolated ridge segments with no stone abutment teeth to relate the framework or articulate the occlusion.
  • B. Remove the functional impression and reline the old cast with wax: Removing the functional impression destroys the recorded mucosal contour and defeats the entire purpose of the altered cast procedure.
  • D. Duplicate the impression and cast a second framework: The existing cobalt-chromium framework has already been tried in and verified; it does not need to be recast in metal.
  • E. Press the impression onto the uncut ridges: Pressing the impression against uncut stone ridges breaks the impression and bends the framework rather than correcting the ridge contour.

Key point: Outline the laboratory steps for sectioning the original master cast, seating the framework, and pouring the altered cast.

Question 9 · Complete dentures · easy

A 62-year-old retired teacher attends a dental teaching hospital in Peshawar two days after insertion of his new maxillary and mandibular complete dentures. His upper denture stays firm at rest and during gentle speaking, but drops immediately whenever he yawns widely or moves his mandible sideways to chew tough chapati. Intraoral examination shows thick, overextended distobuccal flanges on the maxillary prosthesis that impinge on the posterior vestibule during excursion.

Which structure catches the denture during these movements?

  1. A. Zygomatico-alveolar crest
  2. B. Coronoid process
  3. C. Hamular notch
  4. D. Buccal frenum
  5. E. Pterygomandibular raphe
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Coronoid process

The distobuccal vestibule (coronomaxillary space) of the edentulous maxilla is bounded laterally by the mucosa covering the medial aspect of the mandibular ramus and its anterior projection. When a patient opens the mouth widely or makes lateral working and balancing excursions, this bony projection moves anteriorly and medially, narrowing the space lateral to the maxillary tuberosity. If the distobuccal flange of the maxillary complete denture is crafted too thick in acrylic or record rim wax, the advancing ramus pushes against the flange, breaking the peripheral seal and unseating the denture. During border moulding and try-in, the patient must open widely and move the jaw from side to side to shape the distobuccal contour accurately.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Zygomatico-alveolar crest: Overextension over the malar process causes soreness over the molar cheek prominence during biting rather than sudden dropping on wide yawning or lateral movement.
  • C. Hamular notch: Overextension into the pterygomaxillary cleft produces sharp pain behind the tuberosity on swallowing rather than displacement during lateral mandibular excursion.
  • D. Buccal frenum: Encroachment on this fold causes ulceration near the premolar region when the orbicularis oris and buccinator contract during smiling, not during wide yawning.
  • E. Pterygomandibular raphe: Impingement on a prominent raphe dislodges the maxillary denture at the extreme posterior border only on wide opening, not during lateral chewing excursions.

Key point: Identify how distobuccal flange overextension of a maxillary complete denture interferes with mandibular movement and causes dislodgement.

Question 10 · Bridges and occlusion · easy

A 37-year-old woman attends a dental hospital in Mardan requesting a fixed bridge to replace her missing maxillary right first and second premolars (14 and 15). Periapical radiographs of the proposed abutment teeth—the maxillary right canine (13) and maxillary right first molar (16)—show healthy lamina dura and no horizontal bone loss. Before finalising the design, the clinician compares the combined pericemental root surface area of teeth 13 and 16 against the normal root surface area of the missing premolars.

What does Ante's law require of the abutments?

  1. A. Combined abutment root length must be at least twice the facial clinical crown height
  2. B. Each proposed abutment tooth must possess at least two widely divergent roots in bone
  3. C. The combined root surface area of the abutments must equal or exceed that of the teeth being replaced
  4. D. Total mesiodistal width of the abutment crowns must exceed the edentulous span width
  5. E. Abutment pulp chamber volume must measure less than half the total root canal volume
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. The combined root surface area of the abutments must equal or exceed that of the teeth being replaced

Formulated by Irwin H. Ante in 1926 and popularised by Johnston, Phillips, and Dykema, Ante law states that the combined pericemental (periodontal ligament) root surface area of the abutment teeth supporting a fixed dental prosthesis must be equal to or greater than the combined pericemental root surface area of the tooth or teeth being replaced. In the maxilla, the first molar (approximately 433 square millimetres) and canine (approximately 273 square millimetres) have the largest root surface areas, making them strong abutments to replace the first and second premolars (approximately 234 and 220 square millimetres respectively). Although modern evidence shows that teeth with reduced but healthy periodontium can occasionally support bridges if plaque and occlusal forces are controlled, Ante law remains the standard clinical benchmark against overloading abutments.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Combined abutment root length must be at least twice the facial clinical crown height: Irwin Ante formulated his rule around three-dimensional periodontal membrane surface area in bone rather than linear root-to-crown length proportions.
  • B. Each proposed abutment tooth must possess at least two widely divergent roots in bone: Canines and premolars are single-rooted teeth yet routinely serve as valid bridge abutments when their pericemental surface area meets the span demand.
  • D. Total mesiodistal width of the abutment crowns must exceed the edentulous span width: Abutment assessment under this classical law compares periodontal root surface attachment in alveolar bone rather than coronal mesiodistal crown widths.
  • E. Abutment pulp chamber volume must measure less than half the total root canal volume: Pulp chamber volume guides safe axial reduction depth during tooth preparation but plays no role in the periodontal load-bearing calculation of Ante law.

Key point: State Ante law regarding the required pericemental root surface area of abutment teeth relative to the teeth being replaced.
