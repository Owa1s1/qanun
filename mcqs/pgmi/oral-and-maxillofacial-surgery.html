Question 1 · Local anaesthesia, exodontia and minor oral surgery · easy

A 39-year-old man returns to the emergency dental clinic in Charsadda 4 hours after an uncomplicated forceps extraction of his lower right permanent first molar (46) under 2% lidocaine with 1:80,000 adrenaline. Primary haemostasis was confirmed before discharge, and he has no personal or family history of bleeding disorders, takes no medications, and has an INR of 1.0 (normal 0.8-1.2) and platelets of 235,000/µL (150,000-400,000). Examination shows a large, jelly-like liver clot overflowing the socket with continuous dark-red oozing from the buccal gingival margin.

What type of haemorrhage is this, and what is the first management?

  1. A. Primary haemorrhage; administer intravenous tranexamic acid and fresh frozen plasma in the emergency room immediately
  2. B. Reactionary haemorrhage; remove the loose clot, pack with oxidised cellulose and suture the socket
  3. C. Secondary haemorrhage; prescribe oral amoxicillin 500 mg and metronidazole 400 mg without disturbing the clot
  4. D. Disseminated intravascular coagulation; cauterise the entire alveolar bone socket wall with monopolar diathermy
  5. E. Arteriovenous malformation haemorrhage; ligate the ipsilateral external carotid artery in the neck urgently
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Reactionary haemorrhage; remove the loose clot, pack with oxidised cellulose and suture the socket

Post-extraction haemorrhage is classified into three temporal categories: primary haemorrhage (occurring intraoperatively during the extraction), reactionary (intermediate) haemorrhage (occurring within 2 to 24 hours postoperatively as local adrenaline vasoconstriction wears off, reactive vasodilation occurs, or systemic blood pressure rises from pain or anxiety), and secondary haemorrhage (occurring 7 to 10 days postoperatively when secondary infection causes proteolytic lysis of the organising blood clot). Over 90% of post-extraction bleeding originates from the gingival mucoperiosteal margins rather than bone. Under local anaesthesia, the clinician should gently aspirate the exuberant non-adherent 'liver clot' to inspect the socket, place a resorbable local haemostatic agent (such as oxidised regenerated cellulose or absorbable gelatin sponge) into the socket, place a horizontal mattress suture across the socket to compress the gingival periosteum against bone, and apply firm gauze pressure for 20 to 30 minutes.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Primary haemorrhage; administer intravenous tranexamic acid and fresh frozen plasma in the emergency room immediately: Primary haemorrhage occurs during the operation itself, and systemic blood products are unnecessary in a healthy patient with normal platelets (235,000/µL) and INR (1.0) bleeding from a gingival margin.
  • C. Secondary haemorrhage; prescribe oral amoxicillin 500 mg and metronidazole 400 mg without disturbing the clot: Secondary haemorrhage occurs 7 to 10 days postoperatively due to bacterial fibrinolysis of the clot by secondary infection, not 4 hours after extraction.
  • D. Disseminated intravascular coagulation; cauterise the entire alveolar bone socket wall with monopolar diathermy: His normal platelet count and INR rule out coagulopathy, and extensive monopolar bone diathermy causes thermal osteonecrosis of the alveolar socket.
  • E. Arteriovenous malformation haemorrhage; ligate the ipsilateral external carotid artery in the neck urgently: Slow dark-red oozing from the gingival margin 4 hours postoperatively is capillary/venous mucosal bleeding after adrenaline washout, not high-flow arterial haemorrhage.

Key point: Classify reactionary post-extraction haemorrhage and describe the step-by-step local haemostatic protocol.

Question 2 · Infections, medically compromised patients, sedation and forensic dentistry · easy

A boy of unknown chronological age is brought by child protection services to a dental teaching hospital in Peshawar for forensic age estimation. Clinical examination shows a mixed dentition with erupted first permanent molars (16, 26, 36, and 46) and erupting mandibular second premolars (35 and 45). An orthopantomogram is taken to score tooth mineralisation stages from A to H across the seven permanent teeth of the left mandibular quadrant (31 to 37), excluding the third molar.

Which age estimation method is being used?

  1. A. Gustafson six-point histological scoring system
  2. B. Bangan and Ramm root dentine amino acid racemisation assay
  3. C. Demirjian eight-stage dental maturity method
  4. D. Lamendin single-rooted periodontosis and translucency formula
  5. E. Kvaal pulp-to-tooth radiographic area ratio
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Demirjian eight-stage dental maturity method

In children and adolescents, chronological age is most accurately estimated by assessing the radiographic stages of tooth crown and root mineralisation, which are under strong genetic control and are much less affected by nutritional deficiency or endocrine disturbances than either clinical tooth eruption or skeletal hand-wrist ossification. The Demirjian method (1973) is the most widely used international radiographic scoring system for sub-adult age estimation. Using an orthopantomogram (OPG), each of the seven permanent teeth in the mandibular left quadrant—from the central incisor (31) to the second molar (37)—is assigned one of eight defined developmental stages (letters A through H, from initial crypt calcification to closure of the root apex). Each stage carries a sex-specific biological maturity score, and the sum of the seven scores is converted to dental age using population reference tables.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Gustafson six-point histological scoring system: Gustafson scoring evaluates degenerative post-eruptive changes on ground sections of adult teeth rather than radiographic crown-root calcification in children.
  • B. Bangan and Ramm root dentine amino acid racemisation assay: Aspartic acid racemisation is a biochemical laboratory assay requiring extracted dentine rather than radiographic scoring of seven mandibular teeth.
  • D. Lamendin single-rooted periodontosis and translucency formula: The Lamendin method measures root height, periodontal recession, and apical translucency on extracted adult single-rooted teeth over age 25 years.
  • E. Kvaal pulp-to-tooth radiographic area ratio: The Kvaal method estimates adult age by measuring secondary dentine narrowing of the pulp chamber after root apices have completely closed.

Key point: Describe the Demirjian radiographic method of dental age estimation using the seven left mandibular permanent teeth in children.

Question 3 · Surgery for jaw pathology, TMJ, deformity and clefts · easy

An 11-year-old boy in Charsadda undergoes surgical release of right temporomandibular joint bony ankylosis. After excising a 1.5 cm segment of ankylotic bone and performing an ipsilateral coronoidectomy to achieve a 38 mm intraoperative mouth opening, the surgeon rotates an inferiorly based pedicled temporalis myofascial flap over the zygomatic arch to line the raw glenoid fossa and reconstructs the ramus-condyle unit with an autogenous 6th rib costochondral graft carrying a 2 to 3 mm cartilaginous cap.

Why are a temporalis flap and costochondral graft used instead of leaving a gap?

  1. A. Eliminate the need for any postoperative active jaw-opening physiotherapy exercises
  2. B. Immobilise the mandible in rigid intermaxillary fixation for eight weeks to promote fusion
  3. C. Protect the lingual nerve from being compressed against the medial pterygoid plate
  4. D. Denervate the masseter muscle permanently so that bite force cannot compress the joint
  5. E. Provide growth potential while preventing raw bone re-ankylosis and ramus shortening
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Provide growth potential while preventing raw bone re-ankylosis and ramus shortening

Simple gap arthroplasty—creating a 1.0 to 1.5 cm ostectomy space between the skull base and the mandibular ramus without interpositional tissue or condylar reconstruction—carries two major drawbacks: first, blood clot filling the empty space between raw bone surfaces readily ossifies, causing bony re-ankylosis; second, removing 1.5 cm of vertical ramus height shortens the posterior face, causing premature ipsilateral molar contact, chin deviation in unilateral cases, or an anterior open bite in bilateral cases. Interpositional arthroplasty places a biological barrier—most reliably the salvaged articular disc or a pedicled temporalis myofascial flap—between the raw bone ends to prevent re-fusion. In growing children, reconstructing the condyle-ramus unit with an autogenous costochondral rib graft (retaining a 2 to 3 mm cap of hyaline cartilage to avoid overgrowth) restores ramus height and supplies adaptive growth potential.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Eliminate the need for any postoperative active jaw-opening physiotherapy exercises: Aggressive, early postoperative jaw physiotherapy remains mandatory after every ankylosis operation regardless of whether an interpositional flap or costochondral graft is used.
  • B. Immobilise the mandible in rigid intermaxillary fixation for eight weeks to promote fusion: Prolonged intermaxillary fixation after ankylosis release causes rapid fibrous or bony re-ankylosis; modern protocols use rigid fixation to start mobilisation within days.
  • C. Protect the lingual nerve from being compressed against the medial pterygoid plate: The lingual nerve lies inferiorly in the pterygomandibular space and is not compressed against the glenoid fossa after gap arthroplasty.
  • D. Denervate the masseter muscle permanently so that bite force cannot compress the joint: Temporalis myofascial flap rotation and costochondral grafting do not denervate the masseter muscle; preserving masticatory function is a core goal of reconstruction.

Key point: Explain how interpositional temporalis myofascial arthroplasty with costochondral grafting prevents re-ankylosis, maintains vertical ramus height, and provides growth potential in children.

Question 4 · Maxillofacial trauma · easy

A 4-year-old boy is brought to a hospital in Quetta after falling from a first-floor balcony. His Glasgow Coma Scale score is 15/15, pulse is 105/min, and blood pressure is 96/60 mmHg. Intraoral examination shows an intact primary dentition from the maxillary right second primary molar (55) to the mandibular right second primary molar (85), with unerupted permanent tooth buds occupying the mandible on radiography. While his midface and mandibular body are intact, computed tomography demonstrates a fracture of the supraorbital rim and frontal bone.

Why do young children have more frontal and fewer midface fractures than adults?

  1. A. The sinuses are fully pneumatised at birth
  2. B. The midface sutures fuse before the age of two
  3. C. The child's mandible has no condylar growth centre
  4. D. A large cranium and prominent forehead shield a small, elastic face
  5. E. The temporomandibular disc is calcified in early childhood
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. A large cranium and prominent forehead shield a small, elastic face

Paediatric facial fractures account for less than 10% of all maxillofacial fractures, and their anatomical distribution changes with craniofacial growth. At birth, the cranium-to-face volume ratio is approximately 8:1, decreasing to 4:1 by age 5 and 2:1 in adulthood. Because the neurocranium and forehead are disproportionately large and prominent in children under 5 years of age while the midface and mandible are small and retruded beneath the cranial vault, blunt impacts from falls strike the frontal bone and supraorbital rims first. In addition, the paediatric maxilla and mandible are protected by thick, vascular periosteum, a high cancellous-to-cortical bone ratio that favours greenstick bending rather than brittle fracture, a lack of paranasal sinus pneumatisation, prominent buccal fat pads, and a shock-absorbing effect from the primary and mixed dentition.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. The sinuses are fully pneumatised at birth: In a 4-year-old child, the frontal sinuses are not yet pneumatised and the maxillary sinuses are very small; sinus pneumatisation actually increases midfacial fractures in adults.
  • B. The midface sutures fuse before the age of two: Craniofacial sutures in young children are wide and fibrous rather than fused, allowing them to absorb mechanical energy without brittle fracture.
  • C. The child's mandible has no condylar growth centre: The paediatric mandibular condyle possesses a vascular, thin neck and active subcondylar cartilage, making the condyle the most frequently fractured mandibular site in children.
  • E. The temporomandibular disc is calcified in early childhood: The articular disc is composed of dense avascular fibrocartilage throughout life and never calcifies in healthy children.

Key point: Explain how the high cranium-to-face ratio, lack of sinus pneumatisation, and bone elasticity alter facial fracture patterns in young children.

Question 5 · Local anaesthesia, exodontia and minor oral surgery · medium

A 32-year-old woman presents to a private dental clinic in Abbottabad for extraction of a grossly carious lower right permanent second molar (47). During administration of a right inferior alveolar nerve block with a 27-gauge long needle, the dentist places the syringe barrel over the lower right permanent canine (43) and encounters firm bone resistance after advancing the needle only 7 mm through the mucosa. Aspiration is negative, but depositing solution here would fail to anaesthetise the tooth.

What is the most appropriate adjustment to guide the needle to the target sulcus?

  1. A. Deposit half a cartridge immediately at this depth to anaesthetise the mental nerve
  2. B. Force the needle tip firmly through the cortical plate toward the mandibular canal
  3. C. Withdraw the needle and reinsert 10 mm posterior to the pterygomandibular raphe
  4. D. Withdraw slightly, swing barrel over contralateral premolars, and advance
  5. E. Withdraw to 5 mm and swing the barrel further toward the central incisors
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. Withdraw slightly, swing barrel over contralateral premolars, and advance

During a standard inferior alveolar nerve block, average soft-tissue penetration depth to the mandibular sulcus just above the lingula is 20 to 25 mm (roughly two-thirds to three-quarters of a 32 mm long needle). When bone is contacted prematurely at less than 10 to 15 mm depth, the needle tip has struck the anterior border of the ramus (internal oblique ridge) because the syringe barrel was positioned too far anteriorly over the ipsilateral teeth rather than the contralateral premolars. To correct this, the clinician should not withdraw the needle completely from the tissues; instead, withdraw the tip slightly, temporarily swing the barrel anteriorly so the tip clears the internal oblique ridge, then reposition the barrel over the contralateral premolars and advance to 20 to 25 mm.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Deposit half a cartridge immediately at this depth to anaesthetise the mental nerve: Bone contact at 7 mm on the medial aspect of the anterior ramus lies near the internal oblique ridge, far posterior and superior to the mental foramen.
  • B. Force the needle tip firmly through the cortical plate toward the mandibular canal: Forcing a 27-gauge needle against dense mandibular cortical bone risks bending or fracturing the needle at the hub and causes subperiosteal trauma without entering the pterygomandibular space.
  • C. Withdraw the needle and reinsert 10 mm posterior to the pterygomandibular raphe: Inserting medial or posterior to the pterygomandibular raphe pierces the superior pharyngeal constrictor or medial pterygoid muscle, causing post-injection trismus and pharyngeal anaesthesia.
  • E. Withdraw to 5 mm and swing the barrel further toward the central incisors: Swinging the barrel even further anteriorly toward the midline pushes the needle tip only transiently posteriorly before angling it straight back into the anterior ramus.

Key point: Correct premature bone contact on the internal oblique ridge during an inferior alveolar nerve block.

Question 6 · Infections, medically compromised patients, sedation and forensic dentistry · easy

A 29-year-old man presents to a dental teaching hospital in Peshawar with a 3-day history of rapidly worsening right facial swelling and throbbing pain from a grossly carious lower right first molar (46). His temperature is 38.4 °C (36.5-37.5), pulse is 98/min (60-100), and blood pressure is 126/80 mmHg. Extraoral examination reveals a diffuse, warm, erythematous, board-like swelling over the right cheek and submandibular region with poorly defined borders, severe tenderness on palpation, and complete absence of central fluctuation or purulent discharge.

Which clinical stage of odontogenic infection best describes this presentation?

  1. A. Cellulitis (spreading) stage
  2. B. Inoculation stage
  3. C. Mature abscess stage
  4. D. Post-drainage resolution stage
  5. E. Chronic draining fistula stage
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Cellulitis (spreading) stage

Odontogenic infections progress through four classical clinical stages as they spread beyond the tooth apex into adjacent periapical and fascial tissues. During the first 1 to 3 days (inoculation or oedema stage), invading bacteria provoke a mild, soft, doughy interstitial swelling. Between days 2 and 5, proliferating facultative aerobic streptococci release hyaluronidase and streptokinase, breaking down connective tissue barriers to produce the cellulitis stage, characterised by a diffuse, red, hot, severely painful, board-like indurated swelling with indistinct margins and no pus collection. By days 5 to 7, falling tissue oxygen tension allows obligate anaerobes to predominate, liquefying necrotic tissue and neutrophils into a walled-off, centrally fluctuant abscess stage, followed by resolution once drained.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Inoculation stage: The initial inoculation (oedema) stage occurs within the first 24 to 72 hours and presents with soft, doughy, mildly tender interstitial swelling rather than board-like induration.
  • C. Mature abscess stage: A mature abscess typically develops after day 5 and exhibits a well-circumscribed, centrally fluctuant swelling filled with purulent exudate rather than diffuse wooden induration.
  • D. Post-drainage resolution stage: Resolution occurs only after spontaneous rupture or surgical drainage of pus, with subsiding erythema, falling temperature, and softening of surrounding tissues.
  • E. Chronic draining fistula stage: A chronic draining sinus tract presents with a painless mucosal or cutaneous nodule discharging intermittent pus over weeks rather than acute toxic diffuse swelling.

Key point: Distinguish the clinical features of the acute cellulitis stage from the inoculation and abscess stages of an odontogenic infection.

Question 7 · Surgery for jaw pathology, TMJ, deformity and clefts · easy

A 6-week-old boy born with a complete unilateral left cleft lip and palate is brought by his parents from Kohat to a cleft centre in Peshawar for surgical planning. He is feeding well with a squeeze bottle and specialized cleft teat. His current weight is 3.8 kg (8.4 lb), haemoglobin is 9.2 g/dL (normal infant reference 10.0–14.0 g/dL), and total leukocyte count is 8,400/µL (5,000–15,000/µL). The surgeon explains the classic 'Rule of 10s' (Wilhelmsen and Musgrave criteria) used to determine safe readiness for primary cheilorrhaphy under general anaesthesia.

What is the 'rule of 10s' for timing cleft lip repair?

  1. A. Age 10 days, weight 10 kg, and platelet count above 10,000/µL
  2. B. Age 10 months, haemoglobin 10 g/dL, and serum albumin 10 g/dL
  3. C. Age 10 weeks, weight 10 pounds, and haemoglobin at least 10 g/dL
  4. D. Age 10 weeks, fasting blood glucose 10 mg/dL, and international normalised ratio 10
  5. E. Age 10 years, overjet 10 mm, and 10 erupted permanent teeth
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Age 10 weeks, weight 10 pounds, and haemoglobin at least 10 g/dL

Primary surgical repair of a cleft lip (cheilorrhaphy, using techniques such as the Millard rotation-advancement or Tennison-Randall triangular flap) restores orbicularis oris sphincter continuity, improves nasal alar symmetry, and aids parental bonding. Historically and clinically in many South Asian centres, safe readiness for general anaesthesia and elective lip repair is guided by Wilhelmsen and Musgrave's 'Rule of 10s': age at least 10 weeks (approximately 3 months), weight at least 10 pounds (approximately 4.5 kg), haemoglobin concentration at least 10 g/dL, and total leukocyte count below 10,000/µL to rule out occult respiratory or middle-ear infection. At 10 to 12 weeks of age, the infant has passed the nadir of physiological neonatal anaemia, anatomical landmarks of Cupid's bow and the philtral columns are larger for precise micro-approximation, and pulmonary maturation reduces postoperative apnoea risk.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Age 10 days, weight 10 kg, and platelet count above 10,000/µL: A neonate never weighs 10 kg at 10 days of life, and a platelet count of 10,000/µL represents life-threatening severe thrombocytopenia.
  • B. Age 10 months, haemoglobin 10 g/dL, and serum albumin 10 g/dL: Waiting until 10 months is timing for palatoplasty rather than lip repair, and a serum albumin of 10 g/dL is physiologically impossible.
  • D. Age 10 weeks, fasting blood glucose 10 mg/dL, and international normalised ratio 10: A blood glucose of 10 mg/dL is fatal hypoglycaemia and an INR of 10 causes catastrophic coagulopathy.
  • E. Age 10 years, overjet 10 mm, and 10 erupted permanent teeth: Ten years of age corresponds to the mixed-dentition window for secondary alveolar bone grafting, not infant primary cleft lip repair.

Key point: Apply the classic Rule of 10s (age ≥ 10 weeks, weight ≥ 10 lb, haemoglobin ≥ 10 g/dL, leukocyte count < 10,000/µL) when scheduling primary cleft lip repair.

Question 8 · Maxillofacial trauma · medium

A 31-year-old man is brought to the emergency department in Kohat after being struck across the lower jaw with a heavy iron rod. He has bilateral fractures of the mandibular parasymphysis running through the sockets of the left and right mandibular canines (33 and 43), creating a mobile anterior mandibular segment. When placed supine, he immediately develops inspiratory stridor, suprasternal recession, and a fall in oxygen saturation to 87% (normal 95-100%), which improves when he leans forward.

Why does his airway obstruct when supine, and how is it relieved?

  1. A. Laryngeal cartilage fracture requiring immediate intravenous hydrocortisone
  2. B. Bilateral tension pneumothorax requiring needle decompression in the fifth intercostal space
  3. C. Superior displacement of the hard palate blocking the nasopharynx, corrected by nasal airway tubes
  4. D. Posterior tongue prolapse, relieved by anterior traction on the tongue and symphysis
  5. E. Spasm of the lateral pterygoid muscles pulling both condylar heads downward into the glottic inlet
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. Posterior tongue prolapse, relieved by anterior traction on the tongue and symphysis

A bilateral fracture of the mandibular parasymphysis (in the region of the left and right mandibular canines, 33 and 43) is classically termed a 'bucket-handle' or 'Andy Gump' fracture. The genioglossus muscles—the primary protruders of the tongue—originate from the superior genial tubercles on the lingual aspect of the mandibular symphysis. When bilateral parasymphyseal fractures detach this central symphyseal segment, the unopposed pull of the genioglossus, geniohyoid, mylohyoid, and anterior bellies of the digastric muscles draws the mobile bony fragment and the base of the tongue posteriorly. In a supine patient, the tongue falls backward and occludes the oropharynx against the posterior pharyngeal wall. Immediate bedside relief is achieved by pulling the tongue and anterior mandibular segment forward manually, with a heavy traction suture placed through the dorsum of the tongue, or by temporary bridle wiring around stable adjacent teeth.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Laryngeal cartilage fracture requiring immediate intravenous hydrocortisone: Positional obstruction relieved immediately by leaning forward results from tongue prolapse rather than fixed intrinsic laryngeal cartilage collapse.
  • B. Bilateral tension pneumothorax requiring needle decompression in the fifth intercostal space: Tension pneumothorax causes absent unilateral breath sounds, tracheal deviation, and hypotension that do not resolve when sitting forward.
  • C. Superior displacement of the hard palate blocking the nasopharynx, corrected by nasal airway tubes: A bilateral parasymphyseal mandibular fracture involves the lower jaw rather than the hard palate, and nasopharyngeal tubes do not advance a prolapsed anterior mandible.
  • E. Spasm of the lateral pterygoid muscles pulling both condylar heads downward into the glottic inlet: The lateral pterygoid muscles insert into the condylar necks near the skull base and have no anatomical connection to the glottic inlet.

Key point: Explain the anatomical basis of acute supine upper airway obstruction in bilateral parasymphyseal mandibular fractures and its immediate bedside relief.

Question 9 · Local anaesthesia, exodontia and minor oral surgery · easy

A 26-year-old man attends a dental teaching hospital in Peshawar for root canal therapy of a symptomatic lower left permanent first molar (36). His blood pressure is 122/78 mmHg and pulse is 74/min. Before administering a standard direct inferior alveolar nerve block using a 27-gauge long needle, the clinician palpates the mucobuccal fold and the anterior border of the left mandibular ramus with the thumb to locate the point of greatest concavity.

Where should the syringe barrel and insertion point be?

  1. A. Barrel over ipsilateral incisors, entering at the mandibular occlusal plane level
  2. B. Barrel over contralateral premolars, 6 to 10 mm above the occlusal plane
  3. C. Barrel parallel to the ipsilateral molars at the maxillary mucogingival junction
  4. D. Barrel over the contralateral canine, aiming at the neck of the condyle
  5. E. Barrel perpendicular to the buccal alveolus opposite the lower first molar
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Barrel over contralateral premolars, 6 to 10 mm above the occlusal plane

A successful direct (Halsted) inferior alveolar nerve block relies on three consistent anatomical landmarks: the coronoid notch (deepest concavity on the anterior border of the mandibular ramus), the pterygomandibular raphe (formed by the buccinator and superior pharyngeal constrictor muscles), and the occlusal plane of the mandibular posterior teeth. The syringe barrel is placed over the contralateral premolars and the corner of the mouth. The needle is inserted into the mucosa on the medial surface of the ramus, lateral to the pterygomandibular raphe, at a height 6 to 10 mm above the mandibular occlusal plane (corresponding to the midpoint of the palpating thumbnail in the coronoid notch) and advanced 20 to 25 mm until gentle bone contact just superior to the lingula.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Barrel over ipsilateral incisors, entering at the mandibular occlusal plane level: Positioning the barrel over ipsilateral incisors angles the needle too far posteriorly, and entering at the occlusal plane deposits solution too low near the lingula insertion.
  • C. Barrel parallel to the ipsilateral molars at the maxillary mucogingival junction: Aligning the barrel parallel to the maxillary arch at the mucogingival junction describes the closed-mouth Vazirani-Akinosi block rather than a direct inferior alveolar nerve block.
  • D. Barrel over the contralateral canine, aiming at the neck of the condyle: Aiming from the contralateral canine toward the lateral aspect of the condylar neck at the level of the upper second molar describes the Gow-Gates mandibular block.
  • E. Barrel perpendicular to the buccal alveolus opposite the lower first molar: Perpendicular insertion in the buccal vestibule opposite 36 only infiltrates the long buccal nerve and fails to penetrate the thick mandibular cortex to reach the mandibular foramen.

Key point: Identify the intraoral landmarks and syringe orientation required for a standard direct inferior alveolar nerve block.

Question 10 · Infections, medically compromised patients, sedation and forensic dentistry · easy

A 24-year-old man presents to a dental teaching hospital in Mardan with a 4-day history of painful swelling in the right side of the neck following recurrent pericoronitis and deep caries in an impacted lower right third molar (48). Extraoral examination reveals a firm, tender swelling filling the right submandibular triangle below the inferior border of the mandible, whereas the anterior floor of the mouth is completely soft and non-elevated. A periapical radiograph shows a periapical radiolucency around the root apices of 48.

Which landmark decides whether lingual perforation enters the submandibular or the sublingual space?

  1. A. Anterior belly of the digastric muscle
  2. B. Hyoglossus muscle insertion on the hyoid
  3. C. Attachment line of the mylohyoid muscle
  4. D. Superior constrictor muscle of the pharynx
  5. E. Stylomandibular ligament at the angle
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Attachment line of the mylohyoid muscle

The mylohyoid muscle acts as the muscular diaphragm of the floor of the mouth and serves as the key anatomical partition between the sublingual space superiorly and the submandibular space inferiorly. Its origin along the internal oblique (mylohyoid) line of the mandible slopes obliquely upwards as it travels posteriorly. Consequently, the root apices of the mandibular incisors, canines, premolars, and usually the first molar (36/46) lie superior to the mylohyoid attachment, so lingual plate perforation from these teeth discharges pus into the sublingual space, elevating the floor of the mouth. Conversely, the root apices of the mandibular third molar (38/48) and usually the second molar (37/47) lie inferior to the mylohyoid line, directing infection straight into the submandibular space.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Anterior belly of the digastric muscle: The anterior belly of the digastric muscle bounds the submental triangle laterally rather than separating the superior sublingual compartment from the inferior submandibular space.
  • B. Hyoglossus muscle insertion on the hyoid: The hyoglossus muscle forms the medial wall of both the sublingual and submandibular spaces rather than creating the horizontal shelf between them.
  • D. Superior constrictor muscle of the pharynx: The superior pharyngeal constrictor lies posteromedially in the pharyngeal wall and forms the medial boundary of the parapharyngeal space rather than dividing floor-of-mouth spaces.
  • E. Stylomandibular ligament at the angle: The stylomandibular ligament separates the submandibular gland from the parotid gland posteriorly rather than partitioning the sublingual and submandibular fascial spaces.

Key point: Explain how the level of the mylohyoid muscle attachment relative to mandibular root apices governs spread into the sublingual versus submandibular space.
