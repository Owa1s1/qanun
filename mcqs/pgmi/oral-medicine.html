Question 1 · Principles of oral medicine: assessment, investigations and therapy · medium

A 55-year-old man who uses naswar daily attends a dental teaching hospital in Peshawar with a persistent white and red patch on the left buccal mucosa for four months. The patch is not painful and does not rub off. Toluidine blue application shows intense blue staining of the lesion.

What is the most appropriate next step?

  1. A. Treat it as squamous cell carcinoma without biopsy
  2. B. Reassure and review in one year
  3. C. Topical antifungal treatment
  4. D. Incisional biopsy of the stained area
  5. E. Aciclovir tablets
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. Incisional biopsy of the stained area

Toluidine blue is a vital stain that binds to nucleic acids and highlights areas of high nuclear density, such as dysplasia and squamous cell carcinoma. A positive stain does not prove cancer; it identifies a high-risk site that should be biopsied for histological confirmation. It is an adjunct to clinical examination, not a replacement for scalpel biopsy, which remains the gold standard. In Pakistan, naswar and gutka use markedly increase oral cancer risk, so any non-healing white and red patch in a tobacco user warrants urgent assessment. False positives can occur in inflammation and trauma, so clinical correlation is essential.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Treat it as squamous cell carcinoma without biopsy: Toluidine blue is an adjunct; a positive stain never replaces biopsy for a diagnosis of cancer.
  • B. Reassure and review in one year: Inflammation may stain, but intense staining in a persistent patch in a tobacco user must be biopsied.
  • C. Topical antifungal treatment: Candida forms removable plaques and is not diagnosed by toluidine blue.
  • E. Aciclovir tablets: Herpetic lesions are vesicles and ulcers with a short course; they are not detected by this stain.

Key point: To understand that toluidine blue staining marks a high-risk area needing biopsy, not a cancer diagnosis.

Question 2 · Diseases of the oral mucosa, tongue and lips · easy

A 25-year-old man attends a dental clinic in Islamabad with painful mouth ulcers and a skin rash for three days. He had a sore throat one week ago and feels feverish. Examination shows typical target lesions with dark centres on his palms and soles, together with multiple erosions on his lips and buccal mucosa.

What is the most likely diagnosis?

  1. A. Herpes simplex primary infection
  2. B. Pemphigus vulgaris
  3. C. Erythema multiforme
  4. D. Oral lichen planus
  5. E. Acute necrotising ulcerative gingivitis
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Erythema multiforme

Erythema multiforme is an acute immune-mediated reaction, often triggered by herpes simplex virus or occasionally drugs, presenting with sudden target (iris) lesions on the extremities and mucosal erosions. The oral lesions can be painful and may affect the lips. It is usually self-limiting over two to four weeks, but severe forms with extensive mucosal detachment require urgent medical care. Management is supportive with analgesics, antiseptic mouthwash and treatment of any trigger; systemic corticosteroids are controversial and reserved for severe cases.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Herpes simplex primary infection: Primary herpes simplex causes grouped vesicles and gingivostomatitis, not target lesions on the palms and soles.
  • B. Pemphigus vulgaris: Pemphigus vulgaris causes flaccid bullae that rupture easily, without the sudden target-skin rash seen here.
  • D. Oral lichen planus: Oral lichen planus shows white lacy striae, usually bilateral, and does not produce acute target skin lesions.
  • E. Acute necrotising ulcerative gingivitis: Acute necrotising ulcerative gingivitis causes painful bleeding gums with a grey membrane, not skin target lesions.

Key point: Diagnose erythema multiforme from target lesions on the palms and soles with mucosal erosions.

Question 3 · Orofacial pain, the temporomandibular joint and facial palsy · easy

A 62-year-old woman attends a dental teaching hospital in D.I. Khan with sharp stabbing pain in the right throat and ear, triggered by swallowing and talking, lasting seconds, for six weeks. Pain radiates to the angle of the jaw. There is no rash or fever.

What is the most likely diagnosis?

  1. A. Trigeminal neuralgia
  2. B. Glossopharyngeal neuralgia
  3. C. Tonsillitis
  4. D. Otitis externa
  5. E. Cardiac pain
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Glossopharyngeal neuralgia

Glossopharyngeal neuralgia causes brief, severe, stabbing pain in the tonsillar fossa, pharynx, or ear, triggered by swallowing, talking, coughing, or yawning. It is less common than trigeminal neuralgia and may be associated with bradycardia or syncope. Carbamazepine is also first-line. Differential diagnosis includes trigeminal neuralgia, tonsillitis, otitis, and referred cardiac pain, but the trigger and site point to the glossopharyngeal nerve.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Trigeminal neuralgia: Trigeminal neuralgia affects the face, not the throat and ear, and is triggered by chewing or touch.
  • C. Tonsillitis: Tonsillitis causes continuous sore throat with fever and exudates, not brief swallowing-triggered stabs.
  • D. Otitis externa: Otitis externa causes ear pain with ear discharge and tenderness of the pinna, not throat pain.
  • E. Cardiac pain: Cardiac pain is exertional, retrosternal, and not triggered by swallowing or talking.

Key point: To diagnose glossopharyngeal neuralgia from stabbing throat and ear pain triggered by swallowing.

Question 4 · Salivary glands, dry mouth and neck swellings · easy

A 25-year-old woman attends a private dental clinic in Hyderabad. She has a recurrent swelling of the lower lip that appears after she bites it. The swelling is bluish and translucent, comes and goes, and is painless unless traumatised.

What is the most appropriate management?

  1. A. Observation only
  2. B. Marsupialisation
  3. C. Topical corticosteroid
  4. D. Antibiotic therapy
  5. E. Surgical excision
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Surgical excision

A mucocele is a mucus extravasation cyst of a minor salivary gland, most often on the lower lip after trauma. It appears as a bluish, translucent, fluctuant swelling that may rupture and recur. Definitive treatment is surgical excision including the underlying minor salivary glands to prevent recurrence. Marsupialisation is more suitable for ranulas. Observation, steroids and antibiotics do not address the source. Recurrence is common if the gland is left behind.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Observation only: Observation may be acceptable for tiny lesions, but this recurrent symptomatic mucocele needs definitive treatment.
  • B. Marsupialisation: Marsupialisation is mainly used for ranulas in the floor of the mouth, not a lower lip mucocele.
  • C. Topical corticosteroid: Topical steroids do not remove the minor salivary gland source and are not definitive.
  • D. Antibiotic therapy: There is no sign of infection, so antibiotics have no role in a mucocele.

Key point: To manage a recurrent lower lip mucocele by surgical excision with the minor salivary glands.

Question 5 · Oral medicine in systemic disease · medium

A 22-year-old woman attends a dental teaching hospital in Hyderabad with prolonged bleeding after dental scaling and easy bruising since adolescence. Her mother and sister have similar symptoms. Examination shows petechiae on the forearm and no joint swelling. Blood test: platelet count 180,000/µL (150,000-400,000), prothrombin time 12 seconds (11-13.5), activated partial thromboplastin time 30 seconds (25-35).

Which investigation should be done first?

  1. A. Platelet function analyser (PFA-100)
  2. B. Bleeding time
  3. C. Factor VIII assay alone
  4. D. VWF antigen and activity with factor VIII
  5. E. Bone marrow biopsy
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. VWF antigen and activity with factor VIII

Mucocutaneous bleeding with normal platelets, PT and APTT, and an affected mother and sister, points to von Willebrand disease, the commonest inherited bleeding disorder. Current guidance (ASH/ISTH/NHF/WFH 2021) is to test VWF antigen, VWF platelet-binding activity and factor VIII together as the first step. The PFA-100 is a quick screen but can be normal in mild disease, so a normal result does not exclude it. Bleeding time is obsolete. Avoid aspirin and NSAIDs until the diagnosis is made.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Platelet function analyser (PFA-100): A quick screen for primary haemostasis, but it can be normal in mild von Willebrand disease, so it is not the recommended first test.
  • B. Bleeding time: Largely abandoned because of poor reproducibility; replaced by platelet function analysers.
  • C. Factor VIII assay alone: Factor VIII may be low in von Willebrand disease, but alone it misses most cases.
  • E. Bone marrow biopsy: Not indicated; the platelet count is normal and the problem is qualitative.

Key point: Select platelet function analyser as the first test for suspected von Willebrand disease.

Question 6 · Oral radiology · medium

A 35-year-old man attends a dental clinic in Islamabad for implant planning. A panoramic radiograph shows a broad radiolucent band across the upper jaw above the roots of the maxillary teeth. The mandible and condyles are displayed clearly. The patient did not swallow or move during exposure.

Which positioning error most likely caused this appearance?

  1. A. Chin positioned too low
  2. B. Chin positioned too high
  3. C. Tongue not placed against the palate
  4. D. Patient turned slightly to one side
  5. E. Lead apron not placed correctly
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Tongue not placed against the palate

On a panoramic radiograph, the patient must press the tongue against the palate to eliminate the air space above the maxillary teeth. If the tongue rests on the floor of the mouth, that air space is recorded as a broad radiolucent band over the roots of the upper teeth. The mandible and condyles may remain well displayed because the error is confined to the maxillary region. Other positioning errors produce different appearances: a low chin exaggerates the smile curve, a high chin flattens the occlusal plane, and head rotation asymmetrically magnifies one side. Correct positioning prevents these artefacts from being mistaken for pathology.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Chin positioned too low: A low chin exaggerates the smile curve and may cut off the condyles, which are clear here.
  • B. Chin positioned too high: A high chin flattens the occlusal plane and blurs the anterior teeth, not a band above the roots.
  • D. Patient turned slightly to one side: Head rotation makes one side of the jaw larger and the other smaller, not a central radiolucent band.
  • E. Lead apron not placed correctly: An apron error would obscure the lower neck or chin, not create a band across the maxilla.

Key point: Recognise a tongue-not-against-palate error by its radiolucent band across the maxilla on a panoramic image.

Question 7 · Principles of oral medicine: assessment, investigations and therapy · easy

A 60-year-old woman attends a dental teaching hospital in Abbottabad complaining of a dry mouth for six months. She needs to sip water while speaking and wakes at night to drink. She has no nasal obstruction. Her medical file lists several long-term drugs. Intraoral examination shows little pooled saliva and the mirror sticks to the buccal mucosa.

Which part of the history is most important to clarify next?

  1. A. Her daily water intake
  2. B. How often she brushes her teeth
  3. C. Whether she wears a denture at night
  4. D. Her family history of autoimmune disease
  5. E. Her current medicines, including herbal ones
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Her current medicines, including herbal ones

Xerostomia is most commonly caused by medicines in older adults, especially anticholinergics, antihistamines, antihypertensives, antidepressants and diuretics. A careful drug history, including over-the-counter and herbal preparations, should be taken before investigating systemic disease. In Pakistani practice, patients often bring only prescription medicines and forget traditional remedies, so the dentist must ask specifically. Management begins with stopping or substituting the offending drug if possible, alongside saliva substitutes and frequent sips of water.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Her daily water intake: Fluid intake does not explain reduced salivary flow; this patient already sips water frequently.
  • B. How often she brushes her teeth: Oral hygiene frequency is relevant to caries risk but not to the cause of dry mouth.
  • C. Whether she wears a denture at night: Denture wear can cause soreness but does not cause objective salivary reduction.
  • D. Her family history of autoimmune disease: Family history does not cause dry mouth; this history points to medicines or local disease.

Key point: Identify medication history as the key step in evaluating dry mouth in an older adult.

Question 8 · Diseases of the oral mucosa, tongue and lips · medium

A 55-year-old woman is seen at a hospital in Swat with a white and red patch on the floor of her mouth for five months. She uses naswar daily. The patch is fixed to the underlying tissue and bleeds on touch. An OPG shows no bony involvement. A cervical lymph node on the left is 1.5 cm and firm.

What is the most appropriate next step?

  1. A. Prescribe nystatin mouth paint for two weeks
  2. B. Perform an incisional biopsy of the patch
  3. C. Review the patient in three months
  4. D. Prescribe a short course of amoxicillin
  5. E. Refer for routine dental scaling and polishing
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Perform an incisional biopsy of the patch

A red-and-white patch that is fixed and bleeds in a tobacco user is suspicious for malignant change and should not be treated empirically. Incisional biopsy of the most suspicious area provides histology to grade dysplasia or detect carcinoma. Naswar and smoking are major risk factors in Pakistan. Observation, antifungals or antibiotics delay diagnosis and are inappropriate when red and white features with bleeding are present.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Prescribe nystatin mouth paint for two weeks: Prescribe nystatin mouth paint for two weeks - nystatin treats candidosis, but this fixed bleeding red-and-white patch in a naswar user needs histology first.
  • C. Review the patient in three months: Review the patient in three months - a suspicious non-healing patch with bleeding should not be observed without biopsy.
  • D. Prescribe a short course of amoxicillin: Prescribe a short course of amoxicillin - antibiotics treat infection, not a potentially malignant mucosal lesion.
  • E. Refer for routine dental scaling and polishing: Refer for routine dental scaling and polishing - scaling does not address a suspicious premalignant patch.

Key point: Arrange urgent incisional biopsy for a suspicious red-and-white oral patch.

Question 9 · Orofacial pain, the temporomandibular joint and facial palsy · easy

A 28-year-old woman attends a dental teaching hospital in Peshawar with dull aching pain in right preauricular area and temple, worse on waking and during chewing, for two months. She has no joint locking. Examination shows tenderness over right masseter and temporalis, no joint clicking, and mouth opening 40 mm. There is no dental caries.

What is the most likely diagnosis?

  1. A. Internal derangement with disc displacement without reduction
  2. B. Myofascial pain
  3. C. Osteoarthritis of the temporomandibular joint
  4. D. Trigeminal neuralgia
  5. E. Giant cell arteritis
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Myofascial pain

Myofascial pain is the most common temporomandibular disorder. It causes a dull ache in the muscles of mastication, worse on waking, with muscle tenderness and no joint locking. Disc displacement without reduction gives limited opening and a click that suddenly stops. Osteoarthritis occurs in older patients with crepitus. Trigeminal neuralgia is brief and triggered. Initial care is reassurance, a soft diet, warm compresses, and a stabilization splint if needed. Stress-related clenching is common.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Internal derangement with disc displacement without reduction: This patient has no limited opening or locking, which would suggest disc displacement without reduction.
  • C. Osteoarthritis of the temporomandibular joint: Osteoarthritis usually occurs in older patients and shows crepitus on joint movement, absent here.
  • D. Trigeminal neuralgia: Trigeminal neuralgia causes brief electric-shock pain triggered by touch, not a dull ache on waking.
  • E. Giant cell arteritis: Giant cell arteritis affects older adults and causes scalp tenderness with jaw claudication, not seen here.

Key point: To diagnose myofascial temporomandibular pain from waking muscle pain and tenderness without joint locking.

Question 10 · Salivary glands, dry mouth and neck swellings · easy

A 58-year-old man attends a dental teaching hospital in Peshawar complaining of a dry mouth and difficulty swallowing dry food for three months. He takes amlodipine 5 mg once daily, amitriptyline 25 mg at night for neuropathic pain, atorvastatin 40 mg at night, metformin 500 mg twice daily and paracetamol as needed. He has never smoked or used naswar. Examination shows dry, mirrored oral mucosa and pooled saliva is absent.

Which drug is the most likely cause of his xerostomia?

  1. A. Amlodipine
  2. B. Amitriptyline
  3. C. Atorvastatin
  4. D. Metformin
  5. E. Paracetamol
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Amitriptyline

Tricyclic antidepressants such as amitriptyline block muscarinic receptors and reduce salivary secretion. Amlodipine is associated with gingival enlargement rather than xerostomia, and statins are not significant causes. Naswar is absent from his history. Management includes reviewing the medication with the prescriber, sips of water, saliva substitutes, and strict fluoride caries prevention because dry mouth markedly increases root caries risk. Saliva substitutes such as carboxymethylcellulose sprays can moisten the mucosa, while frequent water sips help with speaking and swallowing. Patients should avoid sugary drinks because the lack of saliva allows rapid caries progression.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Amlodipine: Calcium channel blockers more often cause gingival hyperplasia; his dry mirrored mucosa and absent pooled saliva fit an anticholinergic effect.
  • C. Atorvastatin: Statins are not established causes of salivary hypofunction; his medication list points to a different culprit.
  • D. Metformin: Metformin causes gut upset and a metallic taste, not a dry mouth.
  • E. Paracetamol: Paracetamol has no antimuscarinic action and does not reduce saliva.

Key point: Identify the drug most likely to cause xerostomia.
