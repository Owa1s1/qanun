Question 1 · Cell injury, adaptation and death · easy

A 60-year-old shopkeeper from Multan dies in a road accident. His wife says he was told he had high blood pressure 15 years ago but never took the tablets, and he had become breathless on stairs over the last year. At autopsy the heart weighs 520 g and the left ventricular wall is 2 cm thick, with no valve disease and no coronary narrowing. Microscopy shows myocytes that are larger, with big, square nuclei, but are not more numerous.

Which adaptation is this?

  1. A. Hyperplasia
  2. B. Metaplasia
  3. C. Dysplasia
  4. D. Hypertrophy
  5. E. Atrophy
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. Hypertrophy

Hypertrophy is an increase in cell size from more structural proteins and organelles; hyperplasia is an increase in cell number. Permanent cells (cardiac muscle, skeletal muscle, neurons) can only enlarge, so pressure overload from hypertension produces concentric left ventricular hypertrophy, driven by mechanical stretch and growth factors (angiotensin II, IGF-1), with a switch to fetal gene programmes (beta-myosin heavy chain, ANP). Tissues that can divide (uterus in pregnancy, prostate, endometrium) often show both. Beyond a limit, hypertrophied hearts outgrow their blood supply, become stiff, and fail.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Hyperplasia: Hyperplasia means more cells; adult cardiac myocytes barely divide, and his myocytes are larger, not more numerous.
  • B. Metaplasia: Metaplasia replaces one cell type with another, as in a smoker's bronchus; his myocytes are still myocytes.
  • C. Dysplasia: Dysplasia shows disordered growth with atypical nuclei, a precancerous change, not uniform enlargement.
  • E. Atrophy: Atrophy shrinks cells, as in disuse or poor blood supply; his heart weighs 520 g with a 2 cm wall.

Key point: Hypertrophy is larger cells (the only option for permanent cells such as myocardium); hyperplasia is more cells.

Question 2 · Systemic pathology: nervous system and special senses · medium

A 4-month-old girl is brought because she is very floppy and cannot hold her head up, though she was well at birth. She breathes with her abdomen, her chest is bell-shaped, and she has fasciculations of the tongue. She is alert, smiles and follows faces well. Reflexes are absent. Her parents are first cousins.

What is lost and which gene is involved?

  1. A. Anterior horn cells; SMN1
  2. B. Muscle dystrophin; DMD
  3. C. Upper motor neurons; SOD1
  4. D. Neuromuscular junction; botulinum toxin
  5. E. Cerebellum; frataxin
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Anterior horn cells; SMN1

Spinal muscular atrophy: autosomal recessive deletion of SMN1, causing degeneration of anterior horn cells (lower motor neuron disease) with symmetrical flaccid weakness, hypotonia, tongue fasciculations, absent reflexes and preserved intellect. Type 1 (Werdnig-Hoffmann) presents in early infancy and was fatal by 2 years before new treatments (nusinersen, gene therapy). Other floppy infants: congenital myopathies, myotonic dystrophy (maternal transmission), Prader-Willi, hypothyroidism, infant botulism (honey), and central causes such as hypoxic injury.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Muscle dystrophin; DMD: Duchenne dystrophy presents in boys at 3-5 years with calf enlargement, not a floppy 4-month-old girl.
  • C. Upper motor neurons; SOD1: Upper motor neuron loss causes spasticity and brisk reflexes; she is floppy with absent reflexes.
  • D. Neuromuscular junction; botulinum toxin: Infant botulism follows honey with constipation, ptosis and poor suck, not tongue fasciculations.
  • E. Cerebellum; frataxin: Friedreich ataxia starts in later childhood with ataxia, not floppiness and tongue fasciculations in infancy.

Key point: Spinal muscular atrophy (SMN1) destroys anterior horn cells, causing a floppy alert infant with tongue fasciculations.

Question 3 · Systemic pathology: cardiovascular system · medium

A 10-year-old girl in a village near Peshawar had a sore throat three weeks ago that was not treated. She now has fever and painful swelling that moves from her knees to her ankles and wrists over days. She has a new soft apical pansystolic murmur and a pink rash with raised edges on her trunk. ASO titre is 800 IU/mL, and ESR is 80 mm/h. ECG shows a prolonged PR interval.

What would heart biopsy most likely show?

  1. A. Caseating granulomas
  2. B. Myocyte disarray
  3. C. Aschoff bodies
  4. D. Vegetations full of bacteria
  5. E. Eosinophilic infiltrates
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Aschoff bodies

Acute rheumatic fever follows group A streptococcal pharyngitis (2-4 weeks later) by molecular mimicry (antibodies and T cells against M protein cross-react with heart, joint and brain antigens). Jones criteria: major (carditis, migratory polyarthritis, Sydenham chorea, erythema marginatum, subcutaneous nodules) and minor (fever, arthralgia, raised ESR or CRP, prolonged PR), with evidence of recent infection (ASO, anti-DNase B). Carditis is pancarditis: Aschoff bodies (fibrinoid change, lymphocytes, Anitschkow "caterpillar" cells and Aschoff giant cells), fibrinous pericarditis and verrucous vegetations. Chronic rheumatic heart disease follows repeated episodes: mitral stenosis (fish-mouth valve, commissural fusion), with mitral regurgitation and aortic involvement. It remains common in Pakistan; secondary penicillin prophylaxis prevents recurrence.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Caseating granulomas: Caseating granulomas are seen in tuberculosis, not after a streptococcal sore throat.
  • B. Myocyte disarray: Myocyte disarray is hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, an inherited disease without fever or arthritis.
  • D. Vegetations full of bacteria: Bacterial vegetations come with infective endocarditis and positive blood cultures.
  • E. Eosinophilic infiltrates: Eosinophilic infiltrates occur in drug hypersensitivity myocarditis.

Key point: Acute rheumatic carditis shows Aschoff bodies; repeated attacks cause mitral stenosis with a fish-mouth valve.

Question 4 · Systemic pathology: endocrine system · medium

A 45-year-old man comes because his wedding ring no longer fits and his shoes have grown by two sizes over a few years. His wife says his face has changed. He has coarse facial features, a protruding jaw with gaps between his teeth, large sweaty hands, and new diabetes. He snores loudly and has high blood pressure. IGF-1 is 720 ng/mL (normal 90-250) and growth hormone does not fall after an oral glucose load.

What is the cause and the main cause of death if untreated?

  1. A. Prolactinoma; infertility
  2. B. Corticotroph adenoma; infection
  3. C. GH adenoma; heart disease
  4. D. Hypothyroidism; heart failure
  5. E. Gigantism; fracture
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. GH adenoma; heart disease

Pituitary adenomas: prolactinoma (commonest; galactorrhoea, amenorrhoea, low libido; dopamine agonists), somatotroph (gigantism in children, acromegaly in adults: enlarged hands, feet, jaw, tongue, coarse features, sweating, carpal tunnel, diabetes, hypertension, cardiomyopathy, colon polyps; often GNAS mutations; diagnosis by raised IGF-1 and failure of GH suppression by glucose; surgery, somatostatin analogues, pegvisomant), corticotroph (Cushing disease), gonadotroph and non-functioning adenomas (mass effect: bitemporal hemianopia from chiasm compression, headache, hypopituitarism). MEN1 includes pituitary adenomas.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Prolactinoma; infertility: Prolactin excess causes galactorrhoea and low libido, not a growing jaw, hands and feet.
  • B. Corticotroph adenoma; infection: A corticotroph adenoma causes Cushing disease with central obesity and purple striae.
  • D. Hypothyroidism; heart failure: Hypothyroidism coarsens features but does not enlarge bones or raise IGF-1.
  • E. Gigantism; fracture: Gigantism is GH excess before the growth plates fuse, causing tall stature in children.

Key point: Acromegaly: somatotroph adenoma with high IGF-1 and no GH suppression by glucose; heart disease shortens life.

Question 5 · Genetic, paediatric and environmental disease · medium

A previously healthy 3-month-old boy, born at 35 weeks, is found dead in his cot in the morning. He had been put to sleep face down on a soft mattress with a pillow, in a warm room. Both parents smoke at home. There were no signs of illness. Full autopsy, toxicology and a scene investigation find no cause.

Which is the most important preventable risk factor?

  1. A. Breastfeeding
  2. B. Sleeping prone
  3. C. Pacifier use
  4. D. Room sharing
  5. E. Routine immunisation
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Sleeping prone

Sudden infant death syndrome is sudden death of an infant under 1 year that remains unexplained after full investigation, autopsy and review of circumstances. Peak at 2-4 months. Risk factors: prone or side sleeping, soft bedding, bed sharing, overheating, maternal smoking in pregnancy and passive smoke, prematurity and low birth weight, male sex. A proposed vulnerability is abnormal brainstem serotonergic control of arousal and breathing. The "back to sleep" campaign greatly reduced deaths.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Breastfeeding: Breastfeeding protects against SIDS.
  • C. Pacifier use: Pacifier use may protect against SIDS.
  • D. Room sharing: Room sharing without bed sharing protects against SIDS.
  • E. Routine immunisation: Immunisation does not cause SIDS and may protect.

Key point: SIDS peaks at 2-4 months; prone sleeping, soft bedding and smoking are key preventable risks.

Question 6 · Systemic pathology: gastrointestinal tract · easy

A term newborn with Down syndrome has not passed meconium by 48 hours. He has a distended abdomen and green vomiting. Rectal examination produces an explosive release of stool and gas, and the abdomen goes down. A contrast enema shows a narrow rectum with dilated bowel above it. Rectal suction biopsy shows no ganglion cells and thick nerve trunks.

What is the cause?

  1. A. Cystic fibrosis with thick meconium
  2. B. Anal atresia
  3. C. Failed neural crest migration
  4. D. Hypothyroidism
  5. E. Pyloric stenosis
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Failed neural crest migration

Hirschsprung disease (congenital aganglionic megacolon): failure of neural crest cells to migrate to the distal bowel, so the rectum and a variable length of colon lack Meissner and Auerbach plexus ganglion cells and cannot relax. The proximal normal colon dilates. Linked to RET mutations and Down syndrome. Delayed meconium, constipation, abdominal distension, risk of enterocolitis. Diagnosis by rectal biopsy (absent ganglion cells, increased acetylcholinesterase nerve fibres); treated by resecting the aganglionic segment. Meconium ileus is typical of cystic fibrosis.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Cystic fibrosis with thick meconium: Meconium ileus in cystic fibrosis has normal ganglion cells.
  • B. Anal atresia: An anus is present, so this is not anal atresia.
  • D. Hypothyroidism: Hypothyroidism slows transit but leaves ganglion cells intact.
  • E. Pyloric stenosis: Pyloric stenosis causes vomiting after weeks, not failure to pass meconium.

Key point: Hirschsprung disease: absent ganglion cells in the distal colon from failed neural crest migration; linked to RET and Down syndrome.

Question 7 · Systemic pathology: haematology · medium

A 22-year-old engineering student has had bruises on his legs, bleeding gums when brushing and recurrent fevers for three weeks. He took no new medicines, and there was no recent jaundice. He is pale with petechiae on his shins; there are no enlarged lymph nodes and no splenomegaly. Haemoglobin 6.8 g/dL, white cells 1.2 x 10^9/L (neutrophils 0.3), platelets 12 x 10^9/L, reticulocytes 0.1%. The film shows no blasts. Marrow trephine shows less than 10% cellularity, mostly fat, with no abnormal cells.

What is the most likely mechanism?

  1. A. Leukaemic cells crowding the marrow
  2. B. Peripheral destruction of all cells
  3. C. T cell attack on stem cells
  4. D. Fibrosis replacing marrow
  5. E. Splenic sequestration
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. T cell attack on stem cells

Aplastic anaemia is marrow failure with pancytopenia and a hypocellular, fatty marrow without infiltration or fibrosis. Most acquired cases are immune (cytotoxic T cells against stem cells), sometimes triggered by drugs (chloramphenicol, carbamazepine, gold, NSAIDs), benzene, radiation, viral hepatitis (seronegative), or PNH. Inherited forms include Fanconi anaemia (DNA repair defect; short stature, absent thumbs, café-au-lait spots) and telomere disorders. Splenomegaly argues against it. Treatment: transfusion support, immunosuppression (antithymocyte globulin, ciclosporin, eltrombopag) or stem cell transplant. Pure red cell aplasia affects only red cells: thymoma, parvovirus B19 (especially in haemolytic anaemias, causing aplastic crisis), drugs.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Leukaemic cells crowding the marrow: Leukaemic crowding would show blasts and a packed marrow; hers is empty and fatty.
  • B. Peripheral destruction of all cells: Peripheral destruction would leave a busy marrow trying to compensate.
  • D. Fibrosis replacing marrow: Marrow fibrosis shows reticulin and teardrop cells, not empty fatty marrow.
  • E. Splenic sequestration: Splenic sequestration needs an enlarged spleen; his is normal.

Key point: Aplastic anaemia: pancytopenia, fatty empty marrow, no splenomegaly; usually T cell-mediated destruction of stem cells.

Question 8 · Haemodynamic disorders · easy

A 70-year-old obese man had a 3-hour operation for bowel cancer and stayed in bed for a week because of pain; he did not receive heparin injections. His left calf had become swollen, which nobody noticed. He dies suddenly while walking for the first time after abdominal surgery. A large clot is found straddling the main pulmonary artery bifurcation.

Where did this clot most likely form?

  1. A. Superficial leg veins
  2. B. Left atrium
  3. C. Pelvic arteries
  4. D. Coronary arteries
  5. E. Proximal deep leg veins
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Proximal deep leg veins

Most pulmonary emboli (over 95%) arise from deep veins of the legs, especially above the knee (popliteal, femoral, iliac veins). Calf vein thrombi are less likely to embolise unless they extend proximally. Risk factors are those of Virchow triad: immobility, surgery (especially orthopaedic and pelvic), cancer, pregnancy and the puerperium, oestrogens, heart failure, and thrombophilia. A saddle embolus at the bifurcation can cause sudden death by acute right ventricular failure (cor pulmonale) and electromechanical dissociation.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Superficial leg veins: Superficial leg veins rarely produce large emboli.
  • B. Left atrium: Left atrial clots go to the brain and systemic arteries, not the lungs.
  • C. Pelvic arteries: Arterial clots travel downstream to the legs, not to the lungs.
  • D. Coronary arteries: Coronary artery clots stay in the heart's own circulation.

Key point: Pulmonary emboli mostly come from proximal deep leg veins; a saddle embolus can cause sudden death.

Question 9 · Immunopathology · medium

A 24-year-old health worker starting a job in a TB ward has 0.1 ml of tuberculin injected into her forearm, just under the skin. She was not given BCG as a child. Nothing appears for the first day, but at 48-72 hours there is a firm, raised, red area 15 mm across at the site. She has no cough or fever and her chest X-ray is clear.

What causes this reaction?

  1. A. Preformed IgE on mast cells
  2. B. IgG complexes and neutrophils
  3. C. Th1-activated macrophages
  4. D. Complement-mediated cell lysis
  5. E. Plasma cells making IgM
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Th1-activated macrophages

Type IV hypersensitivity is T cell-mediated. Delayed-type hypersensitivity: CD4+ Th1 cells recognise antigen and release IFN-gamma, activating macrophages, and Th17 cells recruit neutrophils; examples are the tuberculin reaction (peaking at 48-72 hours), granulomas, contact dermatitis (nickel, poison ivy), and organ-specific autoimmunity (type 1 diabetes, multiple sclerosis, rheumatoid arthritis, Crohn disease). T cell-mediated cytotoxicity: CD8+ cells kill target cells (viral hepatitis, some transplant rejection, type 1 diabetes). Antibodies play no part, and transfer of T cells, not serum, transfers the reaction.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Preformed IgE on mast cells: IgE reactions appear within minutes as a wheal, not a firm swelling at 48-72 hours.
  • B. IgG complexes and neutrophils: Immune complex (Arthus) reactions peak at 4-8 hours with neutrophils and oedema.
  • D. Complement-mediated cell lysis: Complement-mediated lysis is a type II antibody mechanism, not this delayed induration.
  • E. Plasma cells making IgM: Antibodies do not cause the tuberculin reaction; T cells and macrophages do.

Key point: Type IV hypersensitivity is T cell-mediated: delayed (Th1/macrophage) reactions like the tuberculin test, or CD8 killing.

Question 10 · Inflammation and repair · easy

A 25-year-old man has pain that began around the umbilicus and moved to the right iliac fossa over 12 hours, with vomiting and a fever of 38.2 °C. He has tenderness and guarding at McBurney's point. He has a white cell count of 19 x10⁹/L, 88% neutrophils, with many band forms, and a CRP of 120 mg/L.

What do the band forms indicate?

  1. A. A chronic myeloid leukaemia
  2. B. Viral infection
  3. C. Marrow release of young neutrophils
  4. D. Steroid treatment
  5. E. Neutrophil destruction in the spleen
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Marrow release of young neutrophils

In acute bacterial infection, IL-1 and TNF release neutrophils from the bone marrow storage pool, and G-CSF stimulates production, causing neutrophilia with less mature forms (bands, metamyelocytes) in the blood, a "left shift". Toxic granulation and Döhle bodies may be seen. Other patterns: lymphocytosis in viral infections (and pertussis), eosinophilia in parasites and allergy, monocytosis in tuberculosis and chronic infection. A very high count with immature cells resembling leukaemia (leukaemoid reaction) can occur in severe infection; leukocyte alkaline phosphatase helps distinguish it from CML. Some infections lower the white count (typhoid, overwhelming sepsis, viral infections).

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. A chronic myeloid leukaemia: Chronic myeloid leukaemia shows all stages of myeloid cells with a large spleen, not acute appendicitis.
  • B. Viral infection: Viral infections usually raise lymphocytes, not neutrophils with band forms.
  • D. Steroid treatment: Steroids release mature neutrophils and do not cause many band forms.
  • E. Neutrophil destruction in the spleen: Neutrophil destruction in the spleen would lower the count, not raise it to 19 x10⁹/L.

Key point: A left shift (band forms) reflects release of immature neutrophils from the marrow in acute bacterial infection.
