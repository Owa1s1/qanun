Question 1 · Haematology and immunity · easy

A student is studying white blood cells under the microscope in a laboratory in Peshawar. She sees a granulocyte with large dark blue granules that stain metachromatically. This cell releases histamine and heparin during allergic and inflammatory reactions, increasing blood flow to the area.

Which white blood cell contains heparin and histamine in its granules?

  1. A. Eosinophil
  2. B. Monocyte
  3. C. Basophil
  4. D. Lymphocyte
  5. E. Neutrophil
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Basophil

Basophils are granulocytes whose dark granules store histamine and heparin. Histamine causes vasodilation and increased capillary permeability, while heparin prevents clotting locally. Granulocytes are white cells with granules in their cytoplasm. Basophils release their contents when an allergen cross-links IgE on their surface. This matters because basophil degranulation contributes to allergic symptoms. Other granulocytes have different granule proteins suited to their own roles. Mast cells are related to basophils and also release histamine in tissues. Eosinophils attack parasites, monocytes become macrophages, and lymphocytes coordinate immunity. Neutrophils are phagocytes. The granules are visible with special stains.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Eosinophil: Eosinophils contain major basic protein and are important in parasitic infections and allergy.
  • B. Monocyte: Monocytes become macrophages and do not store histamine or heparin in granules.
  • D. Lymphocyte: Lymphocytes mediate immune responses and do not contain histamine or heparin granules.
  • E. Neutrophil: Neutrophils contain enzymes for bacterial killing but do not store heparin and histamine.

Key point: Identify the white cell that stores histamine and heparin.

Question 2 · Cardiovascular system · easy

A 22-year-old man loses about 1 litre of blood from a stab wound in the thigh over 20 minutes. His heart rate rises to 120 per minute, his skin becomes cold and pale, his capillary refill is slow, and his blood pressure is 105/80 mmHg. He is anxious but talking.

Which compensatory response acts fastest to maintain his blood pressure?

  1. A. The baroreceptor reflex
  2. B. Increased secretion of aldosterone
  3. C. Increased production of red blood cells
  4. D. Increased thirst and drinking
  5. E. Movement of fluid from cells into the plasma
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. The baroreceptor reflex

After haemorrhage, the fall in arterial pressure reduces baroreceptor firing and, at lower pressures, stimulates the chemoreceptors. Within seconds, sympathetic stimulation increases heart rate and contractility, constricts arterioles (raising resistance, which keeps diastolic pressure up and narrows the pulse pressure, as here), and constricts veins to support venous return. Over minutes to hours, capillary reabsorption of interstitial fluid, angiotensin II, ADH and aldosterone restore volume; red cells are replaced over weeks.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Increased secretion of aldosterone: Aldosterone acts over hours to days by making the kidney retain sodium, too slow for the first minutes.
  • C. Increased production of red blood cells: New red cells take days to weeks to be made, so they cannot support blood pressure in minutes.
  • D. Increased thirst and drinking: Drinking cannot quickly replace a large bleed, and he may not be able to drink; it is not the fastest mechanism.
  • E. Movement of fluid from cells into the plasma: Fluid moves into the plasma over minutes to hours, mainly from the interstitium, so it is slower than the reflex.

Key point: The baroreceptor (sympathetic) reflex is the fastest defence of blood pressure after haemorrhage.

Question 3 · Endocrine and reproduction · easy

A 52-year-old woman from Hazara has not had a period for 12 months. She has vaginal dryness. Serum FSH 65 IU/L (premenopausal <10), LH 40 IU/L (premenopausal <20), oestradiol 150 pmol/L (menopausal <200). She has no hot flushes but worries about bone strength.

After the menopause, which oestrogen predominates and where does it come from?

  1. A. Oestradiol, still made by the remaining ovarian follicles
  2. B. Oestriol, made by the adrenal cortex
  3. C. Oestrone, made by aromatase in fat
  4. D. Oestradiol, made by the corpus luteum of the last cycle
  5. E. No oestrogen at all is produced after the menopause
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Oestrone, made by aromatase in fat

The menopause follows exhaustion of the ovarian follicles. Oestradiol and inhibin B fall, and the loss of their negative feedback allows FSH and LH to rise, FSH more than LH because inhibin, which selectively restrains FSH, is lost as well. The remaining oestrogen is oestrone, made by peripheral aromatisation of adrenal androstenedione, chiefly in adipose tissue, so levels depend on body fat. The consequences of oestrogen deficiency are vasomotor symptoms, urogenital atrophy, accelerated bone loss, with the fastest loss in the first years after the last period, and an unfavourable change in lipids. Diagnosis is clinical in a woman over 45; a raised FSH is needed only in younger women, where premature ovarian insufficiency must be confirmed on two samples.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Oestradiol, still made by the remaining ovarian follicles: Once the follicles are exhausted, ovarian oestradiol production ceases.
  • B. Oestriol, made by the adrenal cortex: Oestriol is mainly a product of pregnancy, formed from fetal precursors in the placenta.
  • D. Oestradiol, made by the corpus luteum of the last cycle: The corpus luteum lasts about two weeks and has long since gone.
  • E. No oestrogen at all is produced after the menopause: A small amount of weak oestrogen continues to be produced peripherally.

Key point: Identify oestrone from peripheral aromatisation as the main postmenopausal oestrogen.

Question 4 · Cell, nerve and muscle · easy

Skeletal, cardiac and smooth muscle all use actin and myosin, but they are controlled differently.

Which statement is correct?

  1. A. Smooth muscle is the one type regulated without troponin
  2. B. All three are regulated by troponin
  3. C. Skeletal muscle needs extracellular Ca²⁺ to contract
  4. D. Cardiac muscle can be tetanised like skeletal muscle
  5. E. Smooth muscle has the most organised sarcomeres
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Smooth muscle is the one type regulated without troponin

Skeletal muscle: striated, troponin-regulated, SR calcium released by the DHP-RyR1 mechanical link, voluntary control through motor units, can be tetanised. Cardiac muscle: striated, troponin-regulated, calcium-induced calcium release needing extracellular Ca²⁺, a syncytium joined by gap junctions, long refractory period so it cannot be tetanised. Smooth muscle: no sarcomeres, regulated by Ca²⁺-calmodulin and MLCK, slow economical contraction with latch, controlled by autonomic nerves, hormones and local factors.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. All three are regulated by troponin: Smooth muscle has no troponin, so not all three are troponin-regulated.
  • C. Skeletal muscle needs extracellular Ca²⁺ to contract: Skeletal muscle uses calcium from its sarcoplasmic reticulum, released by direct mechanical coupling, so it does not need calcium from outside.
  • D. Cardiac muscle can be tetanised like skeletal muscle: Cardiac muscle has a long refractory period, so it cannot be tetanised; this protects the heart's pumping rhythm.
  • E. Smooth muscle has the most organised sarcomeres: Smooth muscle has no sarcomeres at all, which is why it is not striated.

Key point: Troponin regulates skeletal and cardiac muscle; Ca²⁺-calmodulin and MLCK regulate smooth muscle.

Question 5 · Gastrointestinal system · easy

A 70-year-old woman taking ibuprofen 400 mg three times a day for knee arthritis for 6 months, without a stomach-protecting drug, vomits blood and passes black stools. She had no pain beforehand. Endoscopy shows a gastric ulcer on the lesser curve. Helicobacter testing is negative.

Why do NSAIDs cause gastric ulcers?

  1. A. They reduce protective prostaglandins
  2. B. They increase gastrin secretion
  3. C. They cause H. pylori infection
  4. D. They stimulate histamine release
  5. E. They directly increase pepsin
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. They reduce protective prostaglandins

Prostaglandins (mainly from COX-1) protect the gastric mucosa by increasing mucus and bicarbonate secretion, maintaining mucosal blood flow and reducing acid. NSAIDs inhibit COX and so remove this protection; they also injure the mucosa directly. Risk is higher in the elderly, with high doses, with steroids, anticoagulants or SSRIs, and after previous ulcers. A proton pump inhibitor protects at-risk patients; misoprostol, a prostaglandin analogue, also prevents NSAID ulcers. COX-2-selective drugs cause fewer ulcers but have cardiovascular risks.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. They increase gastrin secretion: NSAIDs do not increase gastrin; the damage comes from losing the protective effects of prostaglandins.
  • C. They cause H. pylori infection: NSAIDs do not cause infection, and her H. pylori test is negative; the drug alone explains the ulcer.
  • D. They stimulate histamine release: Histamine stimulates acid through H2 receptors, but NSAIDs do not release histamine; they remove protection instead.
  • E. They directly increase pepsin: Pepsin activity is not increased directly by NSAIDs; the mucosa is simply less able to protect itself against acid and pepsin.

Key point: NSAIDs cause ulcers by inhibiting protective COX-1 prostaglandins in the gastric mucosa.

Question 6 · Nervous system and special senses · easy

When a person walks from bright sunlight into a dark cinema, they can see very little at first, but over 20–30 minutes their vision gradually improves.

What mainly explains this improvement?

  1. A. Regeneration of rhodopsin in the rods
  2. B. Constriction of the pupils
  3. C. Increased number of cones
  4. D. Faster conduction in the optic nerve
  5. E. Contraction of the ciliary muscle
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Regeneration of rhodopsin in the rods

Bright light bleaches much of the photopigment. In the dark, cones adapt quickly (within about 5–10 minutes) but only a little; rods adapt slowly but greatly as rhodopsin is regenerated, increasing sensitivity by many thousands of times over 20–40 minutes. Pupil dilatation and neural changes in the retina add smaller effects. The reverse, light adaptation, is faster. Vitamin A deficiency slows dark adaptation.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Constriction of the pupils: The pupils dilate in the dark, not constrict; this helps a little but is quick.
  • C. Increased number of cones: The number of cones does not change.
  • D. Faster conduction in the optic nerve: Conduction in the optic nerve does not speed up.
  • E. Contraction of the ciliary muscle: The ciliary muscle changes focus, not sensitivity to dim light.

Key point: Dark adaptation is mainly due to slow regeneration of rhodopsin in the rods.

Question 7 · Renal physiology, body fluids and acid-base · medium

A 6-year-old boy from a cousin marriage has bowed legs and rickets that does not respond to vitamin D. He is short and thirsty. His urine contains glucose, amino acids, phosphate and bicarbonate, while his blood glucose is normal. He has a normal anion gap acidosis and low phosphate. Crystals are seen in his corneas.

Which part of the nephron is failing?

  1. A. The proximal convoluted tubule
  2. B. The glomerulus
  3. C. The thick ascending limb of the loop of Henle
  4. D. The distal convoluted tubule
  5. E. The collecting duct
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. The proximal convoluted tubule

Fanconi syndrome is a generalised failure of proximal tubular reabsorption, causing loss of glucose, amino acids, phosphate, bicarbonate (proximal renal tubular acidosis), urate and potassium. Loss of phosphate causes rickets or osteomalacia. Causes include inherited disorders such as cystinosis and Wilson's disease, multiple myeloma, heavy metals, and drugs such as outdated tetracycline and tenofovir.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. The glomerulus: Glomerular disease causes protein and blood in the urine, not loss of these small solutes.
  • C. The thick ascending limb of the loop of Henle: The thick ascending limb reabsorbs salt but not glucose or amino acids.
  • D. The distal convoluted tubule: The distal tubule does not reabsorb glucose or amino acids.
  • E. The collecting duct: The collecting duct does not handle glucose, amino acids or phosphate.

Key point: Fanconi syndrome is generalised proximal tubule failure: glycosuria, aminoaciduria, phosphaturia and bicarbonate loss.

Question 8 · Respiratory system · easy

A laboratory technician compares oxygen binding in adult blood and cord blood from a newborn. At the same oxygen partial pressure, cord blood haemoglobin is more saturated with oxygen than adult haemoglobin.

Why does fetal haemoglobin bind oxygen more tightly than adult haemoglobin?

  1. A. Fetal haemoglobin contains more iron atoms per tetramer, allowing it to bind extra oxygen molecules
  2. B. Fetal haemoglobin is converted to methaemoglobin in the placenta, increasing oxygen release to the mother
  3. C. Fetal haemoglobin has a lower affinity for 2,3-BPG, so the curve is shifted left and oxygen affinity is higher
  4. D. Fetal haemoglobin has a higher P50, so it unloads oxygen more readily at the tissues
  5. E. Fetal haemoglobin binds carbon dioxide instead of oxygen, leaving more oxygen for maternal tissues
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Fetal haemoglobin has a lower affinity for 2,3-BPG, so the curve is shifted left and oxygen affinity is higher

Fetal haemoglobin differs from adult haemoglobin in its globin chains. It binds 2,3-bisphosphoglycerate less strongly, so the oxygen dissociation curve is shifted to the left and its P50 is lower. This means that at the same oxygen partial pressure fetal haemoglobin is more saturated than adult haemoglobin. The left shift lets fetal blood extract oxygen from maternal blood in the placenta. Maternal haemoglobin unloads oxygen more readily at the higher carbon dioxide levels of the intervillous space. After birth, fetal haemoglobin is gradually replaced by adult haemoglobin. This matters because it explains how the fetus obtains oxygen from the mother.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Fetal haemoglobin contains more iron atoms per tetramer, allowing it to bind extra oxygen molecules: Fetal haemoglobin, like adult haemoglobin, is a tetramer with four iron atoms and binds four oxygen molecules; it does not bind extra oxygen.
  • B. Fetal haemoglobin is converted to methaemoglobin in the placenta, increasing oxygen release to the mother: Methaemoglobin cannot carry oxygen and would harm oxygen transfer; fetal haemoglobin functions normally in the fetus.
  • D. Fetal haemoglobin has a higher P50, so it unloads oxygen more readily at the tissues: A higher P50 means lower affinity and a right shift; fetal haemoglobin has a lower P50 and higher affinity.
  • E. Fetal haemoglobin binds carbon dioxide instead of oxygen, leaving more oxygen for maternal tissues: Fetal haemoglobin binds oxygen for fetal use; carbon dioxide transport does not replace oxygen binding.

Key point: Describe why fetal haemoglobin has a higher oxygen affinity than adult haemoglobin.

Question 9 · Haematology and immunity · medium

A 20-year-old woman has had heavy periods since they began, frequent nosebleeds and prolonged bleeding after a tooth extraction. She bruises easily but has never had a swollen joint. Her mother has similar problems. Her platelet count is normal, PT is normal and APTT slightly prolonged. Bleeding worsened after she took aspirin.

What is the most likely diagnosis?

  1. A. von Willebrand disease
  2. B. Haemophilia A
  3. C. Immune thrombocytopenia
  4. D. Vitamin K deficiency
  5. E. Factor V Leiden
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. von Willebrand disease

von Willebrand factor has two roles: it binds platelets to collagen (adhesion) and carries and protects factor VIII. In von Willebrand disease, usually autosomal dominant, both sexes have mucosal bleeding (nose, gums, heavy periods, after dental work). The APTT may be prolonged because factor VIII is reduced, and platelet function tests are abnormal. Desmopressin releases stored vWF from endothelial cells and helps mild forms; vWF-containing concentrates are used for severe bleeding. Antifibrinolytics such as tranexamic acid help mucosal bleeding.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Haemophilia A: Haemophilia A is X-linked and affects mainly males, with bleeding into joints and muscles; she has mucosal bleeding and no joint bleeds.
  • C. Immune thrombocytopenia: Immune thrombocytopenia lowers the platelet count; hers is normal.
  • D. Vitamin K deficiency: Vitamin K deficiency prolongs the PT and does not run in families from mother to daughter.
  • E. Factor V Leiden: Factor V Leiden increases clotting; it does not cause bleeding.

Key point: von Willebrand disease causes mucosal bleeding in both sexes; desmopressin releases stored vWF.

Question 10 · Cardiovascular system · medium

A drug is applied to isolated sinoatrial node tissue. It completely abolishes the upstroke (phase 0) of the nodal action potential, while a selective fast sodium channel blocker given earlier had no effect on that upstroke.

The drug most likely blocks which channels?

  1. A. L-type calcium channels
  2. B. Fast voltage-gated sodium channels
  3. C. Delayed rectifier potassium channels
  4. D. Ryanodine receptors of the sarcoplasmic reticulum
  5. E. Muscarinic M2 receptors
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. L-type calcium channels

In "slow response" tissues (SA node and AV node) the resting potential is less negative and fast Na⁺ channels stay inactivated, so the upstroke depends on Ca²⁺ entry through L-type channels. This makes the upstroke slower and conduction through the AV node slow. Non-dihydropyridine calcium channel blockers (verapamil, diltiazem) therefore slow the heart rate and AV conduction.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Fast voltage-gated sodium channels: The question states that blocking fast Na⁺ channels did not affect the nodal upstroke.
  • C. Delayed rectifier potassium channels: Blocking potassium channels slows repolarisation and lengthens the action potential; it would not abolish the upstroke.
  • D. Ryanodine receptors of the sarcoplasmic reticulum: Ryanodine receptors release Ca²⁺ inside the cell for contraction; they do not carry membrane current for the upstroke.
  • E. Muscarinic M2 receptors: M2 receptors mediate vagal slowing; blocking them would speed the node, not abolish its upstroke.

Key point: Phase 0 in nodal cells is due to Ca²⁺ influx through L-type channels, not Na⁺.
