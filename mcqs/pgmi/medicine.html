Question 1 · Acute medicine, poisoning and critical care · medium

A 30-year-old woman collecting firewood in Kohistan is stung on the right index finger by a scorpion. She has severe burning pain and mild local swelling, with pain radiating to the hand. She is calm. Pulse is 84/min, blood pressure 118/74 mmHg, respiratory rate 16/min and temperature 37.0 °C. There is no sweating, salivation, restlessness, vomiting or muscle twitching. Pupils are normal and examination of the chest and abdomen is unremarkable. She reaches a hospital 90 minutes after the sting and asks for antivenom injections.

What is the most appropriate management?

  1. A. Analgesia and local anaesthetic
  2. B. Scorpion antivenom given intravenously now
  3. C. Incision and suction of the wound
  4. D. Tight tourniquet above the sting
  5. E. Prazosin 1 mg orally immediately
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Analgesia and local anaesthetic

Most scorpion stings in adults cause only local pain, paraesthesia and mild swelling. Management is analgesia (paracetamol, NSAIDs, and opioids for severe pain), local anaesthetic infiltration or digital nerve block, cleaning the site, tetanus prophylaxis as required and observation for 4-6 hours for systemic features (sweating, salivation, agitation, vomiting, tachycardia, hypertension, priapism, muscle twitching), which dictate admission, prazosin and antivenom. Ice reduces pain but should not be applied directly. Do not use tourniquets, incision or suction. Children and highly venomous species are at higher risk of severe envenoming and need longer observation. Counsel on shaking out shoes and clothes, and using bed nets and lights at night, in scorpion-prone areas.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Scorpion antivenom given intravenously now: Antivenom is reserved for systemic envenomation, because reactions are common and she has none of its indications.
  • C. Incision and suction of the wound: Cutting does not remove venom and increases infection risk.
  • D. Tight tourniquet above the sting: Tourniquets cause ischaemic injury and do not help.
  • E. Prazosin 1 mg orally immediately: Alpha-blockade is for systemic autonomic features, and her blood pressure is normal.

Key point: Treat local scorpion stings with analgesia and local anaesthesia and observe for systemic signs.

Question 2 · Cardiology · easy

A 60-year-old man is reviewed at a hospital in Mansehra four weeks after primary PCI with a drug-eluting stent for an inferior STEMI. He takes aspirin 75 mg, ticagrelor 90 mg twice daily, bisoprolol, ramipril and atorvastatin 80 mg. He has had no bleeding, his haemoglobin is 13.8 g/dL (13-17) and creatinine is 0.9 mg/dL (0.7-1.3). He has no history of stroke, anaemia or bleeding disorder, and does not take an anticoagulant. He asks how long he must continue both antiplatelet drugs.

What is the recommended duration of dual antiplatelet therapy?

  1. A. 1 month
  2. B. 3 months
  3. C. 6 months
  4. D. 12 months
  5. E. 24 months
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. 12 months

After an acute coronary syndrome, the 2023 ESC guideline recommends dual antiplatelet therapy with aspirin and a P2Y12 inhibitor (ticagrelor or prasugrel preferred over clopidogrel) for 12 months, whether or not a stent was placed, unless there is a contraindication or high bleeding risk. In patients at high bleeding risk, shortening to 1-6 months can be considered, followed by a single antiplatelet. After 12 months a single antiplatelet drug is continued long term, usually aspirin or clopidogrel. Patients should be warned not to stop either drug early without medical advice, as stent thrombosis can be fatal. Adding a proton pump inhibitor is advised for those at gastrointestinal bleeding risk.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. 1 month: Is a shortened course reserved for patients at high bleeding risk; he has no bleeding risk features.
  • B. 3 months: Is shorter than recommended after ACS in a patient at normal bleeding risk and leaves his stent less protected.
  • C. 6 months: Is used for stable disease after elective PCI or for some high bleeding risk patients, not after ACS in a patient without bleeding risk.
  • E. 24 months: Is not routine; extension beyond 12 months is considered only in selected patients at high ischaemic and low bleeding risk.

Key point: State that dual antiplatelet therapy is continued for 12 months after ACS unless bleeding risk is high.

Question 3 · Endocrinology and diabetes · medium

A 55-year-old woman in Bannu has a routine health check. She feels well, has no goitre, and her weight, bowel habit and mood are unchanged. Blood pressure is 124/78 mmHg and pulse 72/min. TSH is 7.1 mIU/L (0.4-4.0) with free T4 15 pmol/L (12-22). The same results were found three months ago. Anti-thyroid peroxidase antibodies are negative and total cholesterol is 190 mg/dL. She has no history of cardiovascular disease or infertility, has no pregnancy plans, and takes no drugs that affect the thyroid.

What is the most appropriate management?

  1. A. Start levothyroxine 100 micrograms daily
  2. B. Repeat TSH every 6-12 months
  3. C. Radioiodine uptake scan
  4. D. Start liothyronine
  5. E. Thyroid fine-needle aspiration
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Repeat TSH every 6-12 months

Subclinical hypothyroidism is a raised TSH with normal free T4, and TSH is often transiently raised, so repeat after 2-3 months. Treatment decisions depend on TSH level, age, symptoms, antibodies and risks. In adults under 65-70, treat when TSH is persistently above 10 mIU/L, or when TSH is 4-10 mIU/L with symptoms (a trial of levothyroxine for three to six months, stopped if no benefit), positive anti-TPO antibodies with a goitre, a plan for pregnancy or infertility treatment, or cardiovascular disease or heart failure. Trials (TRUST) found no symptomatic benefit of levothyroxine in older people with TSH up to 20 mIU/L. Otherwise monitor TSH every 6-12 months, since 2-5% progress to overt disease per year, faster with positive antibodies. Avoid overtreatment, which causes atrial fibrillation and bone loss.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Start levothyroxine 100 micrograms daily: Treating mild subclinical hypothyroidism in an asymptomatic person does not improve symptoms or outcomes in trials, and a full dose risks over-treatment.
  • C. Radioiodine uptake scan: An uptake scan is for hyperthyroidism and thyroid nodules; it adds nothing to the assessment of a mildly raised TSH.
  • D. Start liothyronine: Liothyronine is not indicated for subclinical hypothyroidism and causes fluctuating hormone levels and cardiac stress.
  • E. Thyroid fine-needle aspiration: Aspiration is for suspicious nodules; she has no goitre or nodule, so it has no role.

Key point: Observe rather than treat mild asymptomatic subclinical hypothyroidism.

Question 4 · Gastroenterology · easy

A 29-year-old woman attends a hospital in Swat with eight months of crampy lower abdominal pain relieved by passing stool, with loose urgent stools three to four times a day and bloating. She has no nocturnal diarrhoea, blood in the stool, weight loss or fever, and her examination is normal. Haemoglobin is normal, CRP is 0.2 mg/dL (below 0.5), tissue transglutaminase IgA is negative and faecal calprotectin is 18 µg/g (below 50). She meets the Rome IV criteria for diarrhoea-predominant irritable bowel syndrome.

Which drug is most appropriate for her diarrhoea?

  1. A. Oral prednisolone
  2. B. Loperamide
  3. C. Oral azithromycin
  4. D. Mesalazine
  5. E. Ciprofloxacin
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Loperamide

Irritable bowel syndrome is a functional disorder diagnosed positively using the Rome IV criteria: recurrent abdominal pain on average at least one day a week, related to defaecation or associated with a change in stool frequency or form, for at least three months. Limited tests (full blood count, CRP, coeliac serology and faecal calprotectin) exclude other conditions. NICE CG61 advises lifestyle and dietary advice first, then loperamide as first-line for diarrhoea, antispasmodics (hyoscine, mebeverine) or peppermint oil for pain, and laxatives (avoiding lactulose) for constipation. For persistent symptoms, low-dose tricyclic antidepressants, then SSRIs, are used, together with cognitive behavioural therapy. Reassure patients, as anxiety about cancer is common.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Oral prednisolone: Has no role in irritable bowel syndrome, which is not inflammatory, and causes harm.
  • C. Oral azithromycin: Treats infective diarrhoea; she has no infection and antibiotics would fuel resistance.
  • D. Mesalazine: Treats ulcerative colitis, which is excluded by a normal calprotectin and the absence of blood.
  • E. Ciprofloxacin: Is not a treatment for irritable bowel syndrome and fluoroquinolone resistance is common in Pakistan.

Key point: Use loperamide as first-line treatment for diarrhoea-predominant irritable bowel syndrome.

Question 5 · Haematology and oncology · easy

A 36-year-old woman in Karachi returned two days ago from a 14-hour coach journey from Quetta and now has pain and swelling of the left calf. The left calf circumference is 3.5 cm larger than the right, 10 cm below the tibial tuberosity. There is no pitting oedema, no collateral superficial veins, no previous venous thromboembolism, no recent surgery or immobilisation, no known cancer and no alternative diagnosis more likely. She takes no hormonal treatment, is not pregnant and has no chest symptoms. Her Wells score is 1.

What is the most appropriate next step?

  1. A. Immediate therapeutic LMWH without testing
  2. B. Contrast venography
  3. C. Serum D-dimer
  4. D. Thrombophilia screen
  5. E. CT pulmonary angiogram
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Serum D-dimer

Diagnosis of deep vein thrombosis combines clinical probability with D-dimer and ultrasound. The two-level Wells score (calf swelling above 3 cm, pitting oedema, collateral veins, active cancer, immobilisation or surgery, previous VTE, paralysis, tenderness along veins; minus 2 if another diagnosis is as likely) classifies DVT as unlikely (score 1 or less) or likely (2 or more). In unlikely patients, a negative D-dimer excludes DVT; a positive result needs proximal leg ultrasound. In likely patients, proximal ultrasound is done immediately, with interim anticoagulation if it cannot be done within 4 hours; if negative and D-dimer positive, repeat ultrasound in a week. Long journeys, such as coach travel, are a recognised provoking factor. D-dimer is not useful in patients with cancer, pregnancy or recent surgery.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Immediate therapeutic LMWH without testing: With a Wells score of 1 (DVT unlikely), a D-dimer allows exclusion without anticoagulation, avoiding unnecessary treatment risks.
  • B. Contrast venography: Venography is invasive, rarely done and no longer used when ultrasound and D-dimer are available.
  • D. Thrombophilia screen: Thrombophilia testing does not diagnose acute DVT and is not indicated at this stage.
  • E. CT pulmonary angiogram: She has no chest symptoms, and CTPA is for suspected pulmonary embolism rather than first-line for a suspected calf DVT.

Key point: Use Wells score and D-dimer to evaluate suspected DVT.

Question 6 · Infectious diseases · easy

A 22-year-old man in Mansehra has had 3 weeks of foul-smelling pale loose stools, abdominal bloating, flatulence and mild cramping, with a 3 kg weight loss. He has no fever or blood in the stool. He drank untreated stream water on a hiking trip 5 weeks ago. Examination is normal apart from mild abdominal distension. Stool microscopy shows Giardia lamblia trophozoites and cysts. HIV test is negative.

Which drug is most appropriate?

  1. A. Tinidazole 2 g as a single dose
  2. B. Ciprofloxacin 500 mg twice daily for 7 days
  3. C. Albendazole 400 mg daily for 3 days
  4. D. Praziquantel 600 mg single dose
  5. E. Co-trimoxazole twice daily for 5 days
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Tinidazole 2 g as a single dose

Giardiasis, caused by Giardia lamblia, is spread by contaminated water or faecal-oral contact and causes watery, foul, fatty stools, bloating, flatulence, cramps and weight loss, without fever or blood. Diagnosis is by stool microscopy for cysts or trophozoites (repeated if negative), antigen assay or PCR. Treatment is a single dose of tinidazole 2 g, or metronidazole 400 mg three times daily for 5 days, or nitazoxanide; albendazole is less effective. Chronic cases can cause lactose intolerance and malabsorption, and people with IgA deficiency or HIV are prone to persistent infection. Prevention is by boiling or filtering water, and hand washing. Contacts are treated if symptomatic.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Ciprofloxacin 500 mg twice daily for 7 days: Ciprofloxacin treats bacterial enteritis but has no activity against Giardia; it would not clear his protozoal infection.
  • C. Albendazole 400 mg daily for 3 days: Albendazole has some activity against Giardia but lower cure rates than nitroimidazoles, so it is not first-line when tinidazole or metronidazole is available.
  • D. Praziquantel 600 mg single dose: Praziquantel treats tapeworms and schistosomes; it is ineffective against Giardia, so it would not clear the trophozoites and cysts found in his stool.
  • E. Co-trimoxazole twice daily for 5 days: Co-trimoxazole is used for Cyclospora and Cystoisospora, not for Giardia, so it would fail here.

Key point: Treat giardiasis with a single dose of tinidazole or a short course of metronidazole.

Question 7 · Hepatology · medium

A 22-year-old student attends a clinic in Peshawar because his friends say his eyes turn yellow when he fasts during Ramadan and when he has a fever. He feels well. Total bilirubin is 2.6 mg/dL (0.3-1.2) with direct bilirubin 0.4 mg/dL. ALT is 28 U/L (below 40), ALP 82 U/L (40-129), haemoglobin 14.8 g/dL (13-17), reticulocytes 1.2%, LDH and haptoglobin are normal, and the urine has no bilirubin. Examination is normal and he takes no drugs.

What is the most likely diagnosis?

  1. A. Gilbert syndrome
  2. B. Crigler-Najjar syndrome
  3. C. Dubin-Johnson syndrome
  4. D. Haemolytic anaemia
  5. E. Chronic hepatitis B
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Gilbert syndrome

Gilbert syndrome affects 5-10% of people and results from reduced activity of UDP-glucuronosyltransferase (UGT1A1). It causes mild, intermittent unconjugated hyperbilirubinaemia (usually below 3-4 mg/dL) with normal liver tests, normal haemoglobin and no haemolysis, precipitated by fasting, dehydration, illness, stress and menstruation; fasting during Ramadan is a classic trigger. Urine contains no bilirubin because unconjugated bilirubin is not water-soluble (acholuric jaundice). Diagnosis is clinical once haemolysis and liver disease are excluded; genetic testing is rarely needed. No treatment is required, and reassurance is enough. It is harmless, though it can increase the risk of some drug toxicities (irinotecan). Investigate further if the direct fraction is raised or liver tests are abnormal.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Crigler-Najjar syndrome: Causes severe unconjugated hyperbilirubinaemia from infancy, with levels far above his 2.6 mg/dL.
  • C. Dubin-Johnson syndrome: Causes conjugated hyperbilirubinaemia with bilirubin in the urine, whereas his is unconjugated with no urinary bilirubin.
  • D. Haemolytic anaemia: Is excluded by normal haemoglobin, reticulocytes, LDH and haptoglobin.
  • E. Chronic hepatitis B: Would show raised aminotransferases and positive HBsAg, and his ALT is normal.

Key point: Diagnose Gilbert syndrome from isolated unconjugated hyperbilirubinaemia provoked by fasting.

Question 8 · Neurology · easy

A 34-year-old woman in Zhob woke 24 hours ago with drooping of the right side of her face. She cannot close the right eye, raise the right eyebrow or whistle, and food collects in her right cheek. She has mild pain behind the right ear and sounds seem louder on that side. There is no rash or vesicles in the ear canal, no limb weakness and no other cranial nerve involvement. Blood pressure is 118/76 mmHg and glucose is 94 mg/dL (70-140).

Which treatment is most appropriate?

  1. A. Oral aciclovir 800 mg five times daily alone
  2. B. Reassurance without drug treatment
  3. C. Prednisolone 50 mg for 10 days
  4. D. Facial nerve decompression surgery
  5. E. Intravenous immunoglobulin for 5 days
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Prednisolone 50 mg for 10 days

Bell palsy is an acute unilateral lower motor neuron facial palsy of unknown cause, likely viral, affecting forehead and lower face equally, peaking within 72 hours, often with ear pain, hyperacusis or altered taste. It is a diagnosis of exclusion: look for vesicles (Ramsay Hunt syndrome), otitis media, parotid masses, diabetes, Lyme disease, HIV, sarcoidosis and stroke, where the forehead is spared. Prednisolone 50 mg daily for 10 days, or 60 mg for 5 days then tapered, started within 72 hours improves recovery; antivirals are not routinely recommended. Eye protection is essential, with lubricating drops and taping the eyelid at night. Refer if no improvement by 3 weeks, or sooner if atypical. About 70-85% recover completely.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Oral aciclovir 800 mg five times daily alone: Antivirals alone add nothing to steroids, and she has no vesicles or ear canal rash to suggest herpes zoster; a steroid is the proven treatment.
  • B. Reassurance without drug treatment: Most people recover, but prednisolone started within 72 hours improves the chance of full recovery; this woman presents at 24 hours, so treatment should be offered.
  • D. Facial nerve decompression surgery: Surgery is not indicated in idiopathic facial palsy; the benefit is unproven and the risks include hearing loss, so it is not appropriate in her case.
  • E. Intravenous immunoglobulin for 5 days: Immunoglobulin has no role in facial palsy; it is used for Guillain-Barré syndrome, and she has no limb weakness or bilateral facial involvement.

Key point: Treat Bell palsy with early prednisolone and eye protection.

Question 9 · Nephrology · easy

A 28-year-old married woman in Mardan has had two days of burning on passing urine, frequency and urgency. She has no fever, flank pain, vaginal discharge or vomiting. Temperature is 36.9 °C, pulse 78/min and blood pressure 112/70 mmHg, with mild suprapubic tenderness and no renal angle tenderness. Urine dipstick shows leucocytes 2+ and nitrite positive. A urine pregnancy test is negative, eGFR is 98 mL/min/1.73 m2 and she has no drug allergies. She has had no antibiotics in the past six months.

Which treatment is most appropriate?

  1. A. Oral ciprofloxacin 500 mg twice daily for 7 days
  2. B. Intravenous ceftriaxone 1 g daily for 5 days
  3. C. Oral amoxicillin 500 mg three times daily for 5 days
  4. D. Oral co-amoxiclav 625 mg three times daily for 10 days
  5. E. Nitrofurantoin for 3 days
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Nitrofurantoin for 3 days

Uncomplicated cystitis in a non-pregnant woman presents with dysuria, frequency and urgency without fever, flank pain or vaginal symptoms. A dipstick positive for nitrite and leucocytes makes infection very likely, and culture is not needed at first presentation. NICE and IDSA recommend nitrofurantoin (100 mg modified-release twice daily for three days, avoided if eGFR is below 45) or a single 3 g dose of fosfomycin as first choice. In Pakistan, ciprofloxacin and other fluoroquinolones fail in many Gram-negative infections, amoxicillin is largely ineffective and ESBL-producing E. coli is common, so these are not used empirically. Send culture for treatment failure, recurrent or atypical cases, and in pregnancy. Advise fluids and simple analgesia, and treat fever, loin pain or male infection as complicated.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Oral ciprofloxacin 500 mg twice daily for 7 days: Fluoroquinolone resistance in Escherichia coli is high in Pakistan, and tendon and neuropsychiatric risks are unjustified for uncomplicated cystitis.
  • B. Intravenous ceftriaxone 1 g daily for 5 days: Parenteral therapy is unnecessary for afebrile lower tract infection and promotes ESBL selection; she can take an oral narrow-spectrum drug.
  • C. Oral amoxicillin 500 mg three times daily for 5 days: Most community E. coli are now resistant to amoxicillin, so empirical use risks treatment failure in this otherwise simple infection.
  • D. Oral co-amoxiclav 625 mg three times daily for 10 days: This broad-spectrum course is longer than needed for uncomplicated cystitis and adds avoidable diarrhoea and resistance pressure.

Key point: Choose nitrofurantoin or fosfomycin for uncomplicated cystitis.

Question 10 · Respiratory medicine · easy

A 68-year-old man with known COPD is brought to a hospital in Quetta after three days of increasing breathlessness and cough. The ambulance crew gave 15 L/min oxygen by reservoir mask. He is drowsy, with respiratory rate 24/min, oxygen saturation 98% on that oxygen, pulse 102/min and a flapping tremor of the hands. An arterial blood gas on the oxygen shows pH 7.29, PaCO2 68 mmHg (35-45) and PaO2 140 mmHg. He had a previous admission with respiratory failure.

Which oxygen prescription is most appropriate now?

  1. A. High-flow oxygen 15 L/min by reservoir mask
  2. B. Withhold all oxygen
  3. C. Controlled Venturi oxygen, aim for 88-92%
  4. D. Nasal cannula at 6 L/min
  5. E. Humidified air without oxygen
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Controlled Venturi oxygen, aim for 88-92%

Patients with COPD (and other causes of chronic hypercapnia) who receive excess oxygen can develop worsening carbon dioxide retention through ventilation-perfusion mismatch and the Haldane effect, leading to respiratory acidosis and drowsiness. The British Thoracic Society emergency oxygen guideline advises a target saturation of 88-92% in patients at risk of hypercapnic respiratory failure, using a Venturi mask (24% at 2-3 L/min, or 28% at 4 L/min) or low-flow nasal oxygen, with arterial blood gases checked 30-60 minutes after any change. Paramedics should use the same target. Patients should carry an oxygen alert card. Oxygen is not withheld, as hypoxia is more dangerous than mild hypercapnia. In Pakistan, high-flow oxygen is often given without titration, so staff education matters.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. High-flow oxygen 15 L/min by reservoir mask: Is the cause of his rising carbon dioxide and drowsiness; uncontrolled oxygen worsens hypercapnia in COPD, and his PaO2 of 140 mmHg is far above need.
  • B. Withhold all oxygen: Would risk dangerous hypoxaemia, since tissue oxygen delivery must still be maintained; the aim is controlled, not absent, oxygen.
  • D. Nasal cannula at 6 L/min: Delivers an unpredictable and variable oxygen concentration, which is unsuitable when the target range is narrow.
  • E. Humidified air without oxygen: Gives no supplementary oxygen, and with PaO2 not yet known on air he could become hypoxic.

Key point: Prescribe controlled oxygen with a target saturation of 88-92% in COPD exacerbation with hypercapnia.
