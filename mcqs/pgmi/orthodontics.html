Question 1 · Orthodontic assessment and treatment planning · medium

A 22-year-old woman presents to a private dental clinic in Mardan requesting alignment of mild lower incisor crowding measured at 2.5 mm between the lower left canine (33) and lower right canine (43). She has a Class I incisor and molar relationship, an overjet of 2.5 mm, an overbite of 3 mm, a pleasant profile, and triangle-shaped incisors with dark gingival embrasures ("black triangles"). The orthodontist plans fixed appliances combined with interproximal enamel reduction (IPR) across the six anterior contact points from the mesial of 33 to the mesial of 43.

What is the most enamel that may safely be removed per surface?

  1. A. 0.10 mm per surface (0.20 mm per contact point)
  2. B. 0.25 mm per surface (0.50 mm per contact point)
  3. C. 0.50 mm per surface (1.00 mm per contact point)
  4. D. 0.75 mm per surface (1.50 mm per contact point)
  5. E. 1.00 mm per surface (2.00 mm per contact point)
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. 0.25 mm per surface (0.50 mm per contact point)

Interproximal enamel reduction (IPR), also called reproximation or slenderising, is the controlled removal of outer enamel from the mesial and distal contact surfaces of teeth to create a small amount of space (typically 2 to 4 mm per arch) or to reshape triangular crowns and reduce dark gingival embrasures. Proximal enamel is thinner on lower incisors (approximately 0.5 to 0.7 mm) than on premolars and molars (approximately 0.8 to 1.0 mm). Clinical evidence shows that up to 50% of the enamel thickness—conventionally capped at 0.25 mm per tooth surface, which equals 0.5 mm per interproximal contact point—can be safely removed from anterior teeth (and up to 0.3 to 0.4 mm per surface on posterior teeth if radiographically verified) using abrasive strips, oscillating discs, or fine diamond burs, followed by meticulous polishing and topical fluoride application.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. 0.10 mm per surface (0.20 mm per contact point): Removing only 0.10 mm per surface is unnecessarily conservative and would yield only 1.2 mm across six contacts, failing to relieve her 2.5 mm crowding.
  • C. 0.50 mm per surface (1.00 mm per contact point): Stripping 0.50 mm from a single lower incisor proximal surface removes almost the entire enamel layer, exposing underlying dentine and causing thermal hypersensitivity.
  • D. 0.75 mm per surface (1.50 mm per contact point): Removing 0.75 mm per surface penetrates deeply into coronal dentine on mandibular incisors, where total proximal enamel thickness is only 0.5 to 0.7 mm.
  • E. 1.00 mm per surface (2.00 mm per contact point): Stripping 2 mm at a single contact point destroys the anatomical crown contour, eliminates the interdental septal bone space, and exposes dentine near the pulp.

Key point: State the safe clinical limit of 0.25 mm per tooth surface (0.5 mm per contact point) for anterior interproximal enamel reduction.

Question 2 · Growth, development and malocclusion · easy

A 14-year-old boy attends a dental teaching hospital in Peshawar with his monozygotic twin brother for an orthodontic consultation. Both brothers exhibit a Class III incisor relationship with a reverse overjet of 3 mm, a Class III molar relationship bilaterally, and a concave profile with an ANB angle of -3 degrees (normal 3 ± 2 degrees). However, one brother has 6 mm of maxillary arch crowding with a palatally displaced maxillary right lateral incisor (12), whereas the other has well-aligned arches.

Which craniofacial feature observed in these twins has the highest genetic heritability?

  1. A. Anteroposterior skeletal jaw relationship
  2. B. Maxillary dental arch perimeter and available space
  3. C. Palatal displacement of the lateral incisor
  4. D. Overbite depth in millimetres
  5. E. Degree of incisor alignment and crowding
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Anteroposterior skeletal jaw relationship

Twin studies comparing monozygotic and dizygotic twins (such as those by Lundstrom and Corruccini) demonstrate a clear gradient of heritability across craniofacial traits. Skeletal craniofacial dimensions, particularly the anteroposterior and vertical jaw relationships (such as mandibular prognathism seen in familial Class III patterns), are under strong polygenic control and show high concordance in monozygotic twins. Conversely, dental arch alignment, crowding, and rotational displacements show much lower genetic heritability and are predominantly shaped by local and environmental influences, including resting soft-tissue pressures, dietary consistency, interproximal caries, and premature loss of primary teeth. Consequently, monozygotic twins frequently share virtually identical skeletal profiles while displaying marked differences in dental crowding.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Maxillary dental arch perimeter and available space: Arch perimeter is heavily modified by local environmental factors such as caries, interproximal attrition, and early primary tooth loss in one twin.
  • C. Palatal displacement of the lateral incisor: Local deflection of a single incisor differed between these genetically identical twins, demonstrating a local environmental or eruptive path cause.
  • D. Overbite depth in millimetres: Overbite depth is substantially influenced by variable soft-tissue posture, adaptive tongue resting position, and digit sucking habits rather than genes alone.
  • E. Degree of incisor alignment and crowding: Dental crowding was discordant between these monozygotic twins, reflecting strong environmental and soft-tissue influences on arch alignment.

Key point: Distinguish between highly heritable skeletal craniofacial traits and environmentally influenced occlusal alignment in twin studies.

Question 3 · Orthodontic appliances and treatment · medium

A 13-year-old girl completes nine months of full-time Twin Block functional appliance therapy at a dental teaching hospital in Islamabad, reducing her initial 10 mm Class II division 1 overjet down to 2 mm and achieving a Class I molar relationship at the permanent first molars (16, 26, 36, and 46). Post-functional lateral cephalometric superimposition is performed to evaluate how much of the 8 mm overjet correction came from skeletal jaw changes versus tooth movement.

How is overjet reduction with functional appliances split between dentoalveolar and skeletal change?

  1. A. Over 90% of the correction is true skeletal elongation of the mandibular corpus, with zero tipping of the upper or lower incisors
  2. B. Approximately 50% comes from forward growth of the maxilla and 50% comes from lingual retroclination of the mandibular incisors
  3. C. About 70% to 80% comes from dentoalveolar tipping of the incisors and molars, and 20% to 30% from skeletal change
  4. D. 100% of the correction results from anterior relocation of the temporomandibular joint glenoid fossa into the zygomatic arch
  5. E. Approximately 80% comes from intrusion of the mandibular first molars and 20% from labial proclination of the maxillary incisors
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. About 70% to 80% comes from dentoalveolar tipping of the incisors and molars, and 20% to 30% from skeletal change

When a functional appliance postures the mandible forward, stretch of the elevator and retractor muscles of mastication and the surrounding soft-tissue envelope generates a reciprocal force system transmitted through the appliance to the teeth and jaws. Systematic reviews and randomized controlled trials demonstrate that approximately 70% to 80% of the resulting overjet correction is dentoalveolar in nature: the maxillary incisors (11, 12, 21, and 22) retrocline palatally, the mandibular incisors (31, 32, 41, and 42) procline labially, and differential eruption allows the mandibular molars (36 and 46) to erupt upward and forward while restraining maxillary molar eruption. Only 20% to 30% of the correction (typically 1 to 2 mm clinically) represents net skeletal change from accelerated condylar growth, glenoid fossa remodelling, and mild maxillary restraint.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Over 90% of the correction is true skeletal elongation of the mandibular corpus, with zero tipping of the upper or lower incisors: Randomized clinical trials consistently show that true skeletal mandibular growth increment is modest (1 to 2 mm), whereas incisor tipping accounts for the majority of change.
  • B. Approximately 50% comes from forward growth of the maxilla and 50% comes from lingual retroclination of the mandibular incisors: Functional appliances exert a mild headgear-like restraint on the maxilla rather than forward maxillary growth, and they procline rather than retrocline the lower incisors.
  • D. 100% of the correction results from anterior relocation of the temporomandibular joint glenoid fossa into the zygomatic arch: While minor glenoid fossa bone remodelling occurs on the posterior wall, dentoalveolar tipping and differential molar eruption dominate the clinical correction.
  • E. Approximately 80% comes from intrusion of the mandibular first molars and 20% from labial proclination of the maxillary incisors: Functional appliances encourage upward and forward eruption (extrusion) of the lower molars rather than intrusion, and they retrocline the upper incisors.

Key point: Quantify the relative dentoalveolar (70%–80%) and skeletal (20%–30%) contributions of functional appliances to Class II division 1 overjet reduction.

Question 4 · Orthodontic assessment and treatment planning · medium

A 15-year-old boy is brought by his father to an orthodontic clinic in Mirpur (AJK). He has a Class II division 1 incisor relationship with a 6 mm overjet on the upper right central incisor (11), competent lips, and 5 mm of mild crowding (IOTN Dental Health Component 3d, Aesthetic Component 5). Although his father strongly insists on having fixed appliances placed immediately before a family wedding, the 15-year-old boy clearly understands the procedure, shows no personal concern about his teeth, and states firmly that he does not want braces.

How should the orthodontist proceed?

  1. A. Bond the fixed appliances immediately on the father’s written signature alone
  2. B. Treat because the father, as guardian, has given consent
  3. C. Perform premolar extractions today and wait for the boy to change his mind later
  4. D. Defer treatment until the boy himself is motivated and gives consent
  5. E. Begin with a removable appliance to win his cooperation
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. Defer treatment until the boy himself is motivated and gives consent

Valid informed consent for orthodontic treatment requires that the patient (and parents or legal guardians in the case of a minor) be given clear information in understandable terms regarding the nature of the malocclusion, the proposed treatment plan, all reasonable alternatives (including no treatment), the commitment required (oral hygiene, diet, regular attendance, and long-term retention), and the material risks (root resorption, decalcification, and relapse). Under the principle of Gillick competence and ethical orthodontic guidelines, an adolescent who has sufficient maturity and intelligence to understand the nature and implications of treatment plays a central role in decision-making. Because orthodontic treatment is elective and depends entirely on daily patient cooperation over years, if a parent wants treatment but the adolescent refuses braces, treatment must be deferred until the patient is personally motivated.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Bond the fixed appliances immediately on the father’s written signature alone: Forcing an elective 18-to-24-month appliance treatment on an unwilling, competent adolescent violates patient autonomy and almost guarantees poor compliance, decalcification, and relapse.
  • B. Treat because the father, as guardian, has given consent: A competent 15-year-old who refuses elective treatment should not be treated against his wishes, even with parental consent.
  • C. Perform premolar extractions today and wait for the boy to change his mind later: Extracting healthy permanent teeth in an unmotivated patient who refuses fixed appliances is unethical and leaves unclosed extraction spaces and occlusal tipping.
  • E. Begin with a removable appliance to win his cooperation: Any appliance still needs his consent and daily cooperation; starting treatment he has refused is neither ethical nor likely to succeed.

Key point: Apply ethical consent principles when a parent demands elective orthodontic treatment for an unwilling, competent adolescent.

Question 5 · Growth, development and malocclusion · medium

A 19-year-old male university student in Islamabad attends for an orthodontic review. He has a Class I skeletal pattern with an ANB angle of 3 degrees (normal 3 ± 2 degrees), an overjet of 3 mm, an overbite of 3 mm, and well-aligned dental arches. Intraoral force transducer measurements record that during swallowing his tongue exerts a labial pressure of 120 g/cm² against the lingual surfaces of the mandibular left and right central incisors (31 and 41), whereas resting lower lip pressure is 10 g/cm², yet 31 and 41 remain completely stable.

Why does swallowing not procline his lower incisors?

  1. A. Total daily duration of swallowing falls below the threshold for tooth movement
  2. B. Periodontal ligament collagen fibres permanently neutralise all lingual forces
  3. C. Cortical bone of the mandibular symphysis resists labial remodelling
  4. D. Occlusal forces from the maxillary incisors lock the lower crowns
  5. E. Active contraction of the orbicularis oris during swallowing equals tongue pressure
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Total daily duration of swallowing falls below the threshold for tooth movement

According to Proffit and Weinstein equilibrium theory of tooth position, the duration of a force is far more critical than its magnitude in determining whether biological tooth movement occurs. Experimental and clinical evidence demonstrates that a force must act continuously or near-continuously for a threshold duration of at least 4 to 6 hours per day to initiate osteoclastic and osteoblastic remodelling within the periodontal ligament. Although the tongue generates heavy pressures (over 100 g/cm²) against the incisors during deglutition and speech, each swallow lasts only about 1 second (totalling roughly 15 to 20 minutes over 24 hours). Conversely, light resting pressures (5 to 15 g/cm²) from the lips, cheeks, and resting tongue act continuously for 24 hours a day and therefore govern equilibrium tooth position.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Periodontal ligament collagen fibres permanently neutralise all lingual forces: Collagen fibres remodel readily when subjected to sustained light forces, so they do not permanently block sustained lingual pressures.
  • C. Cortical bone of the mandibular symphysis resists labial remodelling: Mandibular incisors readily procline when subjected to sustained light resting pressures, proving the symphyseal bone can remodel.
  • D. Occlusal forces from the maxillary incisors lock the lower crowns: In a normal 3 mm overjet, the mandibular incisor crowns lie lingual to the maxillary incisors and are not blocked from moving labially.
  • E. Active contraction of the orbicularis oris during swallowing equals tongue pressure: Lip pressure during normal somatic swallowing is much smaller than tongue pressure and does not match 120 g/cm² ounce-for-ounce.

Key point: Apply equilibrium theory to explain why continuous resting soft-tissue pressures govern tooth position rather than intermittent heavy functional forces.

Question 6 · Orthodontic appliances and treatment · medium

A 21-year-old man presents to a dental teaching hospital in Charsadda for combined orthodontic and orthognathic surgical correction of a severe skeletal Class III malocclusion. Cephalometry shows an ANB of -6 degrees (normal 3 ± 2 degrees), upper right central incisor (11) proclined at 123 degrees to the maxillary plane (normal 109 ± 6 degrees), and lower right central incisor (41) retroclined at 77 degrees to the mandibular plane (normal 93 ± 6 degrees). His pre-treatment reverse overjet is 3 mm.

How are the incisors moved before surgery, and what happens to the reverse overjet?

  1. A. Retrocline upper, procline lower; reverse overjet increases
  2. B. Procline the upper and retrocline the lower incisors further
  3. C. Leave the incisors and operate before bonding
  4. D. Procline both to remove the reverse overjet before surgery
  5. E. Retrocline both upper and lower incisors
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Retrocline upper, procline lower; reverse overjet increases

In a patient with a severe skeletal Class III discrepancy (ANB -6 degrees), the soft-tissue envelope naturally masks part of the jaw discrepancy through dentoalveolar compensation: the upper incisors procline (here 123 degrees, normal 109 ± 6 degrees) and the lower incisors retrocline (here 77 degrees, normal 93 ± 6 degrees). If orthognathic surgery were attempted immediately, the incisors would collide into a positive overjet long before the maxilla and mandible reached a normal Class I skeletal relationship. Therefore, presurgical orthodontic decompensation reverses these natural compensations by retroclining the upper incisors (often requiring upper first premolar extractions) and proclining the lower incisors back to normal axial inclinations relative to their respective jaw bases. Patients must be counselled beforehand that decompensation temporarily makes their reverse overjet and facial profile look worse until the day of surgery.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Procline the upper and retrocline the lower incisors further: Further proclining 11 and retroclining 41 increases dentoalveolar compensation rather than decompensating the arches, preventing the surgeon from moving the jaws by the true skeletal amount.
  • C. Leave the incisors and operate before bonding: Without presurgical decompensation, the compensated incisor inclinations block the jaws from being repositioned into a Class I skeletal relationship during surgery.
  • D. Procline both to remove the reverse overjet before surgery: Eliminating the reverse overjet before surgery leaves zero horizontal space for the osteotomy movements, so the jaws cannot be advanced or set back at operation.
  • E. Retrocline both upper and lower incisors: The lower incisors are retroclined as compensation for the Class III base; decompensation proclines them, so retroclining them further is the wrong direction.

Key point: Explain how presurgical orthodontic decompensation in a skeletal Class III patient retroclines the upper incisors, proclines the lower incisors, and temporarily worsens the reverse overjet.

Question 7 · Orthodontic assessment and treatment planning · easy

A 10-year-old girl attends a dental clinic in Mirpur for an initial orthodontic examination. While seated upright in natural head posture, she is asked to relax her jaw as the clinician gently guides her chin onto the retruded arc of closure until the very first tooth contact occurs on the palatal cusp of the maxillary left primary second molar (65). When asked to bite completely together from that point, her mandible slides 3 mm anteriorly and to the left into maximum intercuspation, where she exhibits a unilateral crossbite of 65 and the maxillary left first permanent molar (26).

What specific diagnostic parameter is the clinician recording during this step of the examination?

  1. A. Mandibular displacement from the retruded contact position to the intercuspal position
  2. B. Vertical interocclusal rest space between postural rest position and occlusion
  3. C. Maximum unassisted interincisal mouth opening for temporomandibular joint screening
  4. D. Bennett angle of the non-working mandibular condyle during lateral excursion
  5. E. Sagittal Frankfort-mandibular plane angle measured in habitual centric occlusion
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Mandibular displacement from the retruded contact position to the intercuspal position

During a routine clinical orthodontic examination, the mandibular path of closure must be assessed from the physiological rest position and from the retruded contact position (centric relation) into the intercuspal position (centric occlusion). If a premature occlusal contact exists—most commonly on an instanding incisor or a slightly narrow maxillary deciduous canine or molar (such as 65)—the patient reflexly deviates the mandible anteriorly or laterally to achieve maximum intercuspation. Recording the presence, direction, and magnitude in millimetres of any discrepancy between the retruded contact position and the intercuspal position is essential for two reasons: first, it reveals the true underlying skeletal and dental arch relationships (which are otherwise masked by the posture); second, under the Index of Orthodontic Treatment Need (IOTN), a discrepancy greater than 2 mm upgrades a crossbite to Grade 4c.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Vertical interocclusal rest space between postural rest position and occlusion: Freeway space (interocclusal rest space) is measured vertically from the unguided physiological rest position of the mandible, not from the first premature tooth contact.
  • C. Maximum unassisted interincisal mouth opening for temporomandibular joint screening: Maximum mouth opening measures vertical mandibular translation and rotation in millimetres rather than the occlusal slide from centric relation to centric occlusion.
  • D. Bennett angle of the non-working mandibular condyle during lateral excursion: The Bennett angle is a protrusive/lateral condylar guidance parameter measured on a semi-adjustable articulator, not a premature contact slide on closure.
  • E. Sagittal Frankfort-mandibular plane angle measured in habitual centric occlusion: The Frankfort-mandibular plane angle is an extraoral vertical skeletal measurement between facial planes, not an intraoral occlusal slide assessment.

Key point: Describe the clinical technique and diagnostic importance of detecting a mandibular displacement from retruded contact position to intercuspal position.

Question 8 · Growth, development and malocclusion · easy

A 7-year-old boy is brought to a dental teaching hospital in Peshawar by his father, who is concerned that his newly erupting upper right permanent central incisor (11) and upper left permanent central incisor (21) look disproportionately large compared with his small lower jaw. Extraoral examination shows that his cranial vault circumference is already nearly adult size, whereas his midface and mandible remain juvenile in vertical and anteroposterior dimensions.

Which postnatal growth trajectory accounts for his calvarium reaching nearly adult size by this age?

  1. A. General somatic growth curve
  2. B. Neural (brain) growth curve
  3. C. Lymphoid growth curve
  4. D. Genital growth curve
  5. E. Constant linear cartilaginous trajectory
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Neural (brain) growth curve

Scammon divided postnatal body tissues into four fundamental growth curves: neural, general (somatic), lymphoid, and genital. The brain, eyeballs, and surrounding cranial vault follow the neural curve, growing rapidly in infancy and early childhood so that by 6 to 7 years of age they have reached approximately 90% to 95% of their final adult dimension. In contrast, the maxilla and mandible follow the general somatic curve closely, lagging behind the cranium in early childhood and accelerating during the pubertal growth spurt. This cephalocaudal growth gradient explains why a 7-year-old child has an almost adult-sized forehead and cranium alongside a smaller face in which newly erupted permanent incisors initially appear large.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. General somatic growth curve: Somatic tissues follow an S-shaped trajectory peaking at puberty, which describes his still-immature mandible rather than his nearly adult-sized cranial vault.
  • C. Lymphoid growth curve: Lymphoid tissues overshoot to nearly double adult mass in late childhood before involuting, unlike the cranial vault which plateaus without regression.
  • D. Genital growth curve: Reproductive tissues remain quiescent throughout early childhood and accelerate only at puberty, whereas his cranial vault is already nearly complete at age seven.
  • E. Constant linear cartilaginous trajectory: Postnatal craniofacial systems grow at differential organ-specific velocities rather than following a uniform straight-line rate across childhood.

Key point: Explain how the neural and somatic curves of Scammon produce differential postnatal growth between the cranial vault and the jaws.

Question 9 · Orthodontic appliances and treatment · easy

A 14-year-old boy attends a dental teaching hospital in Peshawar for retraction of his maxillary right permanent canine (13) into the extraction space of the maxillary right first premolar (14). The orthodontist applies a continuous light force of 60 g so that periodontal ligament blood vessels on the distal aspect remain patent while compressed to about one-third of their original width. After 48 hours of steady loading, tooth movement begins smoothly without a delay phase.

Which process removes bone on the compressed side of the root?

  1. A. Frontal (direct) osteoclastic resorption of the socket wall
  2. B. Undermining resorption from neighbouring marrow spaces
  3. C. Direct osteocytic osteolysis deep within cortical Haversian canals
  4. D. Cementoclastic resorption of the compressed root surface layer
  5. E. Fibroblastic hyalinisation of the principal collagenous fibres
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Frontal (direct) osteoclastic resorption of the socket wall

According to the pressure-tension theory of orthodontic tooth movement, applying an optimal light continuous force compresses the periodontal ligament on the side toward which the tooth moves without completely occluding its capillary blood supply. Blood flow is reduced, altering local oxygen tension and releasing chemical messengers including prostaglandins, cytokines, and receptor activator of nuclear factor kappa-B ligand (RANKL). Within 36 to 72 hours, multinucleated osteoclasts differentiate from circulating monocytes directly within the compressed periodontal ligament space and attack the adjacent alveolar lamina dura (bundle bone). This process is termed frontal (direct) resorption and produces smooth, continuous tooth movement with minimal discomfort or root damage.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Undermining resorption from neighbouring marrow spaces: Bone removal from deep marrow spaces occurs only when excessive force obliterates periodontal capillaries and creates an acellular necrotic zone.
  • C. Direct osteocytic osteolysis deep within cortical Haversian canals: Internal lacunar remodelling by osteocytes cannot resorb the bundle bone wall bordering the periodontal ligament space during tooth translation.
  • D. Cementoclastic resorption of the compressed root surface layer: Cementum is more resistant to resorption than alveolar bone and is spared when light physiological forces preserve periodontal vascularity.
  • E. Fibroblastic hyalinisation of the principal collagenous fibres: Cell-free glass-like degeneration of collagen fibrils represents tissue necrosis caused by crushing heavy forces rather than physiological bone remodelling.

Key point: Explain how light continuous orthodontic forces preserve periodontal vascularity and induce frontal resorption along the compressed lamina dura.

Question 10 · Orthodontic assessment and treatment planning · easy

A 13-year-old boy attends a dental teaching hospital in Karachi for an extraoral orthodontic examination. He is seated upright without head support, looking straight ahead at a distant point at eye level so that his Frankfort plane (running from the superior margin of the external auditory meatus to the inferior border of the infraorbital rim) is horizontal. Using the index and middle fingers inside the labial sulci, the orthodontist palpates the deepest midline concavities of the anterior maxilla and mandible while retracting the lips.

In a Class I skeletal pattern, how do these two bony points normally relate?

  1. A. The mandibular point lies 4 to 6 mm anterior to the maxillary point
  2. B. The maxillary point lies 8 to 10 mm anterior to the mandibular point
  3. C. The maxillary point lies 2 to 4 mm anterior to the mandibular point
  4. D. Both points lie on a line angled 45 degrees backward to the Frankfort plane
  5. E. The mandibular point coincides vertically with the anterior tip of the nasal bone
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. The maxillary point lies 2 to 4 mm anterior to the mandibular point

Clinical extraoral assessment of the anteroposterior skeletal pattern is performed chairside with the patient seated upright in natural head posture (looking at a distant object at eye level, which approximates a horizontal Frankfort plane). To avoid being misled by variations in soft-tissue lip thickness or incisor proclination, the clinician retracts the lips and palpates the deepest midline concavity of the anterior maxilla (corresponding to cephalometric subspinale, point A) with the index finger and the deepest midline concavity of the anterior mandible (supramentale, point B) with the middle finger. In a balanced Class I skeletal relationship, the maxillary point A lies 2 to 4 mm anterior to the mandibular point B. If point A lies more than 4 mm ahead of point B, the skeletal pattern is Class II; if less than 2 mm, it is Class III.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. The mandibular point lies 4 to 6 mm anterior to the maxillary point: When the mandibular sulcus (soft-tissue B-point) lies 4 to 6 mm anterior to the maxillary sulcus, the patient has a severe Class III skeletal pattern.
  • B. The maxillary point lies 8 to 10 mm anterior to the mandibular point: An 8 to 10 mm anterior separation of the maxillary sulcus relative to the mandibular sulcus indicates a severe Class II skeletal discrepancy.
  • D. Both points lie on a line angled 45 degrees backward to the Frankfort plane: A 45-degree backward inclination between point A and point B would represent extreme mandibular retrognathia (severe Class II).
  • E. The mandibular point coincides vertically with the anterior tip of the nasal bone: Comparing the mandibular alveolus to the nasal bone tip does not assess the maxillomandibular skeletal base relationship.

Key point: Perform clinical chairside palpation of points A and B in natural head posture to determine the anteroposterior skeletal base relationship.
