Question 1 · Paediatric dentistry · easy

A 2-day-old baby is brought to a dental teaching hospital in Peshawar by worried parents. A firm, well-formed tooth is present in the mandibular anterior region at birth. The mother reports no difficulty with breastfeeding and no ulceration of the tongue. The baby is otherwise healthy with no bleeding history.

What is the most appropriate management of this tooth?

  1. A. Extract it now under local anaesthesia
  2. B. Extract it after vitamin K
  3. C. Observe; extract only if it loosens
  4. D. Urgent cardiac assessment
  5. E. Topical antibiotic gel around the tooth
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Observe; extract only if it loosens

Natal teeth are present at birth, most often mandibular central incisors. If the tooth is firm, not interfering with feeding, and not causing ulceration of the ventral tongue (Riga-Fede disease), no active treatment is required; parents are reassured and the tooth is reviewed. Extraction is reserved for mobile teeth that pose an aspiration risk or for teeth causing significant feeding trauma. Unnecessary extraction in a neonate is avoided because of bleeding risks and the possibility that the tooth is part of the normal primary dentition.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Extract it now under local anaesthesia: Extraction is not needed for a firm, asymptomatic natal tooth and creates unnecessary risk in a newborn.
  • B. Extract it after vitamin K: Vitamin K is considered if extraction is required, but this firm tooth does not meet criteria for removal.
  • D. Urgent cardiac assessment: Most natal teeth are isolated and not associated with cardiac defects; no dysmorphic features are described.
  • E. Topical antibiotic gel around the tooth: Antibiotics are not indicated for an intact natal tooth with no signs of infection.

Key point: To identify that a firm, asymptomatic natal tooth in a newborn is managed by observation rather than routine extraction.

Question 2 · Paediatric dentistry · medium

An 8-year-old girl returns to a general practice in Bannu six months after a band and loop was fitted following loss of her upper right primary first molar (54). The band on 55 is loose, and the gingiva around it is inflamed.

What should be done first?

  1. A. Re-cement the loose band immediately
  2. B. Replace it with a lingual holding arch
  3. C. Leave the loose band in place until the permanent successor erupts and then remove it
  4. D. Remove the band, restore any caries, and re-cement or remake the maintainer
  5. E. Extract the abutment tooth and remove the maintainer
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. Remove the band, restore any caries, and re-cement or remake the maintainer

A loose band on a space maintainer with gingival inflammation suggests plaque accumulation and possible caries beneath the band. The appliance should be removed, the abutment tooth examined and restored if needed, and the space maintainer re-cemented or replaced. Leaving it loose can lead to decalcification, caries, and periodontal problems. A lingual holding arch is not indicated for a unilateral issue, and extracting a sound abutment is inappropriate.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Re-cement the loose band immediately: Re-cementing without assessing for caries under the band risks trapping decay and worsening gingival inflammation.
  • B. Replace it with a lingual holding arch: A lingual holding arch is for bilateral loss and is not indicated for a unilateral loose band.
  • C. Leave the loose band in place until the permanent successor erupts and then remove it: A loose band with inflamed gingiva should not be left in place; it can cause caries and periodontal damage.
  • E. Extract the abutment tooth and remove the maintainer: Extraction of a sound abutment is unnecessary and would create further space problems.

Key point: Manage a loose band and loop space maintainer with inflamed gingiva.

Question 3 · Paediatric dentistry · easy

A 4-year-old girl attends a general practice in Abbottabad for a fissure sealant on 65, the upper left second primary molar. She sits quietly and opens her mouth when asked. The dentist praises her several times during the procedure and gives her a sticker afterwards.

Which behaviour-guidance technique is being used?

  1. A. Positive reinforcement
  2. B. Voice control
  3. C. Tell-show-do
  4. D. Distraction
  5. E. Nitrous oxide sedation
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Positive reinforcement

Positive reinforcement rewards the behaviour the dentist wants, using praise, a sticker or a small prize, so that the behaviour is repeated at later visits. It is used with every child, not only anxious ones, and should follow the cooperative behaviour promptly. Voice control, tell-show-do and distraction are different non-pharmacological techniques, and nitrous oxide sedation is pharmacological. A cooperative child who is praised and rewarded is more likely to cope with later treatment.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Voice control: Voice control is a deliberate change in voice volume or tone to gain attention, which is not described.
  • C. Tell-show-do: Tell-show-do is an explanation, demonstration and then treatment sequence, not praise or a reward.
  • D. Distraction: Distraction diverts attention from the procedure, for example with a video or a story.
  • E. Nitrous oxide sedation: Sedation is a pharmacological technique; this child needed no drug.

Key point: State the correct order of tell-show-do steps.

Question 4 · Paediatric dentistry · easy

A 7-year-old boy is brought by his uncle to a dental clinic in Mardan. He has multiple fractured anterior teeth, a healing lip laceration, and bruising on the upper arms in different stages. He is withdrawn and avoids eye contact. The uncle gives a vague history of falling down stairs.

Which finding is most suggestive of non-accidental injury?

  1. A. Bruises of different ages
  2. B. Fractured anterior teeth
  3. C. Healing lip laceration
  4. D. Withdrawn behaviour
  5. E. Vague history from the uncle
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Bruises of different ages

Bruises on the trunk, ears, neck, or upper arms, especially when they are in different colours, are a warning sign of non-accidental injury. Accidental bruises in children usually occur over bony prominences such as the forehead, knees, and shins after a fall. The pattern in this story, with bruises of different ages on soft tissue and a vague history, suggests repeated trauma. Dental professionals have a duty to recognise and act on suspected abuse rather than accept an implausible explanation. Multiple fractured teeth and a lip laceration can happen accidentally, so the bruising pattern is the most reliable clue in this child.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Fractured anterior teeth: Fractured anterior teeth can result from accidental falls or caries and are not specific to abuse.
  • C. Healing lip laceration: A healing lip laceration may occur from a fall and does not by itself indicate non-accidental injury.
  • D. Withdrawn behaviour: Withdrawn behaviour is nonspecific and can occur in anxious or shy children without abuse.
  • E. Vague history from the uncle: A vague history raises concern but is less objective than a bruising pattern in different stages.

Key point: Recognise bruising patterns that suggest non-accidental injury in a child.

Question 5 · Paediatric dentistry · easy

A 10-year-old boy with Down syndrome attends a general practice in Islamabad. He has a carious lesion on 36, the lower left first permanent molar, and is cooperative with simple instructions. He has no cardiac symptoms.

Which factor is most important before planning restorative treatment under local anaesthesia?

  1. A. Checking his Frankl score
  2. B. Requesting a full blood count
  3. C. Scheduling treatment in the afternoon
  4. D. Using only glass ionomer cement for all restorations
  5. E. Cardiac status and need for antibiotic prophylaxis
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Cardiac status and need for antibiotic prophylaxis

Children with Down syndrome commonly have congenital heart defects, some of which increase the risk of infective endocarditis after dental treatment. Before providing local anaesthesia or restorative care, the dentist should confirm the cardiac status and whether antibiotic prophylaxis is recommended. Behaviour assessment, blood tests, the time of day, and the choice of restorative material do not replace this medical evaluation. A thorough history and, when needed, consultation with a paediatric cardiologist ensure safe treatment. Down syndrome alone does not contraindicate routine dentistry, but associated heart disease must be excluded first.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Checking his Frankl score: The Frankl score assesses behaviour and is not a medical prerequisite before local anaesthesia.
  • B. Requesting a full blood count: A full blood count is not routinely required before restorative treatment in a child with Down syndrome.
  • C. Scheduling treatment in the afternoon: The time of day does not determine the need for cardiac assessment or antibiotic prophylaxis.
  • D. Using only glass ionomer cement for all restorations: The choice of restorative material depends on the lesion, not on the presence of Down syndrome.

Key point: Confirm cardiac status before treating a child with Down syndrome.

Question 6 · Paediatric dentistry · easy

A 2-year-old girl is brought to a dental teaching hospital in Peshawar by her mother. The child has slept with a milk bottle every night since infancy. The mother noticed chalky white marks on the upper front teeth two months ago. The child has no pain, swelling or fever. Examination shows white spot lesions on the labial cervical third of 51, 52, 61 and 62 with intact surfaces; the lower incisors are normal. There is no medical history.

What is the most appropriate initial management?

  1. A. Dietary counselling and fluoride varnish application
  2. B. No treatment until the permanent incisors erupt
  3. C. Extraction of 51, 52, 61 and 62 with a space maintainer
  4. D. Stainless steel crowns on all four maxillary incisors
  5. E. Pulpotomy on 51, 52, 61 and 62 followed by crowns
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Dietary counselling and fluoride varnish application

Early childhood caries often begins as non-cavitated white spot lesions on maxillary incisors associated with night bottle feeding. First-line management is behavioural—stopping night bottle feeding and shifting to cup drinking—combined with professional fluoride varnish to promote remineralisation. Restorative treatment, pulp therapy or extraction is reserved for cavitated lesions, pain, swelling or pulp involvement. In Pakistan, prolonged bottle feeding and sugary milk are common, so counselling the mother is essential. No radiographs are needed unless clinical signs suggest deeper involvement.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. No treatment until the permanent incisors erupt: The lesions are active and non-cavitated; waiting risks progression to dentine involvement and pain in this toddler.
  • C. Extraction of 51, 52, 61 and 62 with a space maintainer: Extraction is not indicated for asymptomatic, non-cavitated anterior lesions in a two-year-old, and a space maintainer is rarely needed for incisors.
  • D. Stainless steel crowns on all four maxillary incisors: Crowns are for cavitated multi-surface lesions or after pulp therapy, not for intact white spot lesions.
  • E. Pulpotomy on 51, 52, 61 and 62 followed by crowns: Pulpotomy requires pulp exposure or irreversible pulpitis; this child has no symptoms and intact enamel surfaces.

Key point: Identify first-line non-operative management of early childhood caries.

Question 7 · Paediatric dentistry · easy

A 6-year-old boy attends a dental teaching hospital in Mardan. He has a carious lesion on the occlusal and mesial surface of 85, the lower right second primary molar. The child is cooperative but anxious. The tooth is vital with no periapical pathology. The dentist plans to place a stainless steel crown without caries removal or local anaesthesia.

Which technique is being described?

  1. A. Conventional stainless steel crown preparation
  2. B. Open sandwich restoration
  3. C. Direct pulp capping
  4. D. Atraumatic restorative treatment
  5. E. Hall technique
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Hall technique

The Hall technique is a minimally invasive method for managing carious primary molars by sealing caries under a preformed stainless steel crown. No caries removal, local anaesthesia or tooth preparation is needed; the crown is cemented with glass ionomer after separating the teeth. It is suitable for vital primary molars with no signs of pulp necrosis or periapical pathology. It arrests caries by depriving bacteria of nutrients. In Pakistani practice, it is useful where chair time and behaviour management are challenging. Conventional crowns, ART and pulp capping are different procedures.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Conventional stainless steel crown preparation: Conventional crown preparation requires caries removal, tooth reduction and usually local anaesthesia, which this plan avoids.
  • B. Open sandwich restoration: The open sandwich technique uses glass ionomer and composite for class II cavities, not crown placement without caries removal.
  • C. Direct pulp capping: Direct pulp capping is for a small mechanical or carious pulp exposure in a vital tooth, not a crown technique.
  • D. Atraumatic restorative treatment: Atraumatic restorative treatment uses hand instruments and glass ionomer for cavitated lesions, not a preformed metal crown.

Key point: Identify the Hall technique for primary molar crown placement.

Question 8 · Paediatric dentistry · easy

A 4-year-old girl attends a private dental clinic in Quetta after her upper right primary central incisor (51) was avulsed in a fall at home. Her mother brings the tooth in a tissue. The child is otherwise well, and there is no injury to the lip or alveolus.

What should the dentist do?

  1. A. Replant the tooth and splint it for one week
  2. B. Replant the tooth without splinting
  3. C. Replant the tooth and prescribe systemic antibiotics and analgesics for five days
  4. D. Replant the tooth and perform a pulpectomy
  5. E. Do not replant the tooth and monitor the developing permanent incisor
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Do not replant the tooth and monitor the developing permanent incisor

IADT 2020 guidelines state that an avulsed primary tooth should never be replanted. Replantation can damage the developing permanent incisor, causing enamel hypoplasia, dilaceration, or eruption disturbance. The tooth should not be stored or replanted; the parent should be reassured and the child reviewed periodically to monitor the successor. Space maintenance is usually not required for a primary incisor.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Replant the tooth and splint it for one week: An avulsed primary tooth must never be replanted because of the risk of damage to the developing permanent tooth bud.
  • B. Replant the tooth without splinting: Replantation without splinting is still replantation and is contraindicated for a primary tooth.
  • C. Replant the tooth and prescribe systemic antibiotics and analgesics for five days: Antibiotics do not make replantation of a primary tooth safe or successful.
  • D. Replant the tooth and perform a pulpectomy: Pulpectomy is irrelevant because the tooth should not be replanted at all.

Key point: Know that avulsed primary teeth are never replanted.

Question 9 · Paediatric dentistry · easy

A 7-year-old girl attends a dental teaching hospital in Mansehra after extraction of her lower left primary first molar (74). Her lower left first permanent molar (36) has erupted, but her lower left first premolar (34) will not erupt for several years.

Which space maintainer should be used?

  1. A. Crown and loop
  2. B. Band and loop
  3. C. Distal shoe
  4. D. Lingual holding arch
  5. E. No space maintainer
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Band and loop

A band and loop is a fixed, unilateral space maintainer indicated after loss of a single primary molar when the adjacent abutment tooth is sound. It holds the space for the succeeding premolar until eruption. A crown and loop is used if the abutment needs a crown. A distal shoe guides an unerupted permanent first molar. A lingual holding arch is used for bilateral posterior space loss. No maintainer would risk mesial drift and loss of arch length.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Crown and loop: A crown and loop is chosen when the abutment tooth is heavily restored or carious, which is not described here.
  • C. Distal shoe: A distal shoe is used when the permanent first molar has not yet erupted after loss of the primary second molar.
  • D. Lingual holding arch: A lingual holding arch is for bilateral posterior tooth loss, not a single unilateral space.
  • E. No space maintainer: Space loss is likely because the permanent premolar is years away from eruption.

Key point: Select a band and loop for unilateral primary molar space loss.

Question 10 · Paediatric dentistry · easy

A 5-year-old girl attends a private dental clinic in Hyderabad after losing her upper right primary central incisor (51) due to trauma. The permanent successor is developing normally, and there is no crowding.

What should be done?

  1. A. No space maintainer is routinely required
  2. B. Removable acrylic space maintainer with a false tooth
  3. C. Band and loop
  4. D. Distal shoe
  5. E. Lingual holding arch
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. No space maintainer is routinely required

Premature loss of a primary incisor rarely causes significant space loss, and a space maintainer is not routinely indicated unless there is esthetic concern or abnormal arch conditions. The developing permanent incisor should be monitored. Band and loop, distal shoe, and lingual holding arch are posterior appliances. Removable acrylic maintainers are not standard for this situation.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Removable acrylic space maintainer with a false tooth: Removable space maintainers are rarely used for primary incisors and are not routinely required.
  • C. Band and loop: A band and loop is for posterior primary teeth, not anterior incisors.
  • D. Distal shoe: A distal shoe is for an unerupted permanent first molar after loss of a primary second molar.
  • E. Lingual holding arch: A lingual holding arch is for bilateral posterior space loss in the arch.

Key point: Know that primary incisor loss usually needs no space maintainer.
