Question 1 · General microbiology · easy

A 9-day-old baby born at home in a village in Sindh is brought with poor sucking for 2 days, then stiffness and spasms when touched. His jaw is clenched and his back arches. The cord was cut with an unsterilised blade and dressed with ghee. His mother never received any vaccine in pregnancy. A Lady Health Worker explains that injections given to the mother would have protected him.

Which property of the toxin makes this prevention possible?

  1. A. It is heat-stable lipid
  2. B. It is released at death
  3. C. It is part of the cell wall
  4. D. It acts in the gut
  5. E. It is an antigenic protein
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. It is an antigenic protein

The baby has neonatal tetanus from Clostridium tetani spores entering the cut umbilical cord, still seen in Pakistan after unclean home deliveries. C. tetani secretes tetanospasmin, an exotoxin that travels up motor nerves to the spinal cord and cleaves synaptobrevin (a SNARE protein) in inhibitory interneurons, blocking release of glycine and GABA, so muscles go into spasm (trismus, opisthotonus). Like most exotoxins, tetanospasmin is a protein: it is heat labile, highly potent and strongly antigenic. Treating it with formaldehyde makes a toxoid that has lost its toxicity but still raises antitoxin IgG. Toxoid given to the mother in pregnancy (at least two doses) produces IgG that crosses the placenta and protects the newborn. Endotoxin (lipopolysaccharide of gram-negative bacteria) is heat stable and poorly antigenic, and no toxoid can be made from it. Clean delivery and cord care also prevent neonatal tetanus.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. It is heat-stable lipid: A heat-stable lipid describes endotoxin, which is poorly antigenic and cannot be made into the vaccine his mother needed.
  • B. It is released at death: Release only at bacterial death applies to endotoxin; this toxin is secreted by living organisms in his cord stump.
  • C. It is part of the cell wall: The toxin is secreted rather than part of the wall, which is why antibody in his mother's blood could have neutralised it.
  • D. It acts in the gut: The toxin does not act in the gut: it travels up his motor nerves to block inhibitory transmitters in the cord.

Key point: Exotoxins are antigenic proteins that formaldehyde turns into toxoids; maternal tetanus toxoid IgG crosses the placenta and prevents neonatal tetanus.

Question 2 · Parasitology · medium

A 32-year-old farmer from a rural district of Sindh has had fever for 10 days, coming with shaking chills every second day and settling with drenching sweats. He is mildly jaundiced with a palpable spleen. Haemoglobin is 9.4 g/dL and platelets 92,000/µL. A Giemsa-stained thick film shows parasites, and the thin film shows enlarged red cells containing amoeboid trophozoites with brownish dots.

Which species is most likely?

  1. A. Plasmodium falciparum
  2. B. Plasmodium malariae
  3. C. Plasmodium vivax
  4. D. Plasmodium ovale
  5. E. Plasmodium knowlesi
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Plasmodium vivax

Malaria is diagnosed on Giemsa-stained blood films: the thick film is more sensitive for detecting parasites, and the thin film allows species identification and parasite counting. Plasmodium vivax invades only young red cells (reticulocytes), so infected cells are enlarged and pale, often distorted, and contain large amoeboid trophozoites with Schuffner dots (brownish stippling); parasitaemia stays low because only young cells are infected. Its cycle of 48 hours gives fever on alternate days (benign tertian). Plasmodium falciparum invades red cells of all ages, so parasitaemia can be very high; films show small delicate rings, often several per cell, with double chromatin dots, banana-shaped gametocytes, and few mature forms because they sequester in capillaries. Plasmodium malariae has a 72-hour cycle (quartan) with band-form trophozoites. Rapid antigen tests detect HRP2 (falciparum) or LDH; PCR is used for low-level infection. In Pakistan both vivax and falciparum are common, with vivax predominating.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Plasmodium falciparum: Falciparum invades cells of every age without enlarging them, and his film shows swollen cells with brownish stippling.
  • B. Plasmodium malariae: P. malariae has a 72-hour cycle, so his fever would come every third day rather than on alternate days.
  • D. Plasmodium ovale: P. ovale is rare in Pakistan and gives oval infected cells; his are round and enlarged with amoeboid trophozoites.
  • E. Plasmodium knowlesi: P. knowlesi occurs in South-East Asian forests and gives daily fever, not alternate-day fever in rural Sindh.

Key point: P. vivax infects young red cells, enlarging them with Schuffner dots and a 48-hour tertian fever; P. falciparum infects cells of all ages with high parasitaemia.

Question 3 · Virology · easy

A 10-month-old boy in Multan has had a high fever of 39.5°C for 3 days with irritability but few other signs; he had one brief convulsion during the fever. Today the fever has settled, and as it did so a fine pink rash appeared on his trunk and neck, with a few spots on the face. He is feeding well and his examination is otherwise normal.

Which virus is the most likely cause?

  1. A. Measles virus
  2. B. Rubella virus
  3. C. Parvovirus B19
  4. D. Varicella-zoster virus
  5. E. Human herpesvirus 6
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Human herpesvirus 6

Roseola infantum (exanthem subitum, or sixth disease) is caused by human herpesvirus 6 (and sometimes HHV-7). It affects babies between about 6 months and 2 years, who have 3-5 days of high fever with surprisingly few localising signs, a common cause of febrile convulsions, after which the fever falls abruptly and a fine macular rash appears on the trunk and spreads outwards, fading within 2 days. Management is supportive, and almost all children are infected by the age of 3; the virus then persists lifelong in lymphocytes and can reactivate in the immunosuppressed, causing encephalitis or marrow suppression. The timing distinguishes it from measles and rubella, in which the rash appears while the child is still febrile and unwell. Human herpesvirus 8 is a very different agent: it causes Kaposi sarcoma, which in HIV presents as purple skin and mucosal plaques, and also primary effusion lymphoma and Castleman disease.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Measles virus: In measles the rash appears while the fever is still rising, with cough, coryza and red eyes, none of which he has.
  • B. Rubella virus: Rubella gives a rash with nodes behind the ears and little fever, not 3 days of 39.5°C before the rash.
  • C. Parvovirus B19: Parvovirus gives bright red cheeks in school-age children, usually without high fever beforehand.
  • D. Varicella-zoster virus: Chickenpox gives itchy vesicles in successive crops, not a fine macular rash as the fever settles.

Key point: HHV-6 causes roseola infantum: 3-5 days of high fever, then a rash as the fever settles; HHV-8 causes Kaposi sarcoma.

Question 4 · Immunology · easy

A researcher in Karachi studies host defence mechanisms. She observes that certain immune cells rapidly respond to pathogens without prior exposure using germline-encoded receptors. These receptors recognise conserved microbial structures like lipopolysaccharide and flagellin. This response lacks memory and specificity for individual strains.

Which receptors mediate this rapid, non-specific innate recognition?

  1. A. T-cell receptors
  2. B. B-cell receptors
  3. C. Killer-cell immunoglobulin-like receptors
  4. D. Fc receptors
  5. E. Pattern recognition receptors
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Pattern recognition receptors

Pattern recognition receptors (PRRs), such as Toll-like receptors (TLRs), NOD-like receptors, and RIG-I-like receptors, are expressed by innate immune cells. They recognise pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) like LPS (TLR4) or dsRNA (TLR3). This triggers immediate inflammation but does not confer long-term memory.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. T-cell receptors: T-cell receptors are made by somatic gene rearrangement and need antigen presented on MHC, so they cannot give this fast, germline-encoded response.
  • B. B-cell receptors: Adaptive receptors with somatic diversity; require activation and clonal expansion for effect.
  • C. Killer-cell immunoglobulin-like receptors: Killer-cell immunoglobulin-like receptors on NK cells read self-MHC class I to control killing; they do not bind microbial molecules such as LPS or flagellin.
  • D. Fc receptors: Bind antibody-coated targets for phagocytosis/degranulation; part of effector phase, not initial sensing.

Key point: Germline-encoded receptors recognising PAMPs = Pattern Recognition Receptors (innate).

Question 5 · Bacteriology · easy

A 6-year-old girl in Karachi has had fever and sore throat for 2 days. Today a fine, red rash that feels like sandpaper has appeared on her neck and chest, spreading to her trunk and limbs, and it is more marked in the skin folds of her elbows. Her face is flushed with paleness around the mouth, and her tongue is red with prominent papillae. Her tonsils are swollen with exudate. A throat swab is cultured on blood agar.

Which laboratory result would identify the organism?

  1. A. Optochin sensitive, bile soluble
  2. B. Bacitracin resistant, CAMP positive
  3. C. Catalase and coagulase positive
  4. D. Bacitracin sensitive, PYR positive
  5. E. Grows in 6.5% salt and bile
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. Bacitracin sensitive, PYR positive

This is scarlet fever: streptococcal pharyngitis with a diffuse sandpaper rash (accentuated in skin folds as Pastia lines), circumoral pallor and a strawberry tongue, followed by peeling of the skin. It is caused by strains of Streptococcus pyogenes that make streptococcal pyrogenic exotoxins (erythrogenic toxin), whose genes are carried by a bacteriophage (lysogenic conversion). S. pyogenes forms small colonies with a wide zone of beta (complete) haemolysis on blood agar and is catalase negative. Among beta-haemolytic streptococci, group A is bacitracin sensitive and PYR (pyrrolidonyl arylamidase) positive, and carries the Lancefield group A carbohydrate; rapid antigen tests detect it directly from throat swabs. Treatment is penicillin V or amoxicillin for 10 days (a macrolide if allergic), which also prevents rheumatic fever.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Optochin sensitive, bile soluble: Optochin sensitivity and bile solubility identify pneumococcus, which gives green partial haemolysis rather than her tonsillar exudate with a sandpaper rash.
  • B. Bacitracin resistant, CAMP positive: Bacitracin resistance with a positive CAMP test identifies group B streptococcus, a cause of neonatal rather than childhood scarlet fever.
  • C. Catalase and coagulase positive: Catalase and coagulase positivity identify S. aureus, which can cause a toxin rash but not with this exudative pharyngitis and strawberry tongue.
  • E. Grows in 6.5% salt and bile: Salt and bile tolerance identifies enterococci, which are non-haemolytic gut organisms and do not cause a sandpaper rash with pharyngitis.

Key point: Scarlet fever is caused by toxin-producing group A streptococci, which are beta-haemolytic, bacitracin sensitive and PYR positive.

Question 6 · Mycology · medium

A 45-year-old barefoot farmer in Tharparkar has had painless swelling of the right foot for 3 years, slowly enlarging, with several openings discharging thick fluid containing small black grains. The foot is deformed but he can still walk, and he has no fever. X-ray shows bone destruction. Microscopy of a crushed grain shows thin branching filaments, and culture grows a mould.

How did this infection most likely begin?

  1. A. Inhalation of spores
  2. B. Spread through the blood
  3. C. From the patient's own gut
  4. D. Implantation in the skin
  5. E. Person-to-person contact
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. Implantation in the skin

Mycetoma is a chronic, slowly progressive infection of skin, subcutaneous tissue and bone of the foot or hand, following implantation of organisms from soil or thorns, so it affects barefoot farmers and labourers; it is endemic in a belt including Sudan, India and parts of Pakistan. There are two kinds. Eumycetoma is caused by fungi such as Madurella mycetomatis, giving black grains, and needs prolonged itraconazole with surgery. Actinomycetoma is caused by aerobic actinomycete bacteria such as Nocardia and Actinomadura, giving white, yellow or red grains, and responds to long courses of co-trimoxazole with amikacin or dapsone. The lesion is painless, with firm swelling, multiple sinuses discharging grains (compacted colonies), and spread into bone; identifying the grain colour and the organism on microscopy and culture is therefore essential before treatment. Other subcutaneous mycoses are sporotrichosis, from rose thorns and plant matter, which spreads as nodules along lymphatics and responds to itraconazole, and chromoblastomycosis.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Inhalation of spores: Inhaled spores cause lung disease; his illness is confined to one foot that walks barefoot on soil.
  • B. Spread through the blood: Blood spread would have seeded other organs long before now, yet after 3 years he still has only a swollen foot.
  • C. From the patient's own gut: His own gut flora do not cause mycetoma; these organisms live in soil and enter through the skin of a bare foot.
  • E. Person-to-person contact: Mycetoma does not pass between people, so his family are not a source; the grains come from organisms in the soil.

Key point: Mycetoma follows implantation of fungi or actinomycetes from soil, causing painless swelling with sinuses discharging grains; grain colour guides treatment.

Question 7 · General microbiology · easy

After two surgical site infections in a month, the infection control team of a Rawalpindi hospital audits its central sterile services department. The steam autoclave runs at 121°C for 15 minutes. Chemical indicator tape on each pack changes colour, and the machine prints a record of temperature and pressure. The team wants a weekly test that shows the cycle actually kills the most resistant living organisms.

Which test should be added?

  1. A. Bacillus atrophaeus spore strips
  2. B. Clostridium tetani spores
  3. C. G. stearothermophilus spores
  4. D. Bowie-Dick test sheet
  5. E. Staphylococcus aureus broth
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. G. stearothermophilus spores

Sterilisation means killing all living organisms, including bacterial spores. Three kinds of check are used: physical (temperature, pressure and time records), chemical (indicator tape or strips that change colour, and the Bowie-Dick test for air removal in pre-vacuum autoclaves) and biological. Only biological indicators prove that the conditions actually killed resistant spores. For moist heat (autoclave) the indicator is spores of Geobacillus stearothermophilus (formerly Bacillus stearothermophilus), which are very heat resistant; after the cycle the strip is incubated at 55-60°C and must show no growth. Bacillus atrophaeus (formerly B. subtilis var. niger) spores check dry heat (hot air oven) and ethylene oxide. Usual autoclave cycles are 121°C for 15 minutes at 15 psi, or 134°C for 3 minutes.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Bacillus atrophaeus spore strips: Bacillus atrophaeus spores check dry heat and ethylene oxide cycles, not the steam autoclave the department uses.
  • B. Clostridium tetani spores: Cultures of this organism are hazardous to handle and are not used as indicators in a sterile services department.
  • D. Bowie-Dick test sheet: The Bowie-Dick sheet shows whether air was removed from the chamber but proves nothing about killing spores.
  • E. Staphylococcus aureus broth: Vegetative staphylococci die in any adequate cycle, so their death would not prove that resistant spores were killed.

Key point: Geobacillus stearothermophilus spores are the biological indicator for steam sterilisation; Bacillus atrophaeus spores for dry heat and ethylene oxide.

Question 8 · Parasitology · medium

A 9-year-old boy whose family moved to Karachi from West Africa has had several attacks of falciparum malaria but never vivax malaria, although vivax is common where he now lives and his Pakistani classmates get it. Blood grouping shows he lacks a particular red cell surface protein. His haemoglobin electrophoresis is normal and he has no enzyme deficiency.

Which receptor does he lack?

  1. A. Glycophorin A
  2. B. Band 3 protein
  3. C. Spectrin
  4. D. Rhesus D antigen
  5. E. Duffy antigen
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Duffy antigen

Plasmodium vivax merozoites enter red cells by binding the Duffy blood group antigen (Fy). Most people of West and Central African descent are Duffy negative, and their red cells cannot be invaded, which is why vivax malaria is almost absent from much of sub-Saharan Africa; this is a classic example of a host receptor determining the geography of a disease. P. falciparum uses other receptors, chiefly glycophorins, so Duffy-negative people are fully susceptible to it. Other inherited red cell traits that give partial protection against falciparum malaria, and have therefore become common in malarious regions, include sickle cell trait, thalassaemia trait, glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase deficiency and hereditary ovalocytosis. Such protection is against severe disease rather than infection. Knowing the species matters practically too: vivax needs primaquine for hypnozoites, and G6PD status should be checked before giving it because of the risk of haemolysis.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Glycophorin A: Glycophorins are the falciparum receptors, and he has had several attacks of falciparum malaria, so these are present.
  • B. Band 3 protein: Band 3 is a membrane transporter, not the receptor vivax needs, and its loss would not spare him vivax alone.
  • C. Spectrin: Spectrin lies inside the cell and gives no surface attachment site; its loss causes spherocytosis, not selective vivax resistance.
  • D. Rhesus D antigen: Rhesus D is unrelated to parasite entry, so his blood group would not protect him from vivax while allowing falciparum.

Key point: P. vivax needs the Duffy antigen to enter red cells, so Duffy-negative people are resistant; falciparum uses glycophorins instead.

Question 9 · Virology · easy

A 2-year-old girl in Multan has had a runny nose for 2 days and wakes at night with a harsh barking cough, a hoarse cry and noisy breathing on inspiration that is worse when she is upset. Her temperature is 37.9°C and she is drinking normally, with no drooling and no difficulty swallowing. The noise settles when she is calm and comfortable.

Which virus most commonly causes this illness?

  1. A. Respiratory syncytial virus
  2. B. Influenza A virus
  3. C. Adenovirus
  4. D. Parainfluenza virus
  5. E. Rhinovirus
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. Parainfluenza virus

Croup (acute laryngotracheobronchitis) is caused most often by parainfluenza viruses, particularly type 1, with influenza, RSV and others less commonly. Swelling of the subglottic larynx and trachea produces the characteristic barking cough, hoarse voice and inspiratory stridor, usually worse at night and when the child is distressed, after a day or two of coryza. Mild croup needs only calm handling and a single dose of oral dexamethasone; moderate or severe cases add nebulised adrenaline and oxygen, and a very small number need intubation. The important differential is epiglottitis (rapid onset, high fever, drooling, muffled voice, no barking cough, child sitting forward), and also inhaled foreign body and bacterial tracheitis. A chest or neck X-ray is not needed, and examining the throat is avoided if epiglottitis is possible.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Respiratory syncytial virus: RSV mainly causes bronchiolitis with crackles and recession in babies, not a barking cough with stridor in a 2-year-old.
  • B. Influenza A virus: Influenza can cause croup but far less often, and would usually bring higher fever and systemic upset.
  • C. Adenovirus: Adenovirus gives sore throat with red eyes, not the hoarse voice and inspiratory stridor she has at night.
  • E. Rhinovirus: Rhinovirus gives coryza alone and would not produce her barking cough and stridor.

Key point: Parainfluenza virus is the commonest cause of croup, with barking cough and stridor, treated with dexamethasone and adrenaline if severe.

Question 10 · Immunology · easy

During a lecture in Quetta, a professor contrasts antigen presentation pathways. One pathway presents endogenous antigens (e.g., viral proteins synthesized within the cell) to CD8+ cytotoxic T-cells. The other presents exogenous antigens processed in endosomes to CD4+ helper T-cells. The first pathway uses molecules found on nearly all nucleated cells.

Which molecule is primarily involved in presenting endogenous antigens to CD8+ T-cells?

  1. A. MHC class II
  2. B. MHC class I
  3. C. CD1
  4. D. Beta-2 microglobulin
  5. E. Invariant chain
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. MHC class I

MHC Class I molecules (HLA-A, B, C) consist of an alpha chain and beta-2 microglobulin. They load peptides derived from cytosolic proteins (proteasome degradation) and present them to CD8+ T-cells. This allows cytotoxic T-cells to kill virus-infected or tumour cells. MHC Class II presents exogenous antigens to CD4+ cells.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. MHC class II: MHC class II sits mainly on antigen-presenting cells and shows exogenous, endosome-processed peptides to CD4 helper cells, the second pathway described here.
  • C. CD1: CD1 presents lipid and glycolipid antigens to NKT and other unusual T cells, not cytosolic viral peptides to ordinary CD8 cytotoxic cells.
  • D. Beta-2 microglobulin: Beta-2 microglobulin is the small chain that stabilises MHC class I on the cell surface, but it does not bind or present peptide by itself.
  • E. Invariant chain: Blocks MHC II peptide groove in ER; not the presenting molecule itself.

Key point: Endogenous antigen → MHC I → CD8+ T-cell.
