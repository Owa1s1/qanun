Question 1 · Implants, maxillofacial prosthodontics and gerodontology · easy

A 46-year-old schoolteacher attends a dental teaching hospital in Peshawar three months after a titanium endosseous fixture was placed in the lower right first molar (46) site. Radiographs show intimate bone apposition around the threads without a radiolucent line, and percussion produces a high-pitched metallic sound with zero mobility under functional loading.

What connects living bone to the implant surface?

  1. A. Direct contact between living bone and the implant surface, with no fibrous tissue
  2. B. A dense collagenous periodontal ligament capsule around the threads
  3. C. A fibrocartilaginous pseudoarthrosis layer caused by micromotion
  4. D. An epithelial hemidesmosomal attachment along the endosseous thread
  5. E. A continuous cementum-like calcified matrix deposited by cementoblasts
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Direct contact between living bone and the implant surface, with no fibrous tissue

Osseointegration, originally described by Branemark and defined in the Glossary of Prosthodontic Terms, is the direct structural and functional connection between ordered, living bone and the surface of a load-bearing implant without intervening fibrous connective tissue at the light microscopic level. Clinically, a successfully osseointegrated fixture demonstrates complete immobility and yields a clear, high-pitched resonant sound on percussion because no periodontal ligament exists to dampen impact forces. Primary stability at placement is purely mechanical and depends on bone density and thread engagement, whereas secondary stability develops over weeks to months through bone remodelling and woven-to-lamellar bone maturation on the microroughened titanium oxide surface.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. A dense collagenous periodontal ligament capsule around the threads: A periodontal ligament connects natural cementum to alveolar bone, whereas this patient has an ankylosed titanium fixture producing a metallic percussion sound.
  • C. A fibrocartilaginous pseudoarthrosis layer caused by micromotion: Fibrous encapsulation occurs when excessive early micromotion prevents bone healing, which would cause clinical mobility and a dull percussion note here.
  • D. An epithelial hemidesmosomal attachment along the endosseous thread: Hemidesmosomes attach junctional epithelium to the transmucosal abutment collar rather than anchoring the intraosseous load-bearing threads in mineralized bone.
  • E. A continuous cementum-like calcified matrix deposited by cementoblasts: Cementoblasts are absent around titanium fixtures placed in edentulous extraction sockets, so cementum formation does not occur on the implant surface.

Key point: Define osseointegration as direct structural and functional contact between living bone and a load-bearing implant surface without fibrous tissue.

Question 2 · Removable partial dentures · easy

A 44-year-old schoolteacher presents to a dental hospital in Peshawar for a maxillary removable partial denture. Intraoral examination reveals that all third molars (18, 28, 38, 48) and all second molars (17, 27, 37, 47) are missing, and because they are unopposed in both arches, the treatment plan specifies that neither the second nor third molars will be replaced. In addition, the upper right first premolar (14) and upper right second premolar (15) are missing, while the upper right first molar (16) and all remaining teeth from 13 to 26 are healthy and intact.

What is the Kennedy classification of this arch?

  1. A. Kennedy Class I Modification 1
  2. B. Kennedy Class II Modification 1
  3. C. Kennedy Class III
  4. D. Kennedy Class III Modification 2
  5. E. Kennedy Class IV
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Kennedy Class III

Edward Kennedy introduced his morphological classification of partially edentulous arches in 1925, and Oliver Applegate later added eight governing rules to ensure the classification reflects clinical biomechanics. Applegate Rule 2 states that if a third molar is missing and is not to be replaced, it is not considered in the classification. Similarly, Applegate Rule 4 states that if a second molar is missing and is not to be replaced (for instance, when the opposing second molar is also absent), it is likewise excluded. In this patient, excluding the unreplaced 18, 17, 27, and 28 leaves a complete arch from 16 to 26 except for a single unilateral edentulous space bounded anteriorly by 13 and posteriorly by 16 (missing 14 and 15). A single unilateral bounded space with no additional spaces is classified as Kennedy Class III.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Kennedy Class I Modification 1: Class I requires bilateral posterior edentulous spaces that are to be restored, whereas here the missing second and third molars will not be replaced.
  • B. Kennedy Class II Modification 1: Class II denotes a unilateral distal extension space to be restored, but 16 and 26 are present and the more posterior spaces are excluded.
  • D. Kennedy Class III Modification 2: Modification spaces count only additional edentulous areas that will be restored; unreplaced second and third molar spaces do not count as modifications.
  • E. Kennedy Class IV: Class IV requires a single anterior edentulous area crossing the midline anterior to the remaining natural teeth, whereas 13 to 23 are intact.

Key point: Apply Applegate Rules 2 and 4 to classify an arch where missing second and third molars will not be replaced.

Question 3 · Complete dentures · easy

A 70-year-old retired railway worker attends a dental teaching hospital in Karachi for replacement complete dentures after 18 years of edentulism. Intraoral examination shows that the mandibular residual alveolar ridge has resorbed to a thin, knife-edged crest covered by movable, atrophic mucosa. However, the broad cortical bone shelf lateral to the posterior ridge between the crest and the external oblique line remains dense and well preserved.

Why is the buccal shelf the main stress-bearing area for a lower denture?

  1. A. Dense cortical bone lies at right angles to vertical occlusal forces
  2. B. Thick keratinized mucosa adheres tightly to the underlying periosteum
  3. C. Absence of muscle attachments prevents any functional movement here
  4. D. Rich vascular supply accelerates osteogenesis under heavy loading
  5. E. Deep undercut configuration provides mechanical retention against lifting
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Dense cortical bone lies at right angles to vertical occlusal forces

The buccal shelf (bounded medially by the crest of the residual ridge, laterally by the external oblique ridge, anteriorly by the buccal frenum, and posteriorly by the retromolar pad) is the primary stress-bearing area of the edentulous mandible. As the residual alveolar ridge resorbs downward and outward, the crest often becomes narrow, cancellous, or knife-edged, making it unsuitable for bearing heavy occlusal loads. In contrast, the buccal shelf is composed of dense, smooth cortical bone that resists resorption because it is stimulated by the attachments of the buccinator muscle. Furthermore, its horizontal contour lies perpendicular to the direction of vertical occlusal forces, allowing full extension of the mandibular denture base to distribute masticatory loads effectively.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Thick keratinized mucosa adheres tightly to the underlying periosteum: The mucosa covering the shelf is non-keratinized and loosely attached with submucosal tissue, unlike the firm masticatory gingiva of an unresorbed ridge crest.
  • C. Absence of muscle attachments prevents any functional movement here: Inferior fibres of the buccinator muscle attach directly along the shelf horizontally, though their parallel orientation allows the denture base to rest over them.
  • D. Rich vascular supply accelerates osteogenesis under heavy loading: Any mucoperiosteal zone subjected to excessive compressive stress undergoes osteoclastic resorption rather than pressure-induced osteogenesis.
  • E. Deep undercut configuration provides mechanical retention against lifting: The shelf is broad and relatively flat rather than undercut, providing vertical support against seating forces rather than mechanical lock.

Key point: Understand why the mandibular buccal shelf serves as the primary stress-bearing area in complete denture prosthodontics.

Question 4 · Bridges and occlusion · easy

A 51-year-old man from Kohat attends a dental clinic for a three-unit metal-ceramic bridge to replace his missing mandibular left first molar (36). Intraoral inspection reveals marked vertical resorption of the edentulous alveolar ridge in the 36 region, leaving the ridge crest well below the lower lip line during speech and wide smiling. The patient has manual dexterity limitations after a wrist fracture and needs a prosthesis that permits effortless plaque removal beneath the pontic.

Which pontic design is most appropriate for this posterior mandibular edentulous site?

  1. A. Conical bullet-shaped pontic touching the ridge crest
  2. B. Full ridge-lap saddle pontic straddling the buccal and lingual slopes
  3. C. Modified ridge-lap pontic overlapping the facial mucosa
  4. D. Ovate pontic recessed into a surgical depression in the ridge
  5. E. Hygienic (sanitary) pontic held clear of the ridge
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Hygienic (sanitary) pontic held clear of the ridge

Pontic designs are broadly divided into mucosal-contact and non-mucosal-contact groups. In non-aesthetic zones—most notably the mandibular molar region when the edentulous ridge has undergone vertical resorption—restoring the illusion of gingival emergence is unnecessary. The hygienic (or sanitary) pontic is designed entirely out of contact with the residual ridge, maintaining a minimum clearance of 3.0 mm between its smooth, all-convex tissue surface and the underlying mucosa, alongside an occlusogingival pontic thickness of at least 3.0 mm for structural rigidity. Its archway or fish-belly rounded undersurface eliminates acute angles at the connectors and allows gauze strips, interdental brushes, or superfloss to pass freely underneath without tissue impingement, preventing mucosal inflammation in patients with limited dexterity.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Conical bullet-shaped pontic touching the ridge crest: A conical bullet pontic makes point contact with a thin ridge and is used primarily for narrow mandibular ridges rather than leaving wide clearance over a resorbed molar site.
  • B. Full ridge-lap saddle pontic straddling the buccal and lingual slopes: A saddle or full ridge-lap pontic has a concave undersurface that traps plaque against the mucosa and cannot be cleaned with dental floss, causing chronic tissue ulceration.
  • C. Modified ridge-lap pontic overlapping the facial mucosa: A modified ridge-lap contacts the buccal mucosa to satisfy aesthetic demands in visible areas, which unnecessarily complicates hygiene in this non-visible lower molar zone.
  • D. Ovate pontic recessed into a surgical depression in the ridge: An ovate pontic requires thick soft-tissue volume and surgical sculpting to mimic an emerging anterior tooth, offering no benefit in a resorbed non-aesthetic molar site.

Key point: Select a hygienic (sanitary) pontic for non-aesthetic posterior mandibular bridges with resorbed ridges to maximise oral hygiene access.

Question 5 · Implants, maxillofacial prosthodontics and gerodontology · easy

A 63-year-old edentulous farmer from Mardan attends for pre-implant cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) of his mandible after wearing an unstable lower denture for twenty years. Cross-sectional images between the mental foramina reveal a severely resorbed basal ridge composed almost entirely of homogenous compact cortical bone with minimal trabecular marrow.

Which Lekholm and Zarb bone quality class describes this interforaminal mandibular architecture?

  1. A. Type IV bone (thin cortex around low-density marrow)
  2. B. Type III bone (thin cortex around dense trabeculae)
  3. C. Type II bone (thick cortex around dense marrow)
  4. D. Type I bone (almost entirely homogeneous compact bone)
  5. E. Type V bone (non-mineralized fibrous ridge)
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. Type I bone (almost entirely homogeneous compact bone)

The Lekholm and Zarb (1985) classification grades residual jawbone by both volume/shape (A to E, from virtually intact alveolar ridge to extreme basal bone resorption) and bone quality/density (Types I to IV). Type I bone is composed almost entirely of homogenous compact cortical bone; it is most often encountered in the chronically resorbed anterior mandible, offers high mechanical primary stability, and has a poor blood supply that makes it susceptible to frictional overheating during drilling. Type II has a thick layer of compact bone surrounding a core of dense trabecular bone, Type III has a thin cortical shell around dense trabecular bone, and Type IV has a thin cortex around low-density porous trabecular bone (typical of the posterior maxilla).

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Type IV bone (thin cortex around low-density marrow): Type IV bone consists of a thin cortical shell surrounding low-density trabecular bone, typically found in the posterior maxilla.
  • B. Type III bone (thin cortex around dense trabeculae): Type III bone has a thin layer of cortical bone surrounding dense trabecular bone of favourable strength, unlike this homogenous cortical ridge.
  • C. Type II bone (thick cortex around dense marrow): Type II bone features a thick cortical layer surrounding a well-defined core of dense trabecular marrow rather than almost solid compact bone.
  • E. Type V bone (non-mineralized fibrous ridge): The Lekholm and Zarb bone quality classification contains only four density grades (Types I to IV), whereas shape grades run from A to E.

Key point: Classify homogenous compact bone in the resorbed anterior mandible as Lekholm and Zarb Type I bone quality.

Question 6 · Removable partial dentures · easy

A prosthodontist at a dental teaching hospital in Karachi surveys a diagnostic cast for a mandibular cobalt-chromium removable partial denture. After locking the cast at the selected tilt and marking the survey lines with a carbon marker, she attaches an undercut gauge to the vertical spindle of the Ney surveyor to locate the exact position for the retentive tip of a cast circumferential clasp on the lower right first premolar (44).

How is the undercut gauge used, and which size is standard for a cobalt-chromium clasp?

  1. A. Contact only the survey line with the gauge head while using the deep 0.75 mm undercut gauge on the buccal surface
  2. B. Place the vertical gauge shank flat against the free gingival crevice while using the 0.50 mm undercut gauge
  3. C. Touch the prepared occlusal rest seat with the gauge tip while reading the vertical millimetre scale at 1.0 mm
  4. D. Scrape the suprabulge enamel surface with the bevelled knife edge of the 0.25 mm surveyor wax trimmer blade
  5. E. The shank touches the survey line while the 0.25 mm lip of the gauge touches the infrabulge tooth surface
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. The shank touches the survey line while the 0.25 mm lip of the gauge touches the infrabulge tooth surface

Undercut gauges provided with a dental surveyor come in three standard horizontal lip dimensions: 0.25 mm (0.01 inch), 0.50 mm (0.02 inch), and 0.75 mm (0.03 inch). The depth of a retentive undercut is measured horizontally as the distance between the height of contour (survey line) and the infrabulge tooth surface where the retentive clasp terminal will rest. To locate this spot, the vertical spindle carrying the undercut gauge is brought against the tooth so that the vertical shank contacts the survey line at the exact moment that the projecting lip of the gauge contacts the infrabulge surface below the survey line. Because cast cobalt-chromium alloy has a high modulus of elasticity (twice as stiff as gold alloy), a small 0.25 mm horizontal undercut is standard to prevent clasp deformation or excessive lateral force on the abutment.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Contact only the survey line with the gauge head while using the deep 0.75 mm undercut gauge on the buccal surface: A 0.75 mm undercut is far too deep for rigid cobalt-chromium and would deform the clasp or overload the abutment unless a long wrought-wire clasp is used.
  • B. Place the vertical gauge shank flat against the free gingival crevice while using the 0.50 mm undercut gauge: Resting the shank on gingival tissue does not measure the horizontal tooth undercut, and 0.50 mm is typically reserved for wrought wire or gold alloys.
  • C. Touch the prepared occlusal rest seat with the gauge tip while reading the vertical millimetre scale at 1.0 mm: Undercut gauges measure horizontal infrabulge convergence below the survey line rather than vertical depth of an occlusal rest seat.
  • D. Scrape the suprabulge enamel surface with the bevelled knife edge of the 0.25 mm surveyor wax trimmer blade: The wax trimmer is a knife-edged blade used to block out unwanted undercuts in wax on the master cast, never to scrape stone or enamel.

Key point: Describe how a surveyor undercut gauge contacts the tooth and select the 0.25 mm gauge for a cast cobalt-chromium retentive clasp.

Question 7 · Complete dentures · easy

A 59-year-old woman attends a dental hospital in Hyderabad requesting new complete dentures because her 14-year-old prostheses have become loose and stained. She wears her dentures day and night, removing them only to rinse after meals. Examination shows that the palatal and alveolar mucosa beneath the upper denture is erythematous, oedematous, and distorted by a poorly adapted acrylic relief chamber.

What should be done first, before definitive impressions?

  1. A. Proceed directly to zinc oxide eugenol wash impressions in a close-fitting custom tray
  2. B. Institute nocturnal denture removal and line the old denture with tissue conditioner
  3. C. Prescribe oral fluconazole 150 mg daily for four weeks while she wears the dentures continuously
  4. D. Perform radical surgical excision of the entire erythematous palatal mucoperiosteum
  5. E. Construct the new dentures immediately and plan a laboratory reline after six months
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Institute nocturnal denture removal and line the old denture with tissue conditioner

When an edentulous patient wears an ill-fitting complete denture continuously day and night, the underlying mucoperiosteum becomes compressed, hyperaemic, and distorted. Recording primary or definitive impressions while the tissues are in this deformed state captures an inaccurate mucosal contour; once the new denture is inserted and pressure distribution changes, the tissues rebound, leaving the new prosthesis loose and unstable. Ideal diagnosis and treatment planning require mucosal recovery before impression making. Ideally, the patient should leave the old dentures out for 48 to 72 hours prior to impressions. When social or occupational needs prevent going without teeth, instructing the patient to leave the dentures out every night, massage the tissues, and line the existing prosthesis with a viscoelastic tissue conditioner (changed every few days) restores mucosal health.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Proceed directly to zinc oxide eugenol wash impressions in a close-fitting custom tray: Taking a definitive impression over oedematous, distorted mucosa records the displaced shape and guarantees that the new denture will become loose once inflammation subsides.
  • C. Prescribe oral fluconazole 150 mg daily for four weeks while she wears the dentures continuously: Systemic antifungals cannot resolve mechanical mucosal distortion while the patient continues wearing an ill-fitting denture 24 hours a day.
  • D. Perform radical surgical excision of the entire erythematous palatal mucoperiosteum: Erythematous and oedematous denture-bearing mucosa responds well to conservative tissue rest and conditioning without destructive surgery.
  • E. Construct the new dentures immediately and plan a laboratory reline after six months: Fabricating new dentures on distorted casts introduces avoidable occlusal and fit errors that necessitate remaking rather than simple relining.

Key point: Manage inflamed, distorted denture-bearing mucosa conservatively with nocturnal tissue rest and tissue conditioners before making impressions.

Question 8 · Bridges and occlusion · easy

A 20-year-old woman in Swabi has a congenitally missing maxillary left lateral incisor (22) following orthodontic space opening. The adjacent maxillary left canine (23) is vital, caries-free, and has normal clinical crown height. The clinician decides to provide a single-retainer cantilevered resin-bonded (Maryland) bridge using a sandblasted nickel-chromium wing bonded to the palatal surface of the canine with an adhesive phosphate-monomer resin cement.

Which palatal preparation feature on the canine is critical?

  1. A. A finish line 1 mm below the gingiva
  2. B. Palatal reduction of 1.5 mm into dentine
  3. C. No etching, with a eugenol desensitiser
  4. D. Proximal walls tapered at 30 degrees
  5. E. Enamel-only, with a cingulum rest
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Enamel-only, with a cingulum rest

The longevity of a resin-bonded (Maryland) fixed dental prosthesis depends on achieving a high-strength micromechanical and chemical bond between the sandblasted base-metal retainer wing, the adhesive resin cement (such as 10-MDP-containing luting resin), and phosphoric-acid-etched dental enamel. Because bond strength to etched enamel is vastly superior and more durable than bond strength to dentine, any tooth preparation must remain strictly confined within the enamel layer (typically 0.3 to 0.5 mm reduction on the lingual surface). Essential design features include a distinct, light chamfer finish line placed approximately 1.0 mm supragingivally (to stay on thick enamel and permit absolute moisture control under a rubber dam), a positive cingulum or occlusal rest seat to resist apical displacement, and proximal wrap-around of at least 180 degrees where aesthetics permit.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. A finish line 1 mm below the gingiva: Subgingival margins compromise rubber dam isolation, contaminate the resin bond with crevicular fluid, and strip thin cervical enamel to expose poorly bonding cementum or dentine.
  • B. Palatal reduction of 1.5 mm into dentine: Cutting through the palatal enamel into dentine drastically reduces adhesive bond strength and risks pulpal sensitivity; retainer rigidity should come from metal thickness or slight occlusal clearance.
  • C. No etching, with a eugenol desensitiser: Unprepared prismless surface enamel has lower bond strength than phosphoric-acid-etched enamel, and eugenol inhibits the free-radical polymerisation of adhesive resin cements.
  • D. Proximal walls tapered at 30 degrees: When axial guide planes are prepared for a two-wing or wrap-around design, they must be kept nearly parallel (around 6 degrees total convergence) to provide resistance against unseating.

Key point: Outline the enamel-confined preparation principles, cingulum rest, and supragingival margin location required for a resin-bonded bridge.

Question 9 · Implants, maxillofacial prosthodontics and gerodontology · easy

A 58-year-old man from Charsadda with a 25-year history of oral snuff (naswar) use undergoes a classic unilateral hemimaxillectomy for T3 squamous cell carcinoma of the right maxillary antrum. The resection stops precisely at the palatal midline, leaving all left maxillary teeth from the central incisor (21) to the second molar (27) intact.

Into which Aramany class does this patient's partially edentulous maxillectomy defect fall?

  1. A. Aramany Class I (unilateral resection along the midline)
  2. B. Aramany Class II (unilateral posterior defect)
  3. C. Aramany Class III (central hard palate defect)
  4. D. Aramany Class IV (defect crossing the midline)
  5. E. Aramany Class VI (anterior premaxillary defect)
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Aramany Class I (unilateral resection along the midline)

Mohamed Aramany (1978) classified partially edentulous maxillectomy arches into six classes to standardize cobalt-chromium definitive obturator framework design. Class I is the classic unilateral resection along the midline of the hard palate, preserving the contralateral teeth from central incisor to posterior molar; because the remaining teeth lie in a relatively straight line, design uses either a linear or tripodal configuration with direct retention and lingual/buccal guiding planes. Class II is a unilateral posterior defect preserving the anterior teeth on the defect side (similar to a Kennedy Class II arch, giving a favourable tripodal design). Class III is a central palatal defect with all teeth intact, Class IV crosses the midline anteriorly and unilaterally, Class V is a bilateral posterior resection, and Class VI is an anterior resection.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Aramany Class II (unilateral posterior defect): Aramany Class II applies to a unilateral posterior maxillectomy defect that preserves the premaxilla and anterior teeth on the ipsilateral defect side.
  • C. Aramany Class III (central hard palate defect): Aramany Class III describes a central defect of the hard palate where the dentition is preserved on both right and left sides of the arch.
  • D. Aramany Class IV (defect crossing the midline): Aramany Class IV involves a defect that crosses the midline to include both the premaxilla and one entire posterolateral maxilla, leaving a single posterior row of teeth.
  • E. Aramany Class VI (anterior premaxillary defect): Aramany Class VI represents an anterior premaxillary resection anterior to the bilateral remaining posterior teeth, which is the opposite of a posterior defect.

Key point: Classify a midline unilateral hemimaxillectomy with intact contralateral teeth from central incisor to second molar as an Aramany Class I defect.

Question 10 · Removable partial dentures · easy

A 60-year-old woman attends a dental hospital in Peshawar wearing a mandibular Kennedy Class I cobalt-chromium partial denture with free-end saddles that replace the molars and second premolars on both sides. She reports that when she chews firm food on one side, the saddle on that side rocks from side to side across the crest of the narrow residual ridge, although it does not lift away from the ridge or sink at its distal end.

Around which axis does this saddle rotate, and what resists it?

  1. A. Around the fulcrum line through the terminal rests; resisted by indirect retainers anterior to that line
  2. B. Along the ridge crest; resisted by a rigid major connector, stabilising arms and broad flanges
  3. C. Around a vertical axis near the centre of the arch; resisted only by the retentive tips of the buccal clasps
  4. D. Around a horizontal axis through both condyles; resisted by raising the vertical dimension of the saddles
  5. E. Around the long axis of the terminal abutment root; resisted by removing the rests so the saddle settles
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Along the ridge crest; resisted by a rigid major connector, stabilising arms and broad flanges

A distal extension base can move in three ways. It can rotate about the fulcrum line through the terminal rests, toward or away from the ridge; good base support and indirect retainers control this. It can rotate about a longitudinal axis running along the crest of the residual ridge, rocking buccolingually; this is resisted by the rigidity of the major and minor connectors, by stabilising and reciprocal components on the abutments, and by well-extended flanges on a broad base. It can also rotate about a vertical axis near the centre of the arch under horizontal forces, which is resisted by the stabilising components and the cross-arch rigidity of the major connector. A narrow ridge increases buccolingual rocking, so a rigid connector and flanges that use the buccal shelf matter most here.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Around the fulcrum line through the terminal rests; resisted by indirect retainers anterior to that line: Rotation about the fulcrum line lifts or sinks the saddle at its distal end, which she does not describe.
  • C. Around a vertical axis near the centre of the arch; resisted only by the retentive tips of the buccal clasps: Rotation about a vertical axis is a horizontal swing of the whole denture, and clasp tips alone cannot control it.
  • D. Around a horizontal axis through both condyles; resisted by raising the vertical dimension of the saddles: The condylar axis describes jaw opening, not movement of a denture base on its ridge.
  • E. Around the long axis of the terminal abutment root; resisted by removing the rests so the saddle settles: Removing rests would let the saddle sink and would not stop it rocking across the ridge.

Key point: Identify rotation of a distal extension base about the ridge crest and the components that resist it.
