Question 1 · Growth, development and malocclusion · easy

A 14-year-old boy presents to a dental teaching hospital in Mardan with facial asymmetry four years after a road-traffic temporal bone injury that severed the motor nerve to his right temporalis muscle. Intraorally, the lower right permanent first molar (46) and lower left permanent first molar (36) are in Class I occlusion. A panoramic radiograph reveals a markedly underdeveloped right mandibular coronoid process, whereas the mandibular body and condyle have normal dimensions.

Under the functional matrix concept, what loss explains this local bone deficiency?

  1. A. Periosteal functional matrix
  2. B. Capsular functional matrix
  3. C. Primary cartilaginous growth centre
  4. D. Autonomous sutural tissue proliferation
  5. E. Intrinsic osteogenic genetic blueprint
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Periosteal functional matrix

In the functional matrix hypothesis of Melvin Moss, the mandible is not treated as a single uniform bone but as a macroskeletal unit composed of several microskeletal units: the coronoid process, angular process, alveolar process, basal body, and condylar process. Each microskeletal unit responds directly to its own periosteal functional matrix. Specifically, the size and remodelling of the coronoid process depend on the contractile tension of the attached temporalis muscle, just as the gonial angle responds to the masseter and medial pterygoid muscles, and the alveolar bone depends on the presence of teeth. Classical denervation and muscle-excision studies confirm that losing temporalis pull causes selective regression of the coronoid process without arresting overall mandibular translation.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Capsular functional matrix: Capsular matrices such as the expanding brain or oropharyngeal space translate whole macro-skeletal bones passively rather than shaping a single muscular process.
  • C. Primary cartilaginous growth centre: The coronoid process ossifies intramembranously within the temporalis tendon and lacks a primary epiphyseal or synchondroseal cartilage growth centre.
  • D. Autonomous sutural tissue proliferation: The mandible has no posterior suture at the coronoid process, and sutures do not generate primary tissue-separating forces.
  • E. Intrinsic osteogenic genetic blueprint: If bone form were strictly predetermined inside osteocytes, denervating the attached muscle would not cause selective atrophy of the coronoid process.

Key point: Distinguish between periosteal and capsular functional matrices in regulating microskeletal units of the mandible.

Question 2 · Orthodontic appliances and treatment · easy

A 17-year-old girl presents to a dental teaching hospital in Mardan with a Class II division 2 malocclusion, an overbite of 7 mm covering 85% of the mandibular incisors, and retroclined maxillary central incisors (11 and 21). The treatment plan includes true vertical intrusion of the maxillary central incisors (11 and 21) using a utility archwire before torque correction. The postgraduate resident adjusts the archwire to deliver a very light continuous vertical force per tooth.

What force per incisor is best for intrusion?

  1. A. 5 to 8 g
  2. B. 10 to 20 g
  3. C. 50 to 70 g
  4. D. 100 to 120 g
  5. E. 200 to 250 g of continuous force
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. 10 to 20 g

Optimal orthodontic force depends on the surface area of the periodontal ligament over which the applied force is distributed, aiming to produce an ideal capillary-level pressure of approximately 20 to 25 g/cm² of root surface. During intrusion, the entire vertical load is concentrated over a very small cross-sectional area at the root apex. Consequently, a very light continuous force of only 10 to 20 g per tooth is required to achieve the same local pressure at the apex that 70 to 120 g produces across the entire lateral root wall during bodily translation. Applying tipping or bodily force levels during intrusion compresses the apical neurovascular bundle, causing immediate apical hyalinisation, undermining resorption, and permanent shortening of the root apex.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. 5 to 8 g: A force below the biological threshold of capillary blood pressure fails to deform the apical periodontal ligament enough to initiate osteoclastic bone remodelling.
  • C. 50 to 70 g: This intermediate force range is appropriate for tipping or rotation where stress is spread over a larger root surface, but crushes the apex if applied vertically.
  • D. 100 to 120 g: Forces of 100 to 120 g are required for bodily translation across the entire root length and cause severe apical root resorption if used for intrusion.
  • E. 200 to 250 g of continuous force: Heavy forces of this magnitude exceed physiological limits for any single-rooted tooth movement and produce immediate apical necrosis and undermining resorption.

Key point: State the optimal orthodontic force for intrusion (10–20 g) and explain why force must be kept low due to stress concentration at the root apex.

Question 3 · Orthodontic assessment and treatment planning · easy

A 13-year-old girl presents to a dental clinic in Dir for an orthodontic assessment. She has a Class I incisor relationship with an overjet of 3.5 mm, an overbite of 3 mm, and a 3.5 mm contact point displacement of her maxillary right permanent canine (13), giving a borderline Dental Health Component score of Grade 3d. To determine whether she qualifies for publicly funded treatment, the orthodontist assesses her anterior teeth using the 10-point monochrome photographic scale of the IOTN Aesthetic Component.

How is the IOTN Aesthetic Component scored?

  1. A. Find the photograph depicting the exact morphological trait of a buccally crowded canine
  2. B. Add the patient self-score and the clinician score together to obtain a total out of 20
  3. C. Match the overall attractiveness of her anterior teeth to the nearest photograph on the 1 to 10 scale
  4. D. Evaluate the patient lateral facial convexity against ten profile silhouettes
  5. E. Assign Grade 10 automatically whenever a patient expresses psychological distress
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Match the overall attractiveness of her anterior teeth to the nearest photograph on the 1 to 10 scale

The Aesthetic Component (AC) of the Index of Orthodontic Treatment Need (IOTN), developed by Evans and Shaw (SCAN index), consists of 10 standard monochrome intraoral frontal photographs arranged along a continuum of dental attractiveness from Grade 1 (most attractive) to Grade 10 (least attractive). Grades 1 to 4 represent no or slight aesthetic need, Grades 5 to 7 represent borderline or moderate need, and Grades 8 to 10 represent definite aesthetic need for orthodontic treatment (upgrading a borderline DHC Grade 3 case to qualify for funded care when AC is ≥ 6). When scoring the AC, the clinician examines the anterior teeth in frontal view with the lips retracted and matches the overall level of aesthetic dental attractiveness to the equivalent point on the 1 to 10 scale, rather than searching for an exact morphological match.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Find the photograph depicting the exact morphological trait of a buccally crowded canine: Clinicians must not look for a specific morphological matching anomaly (such as an open bite, reverse overjet, or ectopic canine), but rather rate overall dental attractiveness.
  • B. Add the patient self-score and the clinician score together to obtain a total out of 20: The Aesthetic Component is recorded as a single rating from 1 to 10 (with Grades 8 to 10 indicating definite need), never summed out of 20.
  • D. Evaluate the patient lateral facial convexity against ten profile silhouettes: The IOTN Aesthetic Component consists solely of intraoral frontal anterior dental photographs and does not include extraoral profile silhouettes.
  • E. Assign Grade 10 automatically whenever a patient expresses psychological distress: The score must reflect objective visual comparison with the 1 to 10 dental attractiveness scale rather than automatic assignment of Grade 10.

Key point: Describe the correct clinical method and grading thresholds for scoring the Aesthetic Component of the IOTN.

Question 4 · Growth, development and malocclusion · easy

An 11-year-old boy is reviewed at a dental hospital in D.I. Khan. Two years ago, his panoramic radiograph showed the crown of the unerupted lower left permanent second molar (37) superimposed over the root of the lower left permanent first molar (36) and covered by the ascending ramus. Today, a repeat radiograph shows ample bony space behind 36, allowing 37 to erupt vertically into the dental arch.

Which periosteal remodelling mechanism created this posterior alveolar space in the mandible?

  1. A. Anterior ramal resorption and posterior apposition
  2. B. Interstitial expansion of the mandibular corpus bone
  3. C. Heavy bone deposition on the labial chin symphysis
  4. D. Active proliferation of the mental symphyseal suture
  5. E. Anterior bodily translation of the entire lower dentition
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Anterior ramal resorption and posterior apposition

Unlike long bones that lengthen at epiphyseal plates, the body (corpus) of the mandible lengthens posteriorly to accommodate the erupting permanent first, second, and third molars through coordinated periosteal remodelling of the mandibular ramus. As the mandible translates downward and forward, osteoclasts resorb bone continuously along the anterior border of the ascending ramus while osteoblasts deposit new bone along the posterior border of the ramus, the condylar neck, and the coronoid process. Consequently, bone that previously formed the anterior base of the ramus is converted into the posterior extension of the mandibular corpus and alveolar ridge, uncovering the crypts of the permanent second molars (37, 47) and later the third molars (38, 48).

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Interstitial expansion of the mandibular corpus bone: Mineralized intramembranous bone of the mandibular body cannot expand interstitially from within and grows only by surface remodelling.
  • C. Heavy bone deposition on the labial chin symphysis: The anterior labial surface of the alveolar region above the chin is mildly resorptive and cannot create space at the distal end of the molar arch.
  • D. Active proliferation of the mental symphyseal suture: The mandibular midline symphysis fuses before twelve months of age and plays no role in sagittal arch lengthening at age eleven.
  • E. Anterior bodily translation of the entire lower dentition: Mesial migration of molars alone cannot account for uncovering a second molar crypt buried beneath the cortical bone of the ascending ramus.

Key point: Describe how anterior border resorption and posterior border apposition of the mandibular ramus lengthen the lower dental arch.

Question 5 · Orthodontic appliances and treatment · easy

A 14-year-old girl attends a dental teaching hospital in Peshawar complaining of a 3 mm maxillary midline diastema between her maxillary right central incisor (11) and maxillary left central incisor (21) after full eruption of the maxillary permanent canines (13 and 23). An upper periapical radiograph shows normal interdental bone height and identical conical root morphology of 11 and 21. The orthodontist places brackets on 11 and 21 with a sectional wire and an elastomeric chain linking the two teeth.

Which type of anchorage is this?

  1. A. Simple intra-arch dental anchorage
  2. B. Stationary intra-arch dental anchorage
  3. C. Reciprocal anchorage within the same arch
  4. D. Intermaxillary Baker elastic anchorage
  5. E. Absolute cortical skeletal anchorage
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Reciprocal anchorage within the same arch

Orthodontic anchorage is defined as the resistance to unwanted tooth movement, and the anchorage value of any tooth is approximately proportional to its root surface area embedded in alveolar bone (the threshold pressure for movement being tied to periodontal ligament area). In reciprocal anchorage, two teeth or tooth groups having equal root surface areas are connected by an active force element so that the action and reaction forces produce equal and opposite desirable tooth movements. Classic clinical examples of reciprocal anchorage include closing a symmetrical maxillary midline diastema between the right and left central incisors (11 and 21), where both have identical ~200 mm² root surface areas and move mesially toward the midline equally, and symmetrical rapid or slow transverse arch expansion across the midpalatal suture.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Simple intra-arch dental anchorage: Simple anchorage pits a tooth or group of teeth of much larger root surface area against a smaller tooth that is allowed to tip freely.
  • B. Stationary intra-arch dental anchorage: Stationary anchorage pits bodily movement of an anchor unit against simple tipping of the active tooth to resist anchor movement.
  • D. Intermaxillary Baker elastic anchorage: Intermaxillary anchorage uses elastic traction between the maxillary and mandibular arches rather than between two teeth within the same arch.
  • E. Absolute cortical skeletal anchorage: Absolute anchorage relies on bone-borne temporary anchorage devices or ankylosed implants so that zero movement occurs in the anchor unit.

Key point: Define reciprocal orthodontic anchorage and identify clinical situations where equal root surface areas produce symmetrical tooth movement.

Question 6 · Orthodontic assessment and treatment planning · easy

An 11-year-old girl attends a dental teaching hospital in Peshawar with a retained upper right primary canine (53) and no palpable labial canine bulge in the upper right quadrant, while the contralateral permanent canine (23) is palpable high in the buccal sulcus. The orthodontist takes a panoramic radiograph, which shows the unerupted upper right permanent canine (13) overlapping the root of the upper right lateral incisor (12), and then orders an upper standard occlusal view with the tube head positioned higher in the vertical plane.

Which radiographic principle locates 13 from these two views?

  1. A. Horizontal parallax
  2. B. Vertical parallax
  3. C. Rule of isometry
  4. D. Orthogonal projection
  5. E. Tomographic focal trough
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Vertical parallax

Localising an unerupted tooth in three dimensions from two-dimensional radiographs relies on the principle of parallax, first described by Clark. When two radiographs are taken with the X-ray tube shifted between exposures, an object closer to the tube appears to move in the opposite direction to the tube shift, whereas a deeper (palatal) object appears to move in the same direction (SLOB rule: Same Lingual, Opposite Buccal). When a dental panoramic tomogram (where the beam is angled slightly upwards at about -7 degrees to the occlusal plane) is paired with an upper standard occlusal radiograph (where the tube is positioned high over the bridge of the nose at 60 to 65 degrees to the occlusal cassette), the tube shift occurs in the vertical plane. If the canine crown moves apically with the upward tube shift, it lies palatally.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Horizontal parallax: Horizontal tube shift uses two periapical radiographs taken at different mesiodistal angles in the horizontal plane rather than a panoramic and occlusal view.
  • C. Rule of isometry: Cieszynski rule of isometry governs bisecting-angle periapical geometry to prevent tooth elongation or foreshortening rather than localising an unerupted tooth in three dimensions.
  • D. Orthogonal projection: True right-angle imaging requires two views taken at ninety degrees to each other, whereas an upper standard occlusal is angled obliquely at 65 degrees.
  • E. Tomographic focal trough: The focal trough describes the curved zone of sharp focus on a panoramic machine alone, which cannot distinguish buccal from palatal depth without a second view.

Key point: Recognize that combining a panoramic radiograph with an upper standard occlusal radiograph applies vertical parallax to localise an unerupted maxillary canine.

Question 7 · Growth, development and malocclusion · easy

A 6-week-old male infant is brought to a dental teaching hospital in Charsadda by his mother, who is worried that the front part of his upper and lower gums do not touch when his mouth is gently closed, leaving a space through which his tongue rests. Examination reveals healthy pink horse-shoe maxillary and U-shaped mandibular gum pads with distinct transverse grooves, contacting posteriorly in the future primary molar region with no natal teeth near the lower right primary central incisor (81) site.

What is the most appropriate interpretation of this anterior gum pad relationship?

  1. A. Normal physiological anterior gap of infancy
  2. B. Congenital skeletal anterior open bite malocclusion
  3. C. Pathological macroglossia requiring tongue reduction
  4. D. Pierre Robin sequence with severe micrognathia
  5. E. Rickets-induced deformity of the premaxillary segment
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Normal physiological anterior gap of infancy

At birth, the edentulous alveolar processes are covered by firm, pink fibrous mucoperiosteal pads known as gum pads, which are divided by transverse grooves into ten segments corresponding to the developing primary tooth sacs. The groove distal to the crypt of the primary canine extends onto the buccal side as the lateral sulcus. When the upper and lower gum pads are brought together in a normal neonate, the mandibular lateral sulcus lies posterior to the maxillary lateral sulcus, and the pads contact only in the posterior first primary molar region. Anteriorly, an elliptical vertical space (infantile open bite) is always present, accommodating the tongue during suckling. During the first year, rapid forward mandibular growth and eruption of the primary incisors (71, 81, 51, 61) establish normal anterior overlap.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Congenital skeletal anterior open bite malocclusion: An anterior space between neonatal gum pads is a universal physiological feature at six weeks and does not predict a permanent skeletal open bite.
  • C. Pathological macroglossia requiring tongue reduction: In healthy neonates the tongue normally fills the oral cavity and rests between the anterior gum pads to create an anterior seal during breastfeeding.
  • D. Pierre Robin sequence with severe micrognathia: Although the neonatal mandible sits slightly distal to the maxilla, absence of glossoptosis, cleft palate, or airway distress rules out Pierre Robin sequence.
  • E. Rickets-induced deformity of the premaxillary segment: Nutritional rickets does not manifest at six weeks as an isolated anterior gum pad space in an otherwise healthy infant with normal posterior contact.

Key point: Recognize the normal anatomical features and physiological anterior space of neonatal gum pads.

Question 8 · Orthodontic appliances and treatment · easy

A 10-year-old boy attends a dental teaching hospital in Abbottabad for construction of an upper removable appliance to correct a single instanding maxillary right lateral incisor (12). For posterior retention, the laboratory prescription specifies modified arrowhead clasps on the fully erupted maxillary right first permanent molar (16) and maxillary left first permanent molar (26). The technician selects hard-drawn stainless steel wire to fabricate these clasps.

Which wire and undercuts are used for an Adams clasp on first molars?

  1. A. 0.5 mm stainless steel wire engaging the mid-buccal cervical convexity
  2. B. 0.6 mm beta-titanium wire engaging the mesiopalatal and distopalatal undercuts
  3. C. 0.9 mm stainless steel wire engaging the interproximal contact points occlusally
  4. D. 0.7 mm stainless steel wire engaging mesiobuccal and distobuccal undercuts
  5. E. 1.25 mm nickel-titanium wire engaging the buccal furcation below the gingival margin
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. 0.7 mm stainless steel wire engaging mesiobuccal and distobuccal undercuts

The Adams clasp (universal modified arrowhead clasp) is the most widely used retentive component on orthodontic removable appliances. For first permanent molars (16 and 26), it is fabricated from 0.7 mm diameter hard-drawn stainless steel wire (0.6 mm is used for premolars, canines, or primary molars). Rather than engaging the mid-buccal surface—which is often flat in children whose clinical crowns are only partially erupted—its two arrowheads engage approximately 0.25 mm into the mesiobuccal and distobuccal undercuts located at the mesial and distal anatomical corners of the buccal surface just beneath the maximum bulbosity. The bridge connects the two arrowheads at 45 degrees, standing 1 to 2 mm clear of the buccal tooth surface half-way up the clinical crown so the patient can remove the appliance with a fingernail without ulcerating the cheek.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. 0.5 mm stainless steel wire engaging the mid-buccal cervical convexity: A 0.5 mm wire is too flexible for molar retention and is used for active palatal finger springs, while the mid-buccal surface offers less undercut than the corners.
  • B. 0.6 mm beta-titanium wire engaging the mesiopalatal and distopalatal undercuts: A 0.6 mm wire is used for deciduous molars or premolars in stainless steel rather than beta-titanium, and Adams arrowheads engage buccal rather than palatal undercuts.
  • C. 0.9 mm stainless steel wire engaging the interproximal contact points occlusally: A 0.9 mm wire is too rigid to flex into tooth undercuts and is reserved for extraoral bows or palatal arches rather than retentive molar clasps.
  • E. 1.25 mm nickel-titanium wire engaging the buccal furcation below the gingival margin: Superelastic nickel-titanium cannot be bent into permanent clasp arrowheads, and pushing wire into the subgingival furcation causes severe periodontal trauma.

Key point: State the wire diameter (0.7 mm stainless steel) and mesiobuccal/distobuccal undercut sites engaged by an Adams clasp on a permanent first molar.

Question 9 · Orthodontic assessment and treatment planning · easy

A final-year dental student at a teaching hospital in Mardan is tracing a lateral cephalometric radiograph of a 14-year-old girl presenting with a Class II division 1 malocclusion and an overjet of 8 mm. After identifying sella (S) in the pituitary fossa and nasion (N) at the frontonasal suture, she looks at the curved anterior contour of the maxillary alveolar process between the anterior nasal spine superiorly and the crest of the maxillary alveolar bone between the central incisors (11 and 21) inferiorly.

Which cephalometric landmark represents the deepest point on this bony concavity?

  1. A. Subspinale (Point A) on the anterior maxilla
  2. B. Supramentale (Point B)
  3. C. Prosthion (anterior alveolar crest)
  4. D. Pogonion (anterior bony chin)
  5. E. Orbitale (inferior orbital rim)
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Subspinale (Point A) on the anterior maxilla

Subspinale, universally known in Downs and Steiner cephalometric analyses as Point A, is defined as the deepest (most posterior) midline point on the curved bony concavity of the anterior maxilla between the anterior nasal spine (ANS) superiorly and prosthion (the crest of the maxillary alveolus between the central incisors) inferiorly. Because it sits at the junction of the basal maxillary bone and the alveolar bone supporting the upper central incisor roots, Point A is used to represent the anteroposterior position of the anterior maxilla in the SNA and ANB angles. Notice that its position can be subtly remodelled by palatally or labially torquing the roots of the upper central incisors during fixed appliance mechanics.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Supramentale (Point B): Point B lies on the anterior contour of the mandibular symphysis between infradentale and pogonion rather than on the maxillary alveolar process.
  • C. Prosthion (anterior alveolar crest): Prosthion is the lowest, most anterior point on the maxillary alveolar bone crest between the upper central incisors rather than the deepest concavity above it.
  • D. Pogonion (anterior bony chin): Pogonion is the most anterior point on the bony chin contour of the mandible in the median plane, not a maxillary landmark.
  • E. Orbitale (inferior orbital rim): Orbitale is the lowest point on the inferior bony margin of the orbit used to construct the Frankfort plane rather than the anterior maxillary contour.

Key point: Define the anatomical location of Point A (subspinale) on a lateral cephalometric tracing.

Question 10 · Growth, development and malocclusion · easy

A 4-year-old girl is brought to a paediatric dental clinic in Swabi by her mother, who is worried about noticeable gaps between her baby teeth. Intraoral examination shows a caries-free primary dentition with a 2 mm overjet and 2 mm overbite at the upper right primary central incisor (51) and lower right primary central incisor (81). Distinct 2 mm developmental spaces are present bilaterally in a characteristic asymmetrical pattern between the upper and lower primary arches.

Where are the physiological primate (anthropoid) spaces located in the human primary dentition?

  1. A. Distal to upper and mesial to lower primary canines
  2. B. Mesial to both upper and lower primary canines
  3. C. Mesial to upper and distal to lower primary canines
  4. D. Between the primary first and second molars in both jaws
  5. E. Solely in the midline between the primary central incisors
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Mesial to upper and distal to lower primary canines

In a normal primary dentition, two types of physiological spacing are seen: generalized interdental developmental spaces between the primary incisors, and localized primate (anthropoid or simian) spaces. Named by Baume because they are prominent in non-human primates, human primate spaces are consistently located mesial to the maxillary primary canines—between the primary lateral incisor and primary canine (52-53 and 62-63)—and distal to the mandibular primary canines—between the primary canine and primary first molar (73-74 and 83-84). Presence of generous spacing in the primary dentition at age 4 years is a favourable clinical sign that helps accommodate the much wider permanent incisors without crowding and facilitates early mesial shift of the mandibular first permanent molars.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Distal to upper and mesial to lower primary canines: This reverses the true anatomical position in both jaws; upper primate spaces lie anterior to the canine while lower primate spaces lie posterior to it.
  • B. Mesial to both upper and lower primary canines: While the maxillary primate space lies between the lateral incisor and canine, the mandibular primate space lies distal to the lower canine.
  • D. Between the primary first and second molars in both jaws: Primary first and second molars normally have tight broad interproximal contacts; spaces between them suggest interproximal caries rather than primate spaces.
  • E. Solely in the midline between the primary central incisors: Generalized interdental spacing may include the midline, but named anthropoid primate spaces specifically flank the primary canines.

Key point: Identify the exact anatomical locations of maxillary and mandibular primate spaces in the primary dentition.
