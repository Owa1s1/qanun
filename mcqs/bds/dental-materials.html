Question 1 · General properties of dental materials · easy

A 67-year-old retired farmer in Bannu accidentally leaves his maxillary heat-cured acrylic complete denture out on a sunny courtyard shelf in dry summer weather for four days. When he puts the dried denture back in his mouth, it feels slightly tight across the palate, but after he soaks it in a bowl of tap water for 24 hours, it fits comfortably again.

Which mechanism accounts for the expansion of the polymethyl methacrylate denture base when soaked in water?

  1. A. Hydrolysis of the carbon-carbon backbone into methyl methacrylate monomer
  2. B. Diffusion of polar water molecules between polymer chains by water sorption
  3. C. Hygroscopic crystallization of residual benzoyl peroxide initiator
  4. D. Osmotic swelling driven by sodium chloride trapped inside the porcelain teeth
  5. E. Transformation toughening of the amorphous polymer matrix
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Diffusion of polar water molecules between polymer chains by water sorption

Polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) contains polar ester groups (-COOCH3) that have a slight affinity for polar water molecules. When immersed in water or saliva, water molecules diffuse into the intermolecular spaces according to Fick laws of diffusion (water sorption, limited by ISO 20795-1 to ≤32 µg/mm³). The absorbed water acts as a mild plasticizer and pushes the polymer macromolecules slightly apart, causing a linear expansion of approximately 0.2% to 0.4% (roughly compensating for the 0.4% to 0.5% thermal polymerization shrinkage that occurred during processing). Conversely, allowing an acrylic denture to dry out in air causes reversible shrinkage and warpage; therefore, patients must store dentures in water when not worn.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Hydrolysis of the carbon-carbon backbone into methyl methacrylate monomer: Water sorption does not cleave the covalent C-C main chain of polymethyl methacrylate at room temperature.
  • C. Hygroscopic crystallization of residual benzoyl peroxide initiator: Benzoyl peroxide decomposes during heat curing and does not form expanding crystals when exposed to water.
  • D. Osmotic swelling driven by sodium chloride trapped inside the porcelain teeth: Porcelain teeth are impermeable to water, and osmotic pressure from salt contamination is a cause of soft liner blistering, not bulk PMMA expansion.
  • E. Transformation toughening of the amorphous polymer matrix: Transformation toughening is a martensitic crystal phase change in yttria-stabilized zirconia, not an amorphous polymer process.

Key point: Explain the mechanism and clinical significance of water sorption and desorption in acrylic denture bases.

Question 2 · Impression, model, wax and casting materials · easy

In a prosthodontics clinic in Larkana, a house officer prepares a rigid custom acrylic tray to take a polyvinyl siloxane impression. Finding the polyvinyl siloxane tray adhesive bottle empty, he paints the inside of the tray with the brown rubbery adhesive liquid from a polysulphide impression kit, waits 10 minutes for the solvent to evaporate, and takes the impression. When he removes the tray from the mouth, the set silicone peels completely away from the inner tray wall.

Why did the polysulphide tray adhesive fail to retain the polyvinyl siloxane impression?

  1. A. The brown polysulphide adhesive poisoned the platinum catalyst at the tooth-gingiva margin
  2. B. Waiting 10 minutes for the volatile solvent to evaporate inactivated the adhesive film
  3. C. Acrylic custom trays can only retain impressions via mechanical perforations and never bond to chemical adhesives
  4. D. Elastomeric tray adhesives are chemically specific to each polymer class and are not interchangeable
  5. E. Polyvinyl siloxane shrinks away from the tray wall rather than toward the tray wall
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. Elastomeric tray adhesives are chemically specific to each polymer class and are not interchangeable

To prevent an elastomeric impression from pulling away from the tray during removal from tooth undercuts, the material must be firmly attached to the rigid tray wall using a chemical tray adhesive (often supplemented by small mechanical perforations). Notably, when an impression is bonded to the tray, the small polymerization shrinkage pulls the elastomer outward toward the tray wall, slightly enlarging the lumen (helping compensate for metal casting shrinkage); if it detaches from the tray, it shrinks inward and ruins the cast. Elastomeric tray adhesives are solutions of a matching rubbery polymer plus a crosslinker dissolved in a volatile organic solvent (such as acetone, ethyl acetate or toluene) and are strictly non-interchangeable: polyvinyl siloxane adhesives contain polydimethylsiloxane and ethyl silicate (which forms a physical/chemical network with the tray and silicone), polysulphide adhesives contain butyl rubber or polychloroprene, and polyether adhesives contain a polyaziridine/polyether copolymer.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. The brown polysulphide adhesive poisoned the platinum catalyst at the tooth-gingiva margin: The impression set normally; it failed by adhesive delamination at the tray-elastomer interface because the two polymers are chemically incompatible.
  • B. Waiting 10 minutes for the volatile solvent to evaporate inactivated the adhesive film: Allowing the volatile organic solvent (such as ethyl acetate or toluene) to evaporate completely for 5–15 minutes before loading is actually mandatory for all tray adhesives.
  • C. Acrylic custom trays can only retain impressions via mechanical perforations and never bond to chemical adhesives: Chemical tray adhesives bond strongly to non-perforated custom acrylic trays provided the correct polymer-specific adhesive is used and dried.
  • E. Polyvinyl siloxane shrinks away from the tray wall rather than toward the tray wall: When bonded to a rigid tray with the proper adhesive, polymerization shrinkage is directed toward the tray wall (making the die space slightly wider), not peeling away from it.

Key point: Explain why elastomeric tray adhesives are chemically specific to each elastomer family and describe how tray bonding directs polymerization shrinkage toward the tray wall.

Question 3 · Metals, polymers, ceramics and implant materials · easy

In a removable prosthodontics laboratory in Kohat, a dental technician is packing a complete denture acrylic resin into a dental flask using the conventional compression-moulding technique. He measures out polymethyl methacrylate homopolymer/copolymer spherical beads for the powder and methyl methacrylate monomer liquid.

What is the function of hydroquinone in the liquid of heat-cured denture acrylic?

  1. A. Plasticiser that softens the base
  2. B. Initiator that starts the reaction
  3. C. Crosslinking agent
  4. D. Inhibitor (stops early setting)
  5. E. Opacifier for colour
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. Inhibitor (stops early setting)

Heat-cured polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) resins are the global standard for fabricating removable complete and partial denture bases. 1) **Powder (Polymer)**: Consists primarily of **pre-polymerized PMMA spherical beads or granules** (synthesized via suspension polymerization). Minor additives include: **Benzoyl peroxide (BPO, ~0.5–1.0%)**, which acts as the heat-activated free-radical initiator; **plasticizers** (such as dibutyl phthalate, ~2–5%) to slightly lower the glass transition temperature and softening point; and **pigments/opacifiers** (such as mercuric sulphide, cadmium pigments, or titanium dioxide, <0.05%) to mimic natural gingival tissue aesthetics and vascular stippling (nylon/rayon fibres). 2) **Liquid (Monomer)**: Consists of unreacted **methyl methacrylate (MMA) monomer** liquid. Minor additives include: **Hydroquinone (~0.003–0.1%)**, a free-radical scavenger/inhibitor added to prevent spontaneous polymerization during storage and shelf-aging; **crosslinking agents** (such as ethylene glycol dimethacrylate [EGDMA] or 1.4-butanediol dimethacrylate, ~1–2%), which contain two reactive C=C double bonds to form a 3D interconnected network that dramatically improves solvent/crazing resistance and mechanical toughness; and sometimes an amine accelerator (though rarely in heat-cures).

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Plasticiser that softens the base: Dibutyl phthalate is a plasticiser; hydroquinone is not.
  • B. Initiator that starts the reaction: Benzoyl peroxide in the powder is the initiator.
  • C. Crosslinking agent: Glycol dimethacrylate is the crosslinker.
  • E. Opacifier for colour: Titanium and zinc oxides are opacifiers.

Key point: Identify the chemical functions of benzoyl peroxide initiator (powder), hydroquinone inhibitor (liquid), and dimethacrylate crosslinking agents (liquid) in heat-cured PMMA denture resins.

Question 4 · Direct restorative materials, cements and adhesives · easy

In a biomaterials laboratory in Mardan, a 2nd-year BDS student tests a series of dimethacrylate resin specimens in which the volume fraction of silanated barium-silicate glass filler is progressively increased from 0 vol% (unfilled resin) to 30 vol%, 50 vol% and 65 vol%.

Which property *decreases* as the volume fraction of inorganic glass filler is increased from 0 vol% to 65 vol%?

  1. A. Elastic modulus and strength
  2. B. Surface hardness and wear resistance
  3. C. Expansion, shrinkage and sorption
  4. D. X-ray radiopacity
  5. E. Viscosity of the unset paste
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Expansion, shrinkage and sorption

Unfilled dimethacrylate resins (Bis-GMA/TEGDMA) have poor clinical properties for load-bearing restorations: high volumetric polymerization shrinkage (~6–8%), a high linear coefficient of thermal expansion (~80–100 × 10⁻⁶ /°C, roughly 7–8 times that of tooth crown structure at ~11.4 × 10⁻⁶ /°C), low elastic modulus (~2.5 GPa vs ~18 GPa for dentine), high water sorption, and zero radiopacity. Incorporating silanated inorganic glass/silica fillers (up to 55–70 vol% or 75–85 wt% in hybrid/nanohybrid composites) transforms the material by rule-of-mixtures reinforcement: 1) **Increases**: elastic modulus (to 10–18 GPa, matching dentine), compressive/flexural strength, Knoop hardness, wear resistance, paste viscosity, and radiopacity (via heavy-metal atoms Ba, Sr, Zr, Yb with atomic numbers >20). 2) **Decreases**: volumetric polymerization shrinkage (down to 1.5–3.0% because glass does not shrink), coefficient of thermal expansion (down to 25–40 × 10⁻⁶ /°C), water sorption, and exothermic heat of curing.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Elastic modulus and strength: Adding rigid glass filler particles (E ≈ 70 GPa) to a flexible polymer matrix (E ≈ 2–3 GPa) *increases* the overall elastic modulus and strength.
  • B. Surface hardness and wear resistance: Hard inorganic silica/glass fillers *increase* Knoop hardness and wear resistance compared to unfilled acrylic/dimethacrylate resin.
  • D. X-ray radiopacity: Barium-, strontium-, zirconium- or ytterbium-containing glass fillers *increase* radiopacity so secondary caries can be distinguished from the restoration.
  • E. Viscosity of the unset paste: Increasing the filler loading makes the unset paste stiffer and *increases* its viscosity (from flowable to packable/sculptable).

Key point: Predict how increasing the volume fraction of silanated inorganic filler increases modulus, strength and radiopacity while decreasing polymerization shrinkage, thermal expansion and water sorption.

Question 5 · General properties of dental materials · easy

In a dental biomaterials practical in Peshawar, a 1st-year BDS student places a drop of unfilled bonding resin onto two surfaces of an extracted premolar: one covered with an organic salivary pellicle, and the other freshly etched with 37% phosphoric acid, rinsed and dried. On the pellicle-covered enamel the drop beads up with a contact angle of 75°, whereas on the acid-etched enamel it spreads almost flat with a contact angle of 8°.

What does the decrease in contact angle from 75° to 8° indicate about the acid-etched enamel surface?

  1. A. Complete non-wetting has been achieved at the solid-liquid interface
  2. B. The viscosity of the unfilled bonding resin has increased tenfold
  3. C. Cohesive forces within the resin drop now exceed adhesive forces
  4. D. The enamel surface has become strongly hydrophobic
  5. E. Its critical surface tension and surface free energy have increased
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Its critical surface tension and surface free energy have increased

Wetting is the ability of a liquid adhesive to flow over and make intimate molecular contact with a solid adherent surface. It is quantified by the contact angle (θ) formed between the tangent to the liquid drop surface and the solid surface at the three-phase boundary, governed by Young equation: γSV = γSL + γLV cos θ. For spontaneous spreading and good wetting, the surface free energy (critical surface tension, γc) of the solid substrate must be higher than the surface tension (γLV) of the liquid adhesive, resulting in a small contact angle approaching 0°. Unetched enamel coated by organic biofilm or salivary pellicle has a low surface energy (~28 mN/m); acid etching removes this layer and doubles the surface energy (>70 mN/m), allowing low-viscosity resin monomers to wet the microporosities completely.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Complete non-wetting has been achieved at the solid-liquid interface: Complete wetting occurs as the contact angle approaches 0°, whereas complete non-wetting corresponds to a contact angle approaching 180°.
  • B. The viscosity of the unfilled bonding resin has increased tenfold: Surface preparation of the tooth does not increase the intrinsic bulk viscosity of the liquid resin monomer.
  • C. Cohesive forces within the resin drop now exceed adhesive forces: When cohesive forces exceed adhesive forces, the liquid beads up with a contact angle greater than 90° (non-wetting).
  • D. The enamel surface has become strongly hydrophobic: A hydrophobic surface repels polar liquids and shows a high contact angle, whereas etched enamel is hydrophilic and high-energy.

Key point: Relate acid etching of enamel to increased surface free energy, reduced contact angle and improved adhesive wetting.

Question 6 · Impression, model, wax and casting materials · easy

A prosthodontist in Islamabad prepares an upper right first premolar (14) for a monolithic lithium disilicate crown. Instead of using a conventional tray and elastomer, she captures an optical 3D impression of the prepared tooth, adjacent teeth and opposing arch using an intraoral wand camera based on confocal laser scanning. She exports the open-architecture surface mesh file to the laboratory software, where the crown contours are designed virtually and sent to a 4-axis milling machine.

Which file format does an intraoral scanner usually export for the CAD software?

  1. A. PDF document
  2. B. DICOM image stack
  3. C. JPEG image
  4. D. STL surface mesh
  5. E. Plain text spreadsheet
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. STL surface mesh

Digital impressions and Computer-Aided Design / Computer-Aided Manufacturing (CAD/CAM) eliminate physical impression errors (such as tray distortion, polymerization shrinkage, air voids and gypsum expansion). Every dental CAD/CAM system consists of three functional stages: 1) Data acquisition (digitization), using an intraoral optical scanner or extraoral laboratory cast scanner (utilizing triangulation, parallel confocal imaging, or active wavefront sampling) to record 3D surface coordinates; 2) Computer-Aided Design (CAD), where software defines the finish line, cement space (die spacer), proximal contacts and occlusal anatomy; and 3) Computer-Aided Manufacturing (CAM), which fabricates the restoration via subtractive milling (grinding pre-sintered or fully sintered blocks with diamond burs) or additive manufacturing (3D printing/stereolithography/selective laser melting). The scanner exports the 3D point cloud as a triangulated surface mesh in STL (Standard Tessellation Language / Stereolithography) format—or colour-capable PLY/OBJ formats.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. PDF document: PDF is a document format, not a 3D mesh.
  • B. DICOM image stack: DICOM is the format of CT and CBCT volumes, not surface scans.
  • C. JPEG image: A JPEG is a flat photograph with no 3D geometry.
  • E. Plain text spreadsheet: A spreadsheet carries no 3D surface data.

Key point: Identify the three stages of the dental CAD/CAM workflow and the role of the STL (Standard Tessellation Language) 3D surface file format.

Question 7 · Metals, polymers, ceramics and implant materials · easy

An implant surgeon in Peshawar places a screw-shaped commercially pure titanium implant into the healed mandibular molar site of a 45-year-old man. Six months later, histological sectioning of the retrieved bone-implant interface reveals direct structural and functional connection between ordered, living bone and the surface of the titanium implant without intervening soft tissue.

What term describes this direct, functional bone contact with the implant surface?

  1. A. Fibro-osseous integration
  2. B. Osseointegration
  3. C. Ankylosis of a tooth
  4. D. Biointegration
  5. E. Peri-implant mucositis
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Osseointegration

Osseointegration—pioneered and defined by Swedish orthopaedic surgeon Per-Ingvar Brånemark in the 1960s—is clinically defined as the **asymptomatic rigid fixation of an alloplastic material (such as titanium or zirconia) into living bone under functional loading**. Histologically, it represents direct structural and functional contact between ordered, living lamellar bone and the implant surface (termed direct bone-to-implant contact or BIC) with **zero intervening soft tissue or fibrous capsule layer**. The biological success of endosseous titanium implants depends entirely on the spontaneous formation of an adherent, highly stable, biocompatible **titanium dioxide (TiO2)** passive film when titanium meets oxygen or tissue fluid. This oxide layer is chemically inert, non-toxic, highly corrosion-resistant, and presents a surface free energy and hydrophilic topography that promotes osteoblast adhesion, spreading, and localized bone mineralization right up to the implant interface.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Fibro-osseous integration: This would mean a fibrous layer between bone and implant, which counts as failure.
  • C. Ankylosis of a tooth: Ankylosis describes a tooth fused to bone, not an implant.
  • D. Biointegration: This term is sometimes used for chemical bonding to bioactive coatings, not this definition.
  • E. Peri-implant mucositis: This is inflammation of the soft tissue around an implant.

Key point: Define osseointegration (Brånemark definition) and state the role of the passivating TiO2 layer in achieving direct bone-to-implant contact.

Question 8 · Direct restorative materials, cements and adhesives · easy

During an operative dentistry practical in Bannu, a 3rd-year BDS student prepares two cavities on extracted premolars for direct composite resin restorations: Cavity 1 is a box-shaped occlusal Class I cavity with four vertical walls (mesial, distal, buccal, lingual) and a flat pulpal floor; Cavity 2 is a flat facial Class IV/veneer preparation bonded only to the labial enamel surface with four unbonded free surfaces.

What is the C-factor of Cavity 1 (Class I box: four walls and a floor)?

  1. A. 1.0
  2. B. 0.2
  3. C. 5.0
  4. D. 2.0
  5. E. 4.0
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. 5.0

When a composite resin cures inside a tooth cavity, the magnitude of tensile and shear stress developed at the tooth-adhesive interface depends not only on the volumetric polymerization shrinkage and elastic modulus of the composite, but critically on the **Configuration Factor (C-factor)**, defined by Feilzer, De Gee and Davidson (1987) as the ratio of the **bonded surface area** to the **unbonded (free) surface area**: **C = Bonded Surfaces / Unbonded Surfaces**. Before the polymerizing resin reaches its gel point (pre-gel stage), viscous flow inward from the unbonded free surfaces can relieve internal contraction stresses without pulling on the cavity walls. Once cross-linking passes the gel point (post-gel stage), viscous flow ceases and any further shrinkage is locked in as elastic stress pulling on the bonded walls. In a box-shaped Class I or Class V cavity filled in bulk, 5 walls are bonded (buccal, lingual, mesial, distal, pulpal) and only 1 surface (occlusal/facial) is free to flow, giving **C = 5/1 = 5.0**—the highest stress geometry, prone to debonding at the dentinal floor, postoperative sensitivity and cusp deflection. By contrast, a Class II or III cavity has C ≈ 1.0–2.0 (4 bonded / 2 free), a Class IV restoration has C ≈ 0.5 (2 bonded / 4 free), and a flat labial veneer or pedodontic sealant on a flat surface has **C = 1/5 = 0.2**, allowing free inward flow and minimal interfacial stress.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. 1.0: A C-factor of 1 would mean equal bonded and free surfaces.
  • B. 0.2: This is the value for the flat facial preparation, with one bonded and five free surfaces.
  • D. 2.0: A Class II box has a lower C-factor than a Class I box, but not this one.
  • E. 4.0: There are five bonded surfaces, not four, and one free surface.

Key point: Define the Configuration Factor (C-factor = bonded / unbonded surfaces), calculate C-factors for Class I through V cavities, and relate high C-factors to post-gel interfacial shrinkage stress.

Question 9 · General properties of dental materials · easy

A 44-year-old woman attends a dental clinic in Peshawar complaining of a sudden metallic taste and a sharp, momentary electrical pain in her lower right first molar (46) whenever she bites down fully. The lower right first molar (46) was restored yesterday with a fresh silver amalgam filling, while the opposing upper right first molar (16) has had a cast Type III gold inlay for five years.

What is the electrochemical mechanism responsible for this patient’s sharp pain on occlusion?

  1. A. Crevice corrosion under the gold inlay margin
  2. B. Galvanic current between two metals
  3. C. Stress corrosion cracking of the gamma-1 matrix
  4. D. Surface tarnish by hydrogen sulphide deposition
  5. E. Pitting corrosion of the gold-copper lattice
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Galvanic current between two metals

Galvanism (galvanic shock) occurs when two dissimilar metallic restorations with different electrode potentials come into intermittent contact in the presence of saliva acting as an electrolyte. Dental amalgam is more electronegative (less noble) and acts as the anode, undergoing oxidation, while the gold alloy is more electropositive (more noble) and acts as the cathode. When the opposing teeth touch, the circuit closes and a current of several microamperes flows through the restoration and dentinal fluid to the pulp, stimulating nerve endings. Coating the fresh amalgam with cavity varnish or waiting a few days for a passivating oxide/tarnish film to form relieves the symptoms.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Crevice corrosion under the gold inlay margin: Occurs slowly inside stagnant microgaps depleted of oxygen and does not produce an acute electrical current upon occlusal contact.
  • C. Stress corrosion cracking of the gamma-1 matrix: Describes slow mechanical crack propagation under combined tensile stress and corrosion rather than an immediate electrical jolt.
  • D. Surface tarnish by hydrogen sulphide deposition: Tarnish is a non-destructive surface discolouration (such as silver sulphide film) that actually passivates the surface rather than causing shock.
  • E. Pitting corrosion of the gold-copper lattice: Type III gold alloys are noble and resist pitting, which is a localized chloride attack seen in base metal alloys.

Key point: Explain the mechanism of oral galvanic shock when dissimilar metallic restorations contact in saliva.

Question 10 · Impression, model, wax and casting materials · easy

In a dental materials laboratory in Peshawar, a 2nd-year BDS student examines two calcium sulphate hemihydrate powders under a light microscope. Powder X consists of porous, irregular, spongy particles produced by heating gypsum rock in an open kettle at 110–120 °C, whereas Powder Y consists of dense, regular, prismatic crystals produced by calcining gypsum under steam pressure in an autoclave at 125 °C.

How do Powder X and Powder Y compare?

  1. A. Same compound; X needs more water (about 0.5)
  2. B. X is dihydrate and needs less water
  3. C. Same compound; Y needs more water (about 0.6)
  4. D. X sets by polymerisation, Y by chelation
  5. E. Both need exactly 0.186 water-powder ratio
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Same compound; X needs more water (about 0.5)

All dental gypsum products—Type II model plaster (β-hemihydrate), Type III dental stone (α-hemihydrate) and Type IV/V high-strength die stones (modified α-hemihydrate boiled in 30% calcium chloride or sodium succinate)—have the exact same chemical formula: calcium sulphate hemihydrate, (CaSO4)2·H2O or CaSO4·½H2O. While 100 g of hemihydrate theoretically requires only 18.6 mL of water for chemical reaction into calcium sulphate dihydrate (CaSO4·2H2O), additional "gauging water" is necessary to float the particles into a workable fluid mix. Because β-hemihydrate particles are spongy, irregular and microporous, they require 45–50 mL of water per 100 g (W/P = 0.45–0.50); as this excess unreacted water later evaporates, it leaves a porous, weaker set mass. Dense, smooth α-hemihydrate crystals pack closely and need only 28–30 mL/100 g (Type III) or 20–24 mL/100 g (Type IV), yielding a far denser, stronger cast.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. X is dihydrate and needs less water: Both are hemihydrate; the dihydrate is the set product.
  • C. Same compound; Y needs more water (about 0.6): Dense stone particles need less water than plaster, not more.
  • D. X sets by polymerisation, Y by chelation: Both set by dissolution of hemihydrate and precipitation of dihydrate.
  • E. Both need exactly 0.186 water-powder ratio: 0.186 is the water needed chemically; extra water is needed to make a pourable mix.

Key point: Compare the particle morphology, chemical identity and water-powder ratios of beta-hemihydrate (plaster) and alpha-hemihydrate (dental stone).
