Oral Biology MCQs for BDS

10 free oral biology questions for every year of BDS at KMU and UHS, written in the style of the real exam. Try each one, then open the answer: every wrong option is explained too. Question 1 · Embryology of the face, mouth and teeth · easy

During an oral examination at a dental OPD in Larkana, a 45-year-old man develops a strong bilateral gag reflex when the dental mirror touches the mucosa of his tongue immediately posterior to the V-shaped row of circumvallate papillae and sulcus terminalis.

Which embryonic swelling gives rise to the mucosa stimulated by the mirror in this patient?

  1. A. Hypobranchial eminence
  2. B. Intermaxillary segment
  3. C. Lateral lingual swellings
  4. D. Maxillary prominences
  5. E. Tuberculum impar
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Hypobranchial eminence

Behind the foramen caecum and sulcus terminalis lies the posterior one-third (pharyngeal part) of the tongue. In the fourth week of development, two midline elevations appear behind the tuberculum impar: the copula (derived from the ventromedial ends of the second pharyngeal arch) and a larger caudal elevation called the hypobranchial eminence (derived from the mesoderm of the third and fourth pharyngeal arches). As the tongue grows, the cranial (third arch) portion of the hypobranchial eminence expands forward, completely overgrowing the second-arch copula to fuse with the anterior two-thirds along the sulcus terminalis. Because the mucosa of the posterior one-third of the tongue is formed by the third pharyngeal arch, both its general sensation (the afferent limb of the gag reflex) and taste sensation—including the circumvallate papillae just anterior to the sulcus—are carried by the glossopharyngeal nerve (CN IX).

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Intermaxillary segment: The intermaxillary segment develops from the merged medial nasal prominences and forms the primary palate and upper lip philtrum rather than the tongue.
  • C. Lateral lingual swellings: The paired lateral lingual swellings arise from the first pharyngeal arch and form the mucosa of the anterior two-thirds of the tongue rostral to the sulcus terminalis.
  • D. Maxillary prominences: The maxillary prominences of the first arch form the lateral upper lip, cheeks, and secondary palate rather than the floor of the pharynx or tongue.
  • E. Tuberculum impar: The tuberculum impar is a median first-arch swelling located anterior to the foramen caecum that is largely overgrown by the lateral lingual swellings.

Key point: Identify the third pharyngeal arch part of the hypobranchial eminence as the embryonic source of the posterior one-third of the tongue mucosa and explain its glossopharyngeal innervation.

Question 2 · Oral histology: enamel, dentine, pulp and periodontium · easy

During an oral biology examination in Kohat, a student inspects a transmission electron micrograph of the pulpal-dentinal border. A prominent junctional complex (terminal web of tight and adherens junctions) encircles the neck of each odontoblast just where the narrow cytoplasmic process enters the predentine tubule.

How does the organelle content of the odontoblast process inside the dentinal tubule differ from that of the odontoblast cell body in the pulp?

  1. A. Process has cytoskeleton, body has RER
  2. B. The process holds the nucleus and Golgi
  3. C. The process is packed with myofibrils
  4. D. Both are packed with RER along their length
  5. E. The process is collagen with no membrane
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Process has cytoskeleton, body has RER

A fully differentiated secretory odontoblast is a highly polarised, post-mitotic columnar cell (approximately 40 to 50 µm tall in the crown and cuboidal/flattened in the root apex) divided anatomically at its apical neck by a ring of intercellular junctional complexes (zonula adherens, gap junctions, and focal tight junctions associated with an actin terminal web) into two compartments: the cell body in the peripheral pulp and the odontoblast process (Tomes fibre) entering the predentine and dentinal tubule. The cell body houses all the synthetic machinery: a large oval nucleus at the basal (pulpal) pole, a prominent supranuclear Golgi apparatus, abundant cisternae of rough endoplasmic reticulum (rER), and numerous mitochondria. At the terminal web, these large synthetic organelles stop. Consequently, the odontoblast process extending into the tubule is devoid of a nucleus, Golgi apparatus, ribosomes, and rER (only occasional mitochondria reach the proximal predentine portion). Instead, the process is packed with longitudinally aligned microtubules and intermediate filaments (vimentin), a submembranous lattice of actin microfilaments, and exocytic/endocytic vesicles that transport matrix proteins to the mineralisation front and maintain peritubular dentine.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. The process holds the nucleus and Golgi: The nucleus, rough endoplasmic reticulum, and Golgi apparatus are strictly confined to the odontoblast cell body inside the peripheral pulp below the terminal web.
  • C. The process is packed with myofibrils: Odontoblast processes are non-muscular ectomesenchymal extensions lacking myofibrils; dentinal fluid movement is passive rather than driven by muscular contraction.
  • D. Both are packed with RER along their length: Protein synthesis (rER and ribosomes) occurs only in the cell body; the process inside the mineralised tubule serves for transport, secretion, and sensory transduction.
  • E. The process is collagen with no membrane: The odontoblast process (Tomes fibre) is a true membrane-bound cytoplasmic extension of the odontoblast containing cytoskeletal tubules and filaments.

Key point: Contrast the organelle composition of the odontoblast cell body (nucleus, rER, Golgi, mitochondria) with that of the odontoblast process beyond the terminal web (microtubules, microfilaments, vesicles, lacking synthetic organelles).

Question 3 · Orofacial anatomy for dentistry · easy

A 49-year-old woman presents to an oral medicine clinic in Peshawar with numbness over her right cheek, upper lip, and lower lip along with weakness of her right masseter muscle. Magnetic resonance imaging of the middle cranial fossa reveals a meningioma infiltrating the lateral wall of the sphenoid body and floor of the middle cranial fossa.

Through which two openings in the sphenoid bone do the affected maxillary and mandibular divisions of the trigeminal nerve respectively exit the skull?

  1. A. Foramen ovale and foramen spinosum
  2. B. Foramen rotundum and foramen lacerum
  3. C. Superior orbital fissure and foramen rotundum
  4. D. Foramen rotundum and foramen ovale
  5. E. Inferior orbital fissure and stylomastoid foramen
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. Foramen rotundum and foramen ovale

From the semilunar (trigeminal) ganglion in Meckel cave on the petrous temporal apex, the three divisions of the fifth cranial nerve diverge across the middle cranial fossa floor to exit through distinct openings in the sphenoid bone. The ophthalmic nerve (V1, purely sensory) travels in the lateral wall of the cavernous sinus and enters the orbit through the superior orbital fissure. The maxillary nerve (V2, purely sensory) runs along the lower lateral wall of the cavernous sinus and passes anteriorly through the circular foramen rotundum in the greater wing of the sphenoid to enter the pterygopalatine fossa. The mandibular nerve (V3, carrying both a large sensory root and the entire motor root of the trigeminal nerve) does not traverse the cavernous sinus; instead, both roots drop vertically through the oval-shaped foramen ovale to unite as the main mandibular trunk in the infratemporal fossa.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Foramen ovale and foramen spinosum: While the mandibular nerve exits through the foramen ovale, the foramen spinosum transmits the middle meningeal artery and nervous spinosus rather than the maxillary nerve.
  • B. Foramen rotundum and foramen lacerum: The maxillary nerve traverses the foramen rotundum, but the foramen lacerum is filled with fibrocartilage in life and does not transmit the mandibular nerve.
  • C. Superior orbital fissure and foramen rotundum: The ophthalmic division (V1) passes through the superior orbital fissure and the maxillary division (V2) through the foramen rotundum, leaving out the mandibular division (V3).
  • E. Inferior orbital fissure and stylomastoid foramen: The infraorbital continuation of V2 enters the orbit via the inferior orbital fissure outside the cranial cavity, while the stylomastoid foramen transmits the facial nerve (CN VII).

Key point: Identify the foramen rotundum and foramen ovale of the sphenoid bone as the cranial exit pathways of the maxillary (V2) and mandibular (V3) divisions of the trigeminal nerve.

Question 4 · Tooth morphology and occlusion · easy

A second-year BDS student in Mardan compares an extracted permanent maxillary second premolar (15) with an extracted permanent maxillary first premolar (14). Unlike 14, tooth 15 has a single root, a more rounded ovoid occlusal outline, and a wrinkled occlusal surface.

Which combination of features accurately describes the permanent maxillary second premolar (15, 25) in contrast to the maxillary first premolar (14, 24)?

  1. A. Three roots and a deep mesial concavity
  2. B. Longer mesial cusp slope and a mesial groove
  3. C. Two buccal cusps, from five lobes
  4. D. One root, cusps of nearly equal height
  5. E. A root shorter than its crown
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. One root, cusps of nearly equal height

Although the permanent maxillary first (14/24) and second (15/25) premolars share the same general class traits (both develop from four lobes—three buccal and one palatal—and both have crowns that are wider buccolingually [~9.0 mm] than mesiodistally [~7.0 mm], unlike any anterior tooth), six systematic differences separate the maxillary second premolar from the first premolar: (1) Cusp Height Symmetry: On the maxillary first premolar, the palatal cusp is noticeably (~1 mm) shorter than the buccal cusp; on the maxillary second premolar, the buccal and palatal cusps are of almost EQUAL height and equal mesiodistal width, making the proximal profile balanced and the occlusal outline smoothly ovoid rather than sharply angular/hexagonal. (2) Buccal Cusp Slopes: Whereas the first premolar has an anomalous longer mesial buccal cusp slope, the second premolar obeys the standard rule: its mesial buccal cusp slope is SHORTER than its distal buccal cusp slope (placing the buccal cusp tip slightly mesial to the root axis). (3) Absence of Mesial Crown Concavity and Mesial Marginal Groove: The mesial surface of the maxillary second premolar crown is smoothly convex with no mesial crown developmental depression (in fact, on the root of the second premolar, the DISTAL longitudinal root depression is deeper than the mesial root depression!), and no developmental groove crosses the mesial marginal ridge. (4) Occlusal Groove Pattern: On the first premolar, the central developmental groove is long between widely spaced mesial and distal pits with very few supplemental grooves; on the second premolar, the mesial and distal pits lie much closer to the centre of the occlusal table, making the central developmental groove short and giving off numerous radiating supplemental grooves that create a characteristic "wrinkled" occlusal surface. (5) Root and Canal Number: About 85% of maxillary second premolars have a single root (whose length equals or slightly exceeds that of the first premolar, ~14.0 mm) containing a single wide ribbon-shaped canal in ~60-75% of cases (or two canals that often join apically).

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Three roots and a deep mesial concavity: These are the exact opposite of the maxillary second premolar: it has one root, a short central groove, no mesial crown concavity, and buccal and lingual cusps of nearly equal height.
  • B. Longer mesial cusp slope and a mesial groove: A longer mesial buccal cusp slope and a mesial marginal developmental groove are exclusive traits of the maxillary FIRST premolar; on the maxillary second premolar, the mesial buccal cusp slope is shorter than the distal slope and the mesial marginal ridge is unbroken.
  • C. Two buccal cusps, from five lobes: All maxillary premolars (first and second) develop from four lobes (three buccal and one lingual) and have one buccal and one lingual cusp; three-cusped premolars Developing from five lobes occur in the mandibular second premolar.
  • E. A root shorter than its crown: Like the maxillary first premolar, the root of the maxillary second premolar is ~14.0 mm long (nearly twice the ~7.5-8.5 mm crown length) and is flattened mesiodistally (wider buccolingually) in cross-section.

Key point: Contrast the permanent maxillary second premolar with the first premolar across six traits: single root/canal, equal buccal and lingual cusp heights, shorter mesial buccal cusp slope, absence of mesial crown concavity and mesial marginal groove (deeper distal root depression), and short central groove with numerous supplemental grooves.

Question 5 · Oral mucosa, saliva, oral physiology and eruption · easy

During an oral physiology tutorial in Gilgit, a lecturer compares the digestive enzymes present in human saliva with the polypeptide growth factors secreted by the granular convoluted tubules and striated ducts of the submandibular gland.

Which digestive enzyme do the parotid and submandibular glands secrete into saliva?

  1. A. Pepsin
  2. B. Alpha-amylase
  3. C. Maltase
  4. D. Bile salt-activated lipase
  5. E. Trypsin
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Alpha-amylase

Human saliva contains two true digestive enzymes alongside important tissue-protective polypeptides: (1) Salivary alpha-amylase (formerly termed ptyalin) is the single most abundant enzyme in saliva, synthesized and stored in the zymogen granules of parotid serous acinar cells (where it represents ~20% to 30% of total parotid protein) and to a lesser extent (~20% of parotid levels) in submandibular serous acini. Alpha-amylase is a calcium-metalloenzyme that requires chloride ions (Cl-) as an obligate allosteric activator and operates at an optimal pH of 6.7 to 6.9. It is an endo-glycosidase that cleaves internal alpha-(1,4)-glycosidic bonds of cooked dietary starch (amylose and amylopectin) and glycogen into maltose, maltotriose, and branched alpha-limit dextrins (it cannot cleave alpha-1,6 branch points or beta-1,4 cellulose). Although swallowed salivary amylase is inactivated once gastric hydrochloric acid penetrates the food bolus and drops pH below 4.0, it continues digesting starch inside the protected core of the gastric bolus for 15 to 30 minutes; more importantly for dental health, salivary amylase dissolves sticky cooked starch debris Impacted in occlusal fissures and interproximal spaces so it can be washed away before plaque bacteria ferment it. (2) Lingual lipase is a hydrophobic enzyme secreted exclusively by the pure serous minor lingual salivary glands of von Ebner located beneath the circumvallate and foliate papillae. Unlike amylase, lingual lipase has an acidic-to-neutral pH optimum (pH 2.0 to 6.0) and requires no bile salts, remaining active inside the acidic stomach to hydrolyse medium- and long-chain triglycerides—a critical mechanism for digesting milk fat in neonates whose pancreatic lipase is still immature. Additionally, striated ducts secrete Epidermal Growth Factor (EGF, promoting oral and oesophageal mucosal repair alongside histatins), Nerve Growth Factor (NGF), and kallikrein (which cleaves plasma kininogen into the potent vasodilator bradykinin to boost glandular blood flow during active secretion).

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Pepsin: Saliva contains NO digestive proteases like pepsin or trypsin; protein digestion begins in the stomach, not the oral cavity.
  • C. Maltase: Human acinar saliva does not secrete disaccharidases (maltase, sucrase, lactase); fermentable monosaccharides and lactic acid on teeth are produced by dental plaque bacterial metabolism.
  • D. Bile salt-activated lipase: Bile salts and pancreatic elastase are exocrine hepatobiliary and pancreatic secretions entering the duodenum, not salivary enzymes.
  • E. Trypsin: Trypsin is a pancreatic enzyme, not a salivary one.

Key point: Contrast salivary alpha-amylase (parotid/submandibular serous origin, Cl- cofactor, pH 6.8 optimum, internal alpha-1,4 starch cleavage and oral debris clearance) with lingual lipase (von Ebner serous origin, acid-stable in stomach for neonatal fat digestion), and note ductal EGF and kallikrein.

Question 6 · Embryology of the face, mouth and teeth · easy

A dentist at a teaching hospital in Bahawalpur evaluates periapical radiographs of a 5-year-old child, a 22-year-old adult with complete dentition, and a 68-year-old man whose upper left molars (26, 27, 28) were extracted 15 years ago. In the 68-year-old patient, only a paper-thin cortical lamina separates the oral mucosa of the posterior edentulous ridge from a large radiolucent cavity.

Which statement correctly describes the normal development and age changes of this radiolucent cavity?

  1. A. It first appears after birth, with the primary molars
  2. B. It starts in fetal life and enlarges as the teeth erupt
  3. C. It comes from a pharyngeal pouch and is adult-sized at birth
  4. D. It starts in the palatine bone and moves forward
  5. E. It shrinks after age 30 as bone is laid on its floor
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. It starts in fetal life and enlarges as the teeth erupt

The maxillary sinus (antrum of Highmore) is the first of the paranasal sinuses to develop. At approximately the third month of intrauterine life (week 10 to 12), the mucosa of the ethmoidal infundibulum in the middle nasal meatus invaginates laterally into the ectomesenchyme of the developing maxilla. At birth, the sinus is still a small, slit-like or pea-sized cavity Located medial to the infraorbital foramen above the deciduous molar germs. Postnatally, it enlarges in two major spurts correlated with dental eruption: first around ages 6 to 7 years (eruption of first permanent molars) and second around puberty to 18 years as the premolars, second molars, and third molars erupt and the alveolar process grows downward. Following extraction of maxillary molars, loss of functional bone stimulation permits osteoclastic pneumatisation of the sinus floor down to the edentulous alveolar crest.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. It first appears after birth, with the primary molars: The maxillary sinus is the first paranasal sinus to develop in utero, appearing at about 3 months of fetal life as an outpouching of the lateral nasal wall.
  • C. It comes from a pharyngeal pouch and is adult-sized at birth: At birth the maxillary sinus is only a small tubular sac (~7 to 8 mm long) derived from nasal ectoderm, reaching adult size only after permanent third molar eruption.
  • D. It starts in the palatine bone and moves forward: The sinus develops entirely within the body of the maxilla lateral to the nasal cavity and does not extend into the premaxillary incisor region.
  • E. It shrinks after age 30 as bone is laid on its floor: Rather than shrinking, the maxillary sinus enlarges in older adults through osteoclastic resorption of its walls, especially when posterior teeth and their periodontal ligaments are lost.

Key point: Trace the fetal origin and postnatal dental-dependent expansion and post-extraction pneumatisation of the maxillary sinus.

Question 7 · Oral histology: enamel, dentine, pulp and periodontium · easy

During an oral histology tutorial in Muzaffarabad, a student examines a decalcified longitudinal section of a primary canine and notes fine rhythmic lines running strictly perpendicular to the dentinal tubules spaced ~20 µm apart, along with several accentuated dark bands—including a prominent band marking the day of birth.

What are the fine 5-day rhythmic lines in dentine called?

  1. A. Cross-striations
  2. B. Resting lines of Salter
  3. C. Hunter-Schreger bands
  4. D. Von Ebner lines
  5. E. Contour lines of Owen
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. Von Ebner lines

Like enamel, primary dentine is deposited rhythmically at an average daily rate of approximately 4 µm/day in the crown (slowing slightly in the root), leaving three levels of incremental markings running perpendicular to the course of the dentinal tubules: (1) Imbrication (incremental) lines of von Ebner are fine, regular lines running at right angles to the dentinal tubules; ultra-fine daily increments (~4 µm apart) group into prominent 5-day rhythmic lines spaced approximately 18 to 20 µm apart, reflecting periodic shifts in the orientation of type I collagen fibrils and mineral density every 5 days. (2) Contour lines of Owen are wider, accentuated hypomineralised bands that follow the same incremental contour as lines of von Ebner; they represent either phases of systemic metabolic/nutritional disturbance that impair calcification or optical coincidence of adjacent secondary curvatures of the dentinal tubules, making them the exact dentinal homologue of the striae of Retzius in enamel. (3) The neonatal line is the most prominent contour line of Owen, formed during the ~10-to-15 day physiological pause and nutritional transition at birth; it is found in all 20 primary teeth and the mesiobuccal cusp tips of the first permanent molars.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Cross-striations: Cross-striations are daily lines in enamel, not dentine.
  • B. Resting lines of Salter: Salter lines are incremental lines in cementum.
  • C. Hunter-Schreger bands: Hunter-Schreger bands are an optical effect in enamel.
  • E. Contour lines of Owen: Owen lines are the accentuated, broader bands of disturbed mineralisation, such as the neonatal line, not the regular 5-day lines.

Key point: Identify the incremental lines of von Ebner (~4 µm/day and ~20 µm/5-day intervals perpendicular to tubules), contour lines of Owen (homologous to striae of Retzius), and the dentinal neonatal line.

Question 8 · Orofacial anatomy for dentistry · easy

A 22-year-old woman undergoes extraoral incision and drainage of a left submandibular space abscess at a hospital in Quetta. The house officer places a horizontal skin incision 5 mm below the inferior border of the left mandibular body near the facial artery notch. Postoperatively, when the patient smiles, the left corner of her lower lip fails to pull downward and remains elevated and asymmetric.

Which nerve was injured during this incision?

  1. A. Buccal branch of CN VII
  2. B. Cervical branch of CN VII
  3. C. Mental branch of the inferior alveolar nerve
  4. D. Marginal mandibular branch of CN VII
  5. E. Zygomatic branch of CN VII
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. Marginal mandibular branch of CN VII

Among the five terminal motor branches of the facial nerve emerging from the anterior border of the parotid gland, the marginal mandibular branch is the most frequently injured during extraoral submandibular surgery (such as submandibular gland excision, open reduction of mandibular angle/body fractures, or drainage of submandibular abscesses). After leaving the inferior pole of the parotid, the marginal mandibular nerve runs forward toward the lower lip. Posterior to where the facial artery crosses the inferior border of the mandible (at the anteroinferior angle of the masseter), this nerve drops below the inferior border of the mandible into the submandibular triangle—lying between the platysma and the investing layer of deep cervical fascia, superficial to the posterior facial (retromandibular) vein and facial artery—in 20% to 50% of individuals by up to 1 to 1.2 cm. Anterior to the facial artery, it crosses above the lower border of the mandible to innervate the risorius, depressor anguli oris, depressor labii inferioris, and mentalis. Therefore, submandibular incisions must be placed at least 1.5 to 2 cm (one to two fingerbreadths) below the inferior border of the mandible.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Buccal branch of CN VII: The buccal branch supplies the cheek and upper lip muscles and runs with the parotid duct, well above the lower border of the mandible.
  • B. Cervical branch of CN VII: The cervical branch supplies platysma; its injury causes little visible lower lip asymmetry.
  • C. Mental branch of the inferior alveolar nerve: The mental nerve is sensory; its injury numbs the lip but does not paralyse it.
  • E. Zygomatic branch of CN VII: The zygomatic branch supplies orbicularis oculi, far from a submandibular incision.

Key point: Describe the course of the marginal mandibular branch of the facial nerve relative to the inferior border of the mandible and facial vessels, and state the safe distance for submandibular skin incisions.

Question 9 · Tooth morphology and occlusion · easy

During an oral anatomy spot test in Abbottabad, a student examines an extracted single-rooted premolar with a square-to-round occlusal outline. Unlike the mandibular first premolar, this tooth has a broad, functional lingual half bearing two lingual cusps separated by a lingual developmental groove, and its central, mesial,distal, and lingual grooves meet at a central pit to form a distinct letter "Y".

Which tooth is this?

  1. A. Maxillary second premolar
  2. B. Primary mandibular first molar
  3. C. Mandibular first premolar
  4. D. Maxillary first molar
  5. E. Mandibular second premolar
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Mandibular second premolar

The permanent mandibular second premolar (35 and 45) is significantly larger, better developed, and much more molariform in function than the mandibular first premolar (34 and 44), with a longer, stouter single root (~14.5 mm) whose apex lies directly adjacent to the mental foramen. Morphologically, it exists in two distinct anatomical types based on cusp number and occlusal groove pattern: (1) The Three-Cusp Type (the more common angular/square occlusal form, ~55% of cases): Uniquely among premolars, this variant develops from FIVE developmental lobes—three lobes forming the single large buccal cusp and two separate lobes forming two lingual cusps: a larger Mesiolingual (ML) cusp and a smaller Distolingual (DL) cusp. Thus, the strict order of cusp size from largest (tallest and widest) to smallest is: Buccal cusp > Mesiolingual cusp > Distolingual cusp! Because the mesiolingual cusp is much wider and taller than the distolingual cusp, the lingual developmental groove separating them is displaced distally off-centre—providing an instant landmark to tell left (35) from right (45). On the square occlusal table, the mesial, distal, and lingual developmental grooves converge at a deep Central Pit to form a classic "Y" groove pattern (and unlike 34/44, there is NO continuous transverse ridge). (2) The Two-Cusp Type (~43% to 45% of cases): Develops from FOUR lobes (three buccal + one lingual, just like anterior teeth and other premolars) and exhibits a rounded/ovoid occlusal outline with one large buccal cusp and one single well-developed lingual cusp of equal mesiodistal width (whose tip is often shifted mesially). Its central groove runs between mesial and distal developmental pits in either a crescentic "U" pattern or a straighter "H" pattern, with NO central pit and NO lingual developmental groove. In both variants of 35/45, the mesial marginal ridge sits at a normal higher (more occlusal) level than the distal marginal ridge.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Maxillary second premolar: All maxillary premolars have strictly two cusps (one buccal, one palatal) developed from four lobes; a premolar with one large buccal cusp and two lingual cusps (Y-pattern) is the permanent mandibular second premolar.
  • B. Primary mandibular first molar: All primary molars have multiple flared roots (two in mandibular molars) and four or five cusps, never a single root with a three-cusp Y-pattern.
  • C. Mandibular first premolar: The permanent mandibular first premolar has one tiny lingual cusp, a transverse ridge, and a mesiolingual groove rather than a central pit and Y-groove.
  • D. Maxillary first molar: The permanent maxillary first molar has four major cusps, an oblique ridge, and three roots rather than a single root and three-cusped premolar crown.

Key point: Compare the three-cusp (5 lobes, square outline, Y-groove pattern with central pit, Buccal > Mesiolingual > Distolingual cusp size) and two-cusp (4 lobes, round outline, U- or H-groove pattern without lingual groove) variants of the permanent mandibular second premolar.

Question 10 · Oral mucosa, saliva, oral physiology and eruption · easy

A videofluoroscopic barium swallow study at a teaching hospital in Peshawar evaluates a healthy 28-year-old volunteer swallowing a 10 mL bolus of liquid and paste. As the tongue tip presses against the anterior hard palate and the posterior teeth clamp into centric occlusion, the bolus passes the anterior tonsillar pillars and triggers an all-or-none involuntary reflex lasting less than 1 second during which breathing momentarily stops.

Which phase of swallowing is voluntary?

  1. A. All three phases
  2. B. The pharyngeal phase only
  3. C. The oesophageal phase only
  4. D. The oral phase only
  5. E. None of the phases
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. The oral phase only

Normal human deglutition (swallowing, occurring ~600 to 1,000 times per day—roughly 150 times during meals, 350 times while awake between meals, and 50 times during sleep) is divided into three sequential phases coordinated by the medullary swallowing centre (nucleus of the solitary tract and nucleus ambiguus): (1) The Oral (buccal) phase is the ONLY voluntary phase: the lips seal, the elevator muscles bring the posterior teeth into firm intercuspal contact (centric occlusion) to fix the mandible as a stable anchor so the suprahyoid muscles (mylohyoid, geniohyoid, digastric) can elevate the hyoid bone and floor of the mouth, and the intrinsic and extrinsic tongue muscles press upward and backward in a wave against the hard palate to propel the bolus past the palatoglossal arches (anterior faucial pillars) into the oropharynx. (2) The Pharyngeal phase is a rapid (~0.7 to 1.0 second), involuntary all-or-none reflex triggered when mechanoreceptors on the faucial pillars, soft palate, and posterior pharyngeal wall send afferents via CN IX and CN X (plus V2/V3) to the medulla. Simultaneously, four protective mechanisms seal off the respiratory tract: the soft palate elevates and tenses (levator and tensor veli palatini) against Passavant ridge of the superior constrictor to block nasal regurgitation; the respiration centre is inhibited (deglutition apnoea); the suprahyoid and longitudinal pharyngeal muscles (stylopharyngeus, salpingopharyngeus, palatopharyngeus) pull the hyoid bone and larynx upward and forward under the base of the tongue while the epiglottis folds over the laryngeal inlet and the aryepiglottic folds and true/false vocal cords adduct tightly (most critical barrier against aspiration); and the cricopharyngeus (upper oesophageal sphincter) relaxes as the sequential wave of superior, middle, and inferior pharyngeal constrictors propels the bolus into the oesophagus. (3) The Oesophageal phase (~5 to 10 seconds) is an involuntary vagal/enteric peristaltic wave (primary and secondary peristalsis) across striated upper-third and smooth lower-two-thirds muscle through the relaxing lower oesophageal sphincter into the stomach.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. All three phases: Only the first (oral) phase is voluntary; the pharyngeal and oesophageal phases are involuntary brainstem reflexes, and respiration is reflexly inhibited (deglutition apnoea) during the pharyngeal phase to prevent aspiration.
  • B. The pharyngeal phase only: The pharyngeal phase is rapid (lasting ~0.7 to 1.0 seconds), and during normal adult somatic swallowing the posterior teeth come into firm centric occlusion to stabilise the mandible for suprahyoid muscle contraction.
  • C. The oesophageal phase only: The oesophageal phase is involuntary peristalsis.
  • E. None of the phases: The oral phase is voluntary.

Key point: Distinguish the voluntary oral phase from the involuntary pharyngeal (~1 s) and oesophageal (~8-10 s) phases of swallowing, and detail the airway-protection mechanisms of the pharyngeal phase.
