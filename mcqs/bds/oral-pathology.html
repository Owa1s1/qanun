Question 1 · Cysts of the jaws and odontogenic tumours · easy

A 6-year-old girl is brought to a dental clinic in Mardan because of a soft, painless, bluish, dome-shaped swelling on the gum over the area where her lower left first permanent molar (36) is about to erupt. It is fluctuant and translucent. She is otherwise well, and a radiograph shows the molar close to the surface with no bone over the crown.

What is the most appropriate management?

  1. A. Observation until the tooth erupts
  2. B. Enucleation of the cyst under general anaesthesia
  3. C. Biopsy to exclude malignancy
  4. D. Antibiotics for a dental abscess
  5. E. Extraction of the erupting molar
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Observation until the tooth erupts

An eruption cyst is a dentigerous cyst in the soft tissue over an erupting tooth, after the tooth has emerged from bone but before it has broken through the gum. It forms a soft, fluctuant, translucent swelling on the alveolar ridge; bleeding into it makes it blue or purple (eruption haematoma). It is most common over erupting primary teeth and permanent first molars and incisors. Most resolve spontaneously when the tooth erupts through the cyst. If it persists, is painful, or the tooth is delayed, the roof can be excised (uncapped) to expose the crown.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Enucleation of the cyst under general anaesthesia: Enucleation is not needed for a superficial eruption cyst, which resolves as the tooth erupts.
  • C. Biopsy to exclude malignancy: A soft, bluish swelling over an erupting tooth in a child is a classic eruption cyst and does not need biopsy.
  • D. Antibiotics for a dental abscess: There is no infection; an eruption cyst is a fluid-filled swelling over an erupting tooth.
  • E. Extraction of the erupting molar: Extracting a healthy first molar is not appropriate for an eruption cyst.

Key point: Recognise an eruption cyst and know that it usually resolves as the tooth erupts.

Question 2 · Epithelial pathology, potentially malignant disorders and oral cancer · easy

A 58-year-old man from Bannu has used naswar in the left lower buccal vestibule for 35 years. He now has a painless, indurated ulcer with rolled edges in that vestibule extending onto the gingiva. He does not smoke or drink alcohol. Biopsy confirms invasive squamous cell carcinoma.

What best explains the site of his cancer?

  1. A. Cancer always begins on the lateral border of the tongue
  2. B. Spread from a primary cancer in the lung
  3. C. Carcinogen contact at the placement site
  4. D. Sunlight exposure of the lower lip
  5. E. Infection with HPV 16 from the oropharynx
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Carcinogen contact at the placement site

Naswar, a smokeless tobacco product common in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, Balochistan and Afghanistan, contains tobacco, slaked lime and other ingredients. It is held in the labial or buccal vestibule or under the tongue for long periods, delivering tobacco-specific nitrosamines (such as NNK and NNN) in an alkaline environment that increases absorption and irritates the mucosa. Repeated exposure causes keratosis, dysplasia and eventually squamous cell carcinoma at the site of placement. This explains why buccal and vestibular cancers are common in South Asia, while lateral tongue and floor-of-mouth cancers predominate in Western countries where smoking and alcohol are the main risk factors.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Cancer always begins on the lateral border of the tongue: The lateral tongue is the commonest site in Western countries, but in South Asia cancers often arise where quids or naswar are held.
  • B. Spread from a primary cancer in the lung: An indurated ulcer at the site of long naswar placement is a primary oral cancer, not a lung metastasis.
  • D. Sunlight exposure of the lower lip: Sunlight causes lip cancer on the vermilion, not cancer in the buccal vestibule.
  • E. Infection with HPV 16 from the oropharynx: HPV 16 mainly causes oropharyngeal cancer, not vestibular cancers at a naswar placement site.

Key point: Relate the site of oral squamous cell carcinoma to the placement of smokeless tobacco such as naswar.

Question 3 · Caries, pulp and periapical disease, and oral infections · easy

A 32-year-old woman who smokes 15 cigarettes a day had her lower right third molar removed three days ago. She now has severe, throbbing pain radiating to her ear, not relieved by paracetamol, and a bad taste. The socket is empty of clot, with exposed, bare bone and food debris, and there is no swelling or fever.

What is the main mechanism of this condition?

  1. A. Bacterial abscess spreading into the neck
  2. B. Breakdown of the clot by fibrinolysis
  3. C. A retained root fragment causing a cyst
  4. D. Fracture of the mandible
  5. E. Allergy to the local anaesthetic
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Breakdown of the clot by fibrinolysis

Alveolar osteitis (dry socket) occurs in a few percent of extractions, more often after lower third molar removal, in smokers, women on oral contraceptives, after traumatic extractions and with poor oral hygiene. The blood clot breaks down, mainly due to increased local fibrinolysis (plasminogen activators released from traumatised bone and bacteria), leaving bare bone exposed. It causes severe pain starting 2-4 days after extraction, often radiating to the ear, with bad taste and odour, but usually without swelling or fever. Treatment is irrigation of the socket with saline or chlorhexidine, a soothing dressing (such as one containing eugenol), and analgesia; antibiotics are not usually needed. Patients should avoid smoking after extraction.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Bacterial abscess spreading into the neck: There is no swelling or fever; this is not a spreading infection.
  • C. A retained root fragment causing a cyst: A retained root may delay healing, but a cyst does not form in three days.
  • D. Fracture of the mandible: Fracture would cause deformity, mobility or malocclusion, not an empty, painful socket.
  • E. Allergy to the local anaesthetic: An allergic reaction would cause swelling, rash or anaphylaxis soon after injection, not this picture.

Key point: Explain the fibrinolytic mechanism and management of dry socket.

Question 4 · Immunological, skin, blood and metabolic disease; injuries and forensic odontology · easy

A 40-year-old man in Peshawar had toothache in his lower right first molar (46) and held an aspirin tablet against the gum and cheek beside it for several hours to relieve the pain. The next day he has a painful, white, wrinkled patch on the buccal mucosa and gingiva next to the tooth, which peels away to leave a raw, bleeding surface.

What is the diagnosis?

  1. A. Chemical burn from aspirin
  2. B. Pseudomembranous candidosis
  3. C. Leukoplakia
  4. D. Lichen planus
  5. E. Primary herpetic gingivostomatitis
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Chemical burn from aspirin

Chemical burns of the oral mucosa occur when caustic substances contact it for long periods. Aspirin is a classic example: people with toothache sometimes hold a tablet against the gum or cheek, and the acetylsalicylic acid causes coagulative necrosis of the epithelium. The area turns white and wrinkled, and the necrotic layer peels away, leaving a painful, raw, bleeding surface. Other agents include hydrogen peroxide, phenol, silver nitrate, sodium hypochlorite (during root canal treatment), and some traditional remedies. Treatment is removing the cause, pain relief and allowing healing over 1-2 weeks; the underlying dental problem should also be treated. Patients should be advised to swallow analgesics, not hold them against the tissues.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Pseudomembranous candidosis: Candida plaques are not linked to holding a tablet and leave only a slightly red surface when wiped.
  • C. Leukoplakia: Leukoplakia is chronic and does not peel away to a raw surface the day after a chemical exposure.
  • D. Lichen planus: Lichen planus forms bilateral white striae over months, not a sudden peeling patch at one site.
  • E. Primary herpetic gingivostomatitis: Herpes causes vesicles and fever, usually in children, not a single white patch where a tablet was placed.

Key point: Recognise an aspirin burn as a chemical injury of the oral mucosa.

Question 5 · Developmental disturbances of teeth, jaws and oral tissues · medium

A 14-year-old boy from Dir presents to a dental hospital in Peshawar complaining that his teeth are creamy-brown and chip easily when he chews. His maternal uncle has the same dental appearance. He grew up drinking tested municipal water with a fluoride level of 0.3 ppm. Intraoral examination shows that all permanent teeth have normal crown thickness at the contact points, but the enamel is mottled opaque white to yellowish-brown, soft enough to be pierced by a sharp dental probe, and chips away from the underlying normal dentine. Periapical radiographs show normal pulp chambers and root morphology, but the enamel has roughly the same radiodensity as the underlying dentine.

Which stage of enamel formation is primarily defective in this patient?

  1. A. Crystallite growth and protein removal
  2. B. Initial secretion of organic matrix
  3. C. Morphodifferentiation at the bell stage
  4. D. Odontoblastic predentine mineralisation
  5. E. Primary nucleation of hydroxyapatite
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Crystallite growth and protein removal

Amelogenesis imperfecta (AI) comprises a group of hereditary disorders affecting enamel structure across both dentitions without systemic abnormalities. Witkop classified AI into three main types corresponding to the stages of amelogenesis. In hypoplastic AI (defective matrix deposition), the enamel is thin, pitted, or grooved, but hard and more radiopaque than dentine. In hypocalcified AI (defective initial mineralisation), enamel forms in normal thickness but is extremely soft, friable, and less radiopaque than dentine, wearing away rapidly after eruption. In hypomaturation AI (defective crystallite maturation and proteolytic removal of amelogenin matrix via MMP20 or KLK4), enamel is of normal thickness at eruption with normal contact points, mottled opaque white to brown (including "snow-capped" patterns), can be penetrated with a probe tip under pressure, and has a radiodensity similar to dentine.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Initial secretion of organic matrix: Defective matrix secretion by ameloblasts causes hypoplastic amelogenesis imperfecta, where enamel is abnormally thin or pitted but hard and radiopaque.
  • C. Morphodifferentiation at the bell stage: Disturbances of morphodifferentiation alter crown size and shape (such as peg laterals) without making the enamel soft or radiolucent.
  • D. Odontoblastic predentine mineralisation: Defective predentine mineralisation produces dentinogenesis imperfecta with bulbous crowns and obliterated pulps, whereas this patient has normal dentine and pulps.
  • E. Primary nucleation of hydroxyapatite: Failure of initial calcium salt deposition causes hypocalcified amelogenesis imperfecta, where enamel is extremely cheesy, rapidly lost on eruption, and less radiopaque than dentine.

Key point: Differentiate hypomaturation amelogenesis imperfecta from hypoplastic and hypocalcified forms using enamel thickness, probe hardness, and radiographic density.

Question 6 · Bone, soft-tissue and salivary gland pathology · easy

A 40-year-old woman attends a hospital in Islamabad with a painless, slowly growing, firm, mobile lump in front of her right ear for three years. Facial nerve function is normal. Fine-needle aspiration shows epithelial and myoepithelial cells in a fibrillary chondromyxoid background.

What is the most likely diagnosis?

  1. A. Warthin tumour
  2. B. Mucoepidermoid carcinoma
  3. C. Adenoid cystic carcinoma
  4. D. Pleomorphic adenoma
  5. E. Lipoma
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. Pleomorphic adenoma

Pleomorphic adenoma is the commonest salivary gland tumour, accounting for about two-thirds of all salivary tumours, most often in the superficial lobe of the parotid in middle-aged women. It is a painless, slow-growing, firm, mobile mass, and facial nerve function is preserved. Its name reflects its mixed appearance: epithelial cells forming ducts, myoepithelial cells (plasmacytoid or spindle), and a mesenchyme-like stroma that may be myxoid, chondroid or hyaline. Fine-needle aspiration shows these elements in a fibrillary, magenta chondromyxoid background. Treatment is complete surgical excision with a margin.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Warthin tumour: Warthin tumour is common in older male smokers and shows oncocytic cells with lymphocytes, not a chondromyxoid background.
  • B. Mucoepidermoid carcinoma: Mucoepidermoid carcinoma shows mucous and epidermoid cells, not a chondromyxoid stroma.
  • C. Adenoid cystic carcinoma: Adenoid cystic carcinoma shows basaloid cells around hyaline globules and often causes pain or nerve palsy.
  • E. Lipoma: A lipoma would show mature fat cells, not epithelial cells and chondromyxoid stroma.

Key point: Recognise pleomorphic adenoma as the commonest salivary gland tumour and its cytological features.

Question 7 · Cysts of the jaws and odontogenic tumours · easy

A dental student in Multan reviews the pathology records of odontogenic tumours from a teaching hospital over ten years. Most of the lesions were found on radiographs taken because a permanent tooth had failed to erupt, and many were removed from children and teenagers. Some looked like small clusters of tooth-like structures, while others were irregular radiopaque masses.

Which lesion is most likely the commonest in her records?

  1. A. Ameloblastoma
  2. B. Odontogenic myxoma
  3. C. Cementoblastoma
  4. D. Ameloblastic fibroma
  5. E. Odontoma
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Odontoma

Odontomas are the commonest odontogenic tumours and are generally regarded as developmental malformations (hamartomas) rather than true neoplasms. They are usually discovered in the first two decades, often on radiographs taken because a permanent tooth has not erupted or a primary tooth is retained. The compound odontoma contains many small tooth-like denticles and occurs most often in the anterior maxilla; the complex odontoma is an irregular mass of enamel, dentine and other tissues, usually in the posterior mandible. Both are surrounded by a radiolucent rim. Treatment is simple enucleation, which is curative, followed by monitoring of the eruption of the affected tooth.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Ameloblastoma: Ameloblastoma is the most clinically important odontogenic tumour, but it is less common than odontoma.
  • B. Odontogenic myxoma: Myxoma is uncommon and appears as a radiolucency, not tooth-like structures.
  • C. Cementoblastoma: Cementoblastoma is rare and fused to the root of a lower molar.
  • D. Ameloblastic fibroma: Ameloblastic fibroma is rare and radiolucent, without tooth-like structures.

Key point: Recognise odontoma as the commonest odontogenic tumour, often discovered because of a failure of eruption.

Question 8 · Epithelial pathology, potentially malignant disorders and oral cancer · easy

A 45-year-old woman attends a dental clinic in Lahore because her dentist noticed a flat, painless, blue-grey patch, 5 mm across, on the gingiva next to a lower molar that had an amalgam filling and an apicectomy with retrograde amalgam ten years ago. It has not changed in size. A radiograph shows tiny radiopaque particles in the soft tissue at that site.

What is the most likely diagnosis?

  1. A. Oral melanoma
  2. B. Melanotic macule
  3. C. Blue naevus
  4. D. Amalgam tattoo
  5. E. Kaposi sarcoma
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. Amalgam tattoo

Amalgam tattoo (localised argyrosis) is the commonest pigmented lesion of the oral mucosa. Fragments of amalgam become implanted in the soft tissue during placement or removal of fillings, extractions or apicectomy with retrograde amalgam. It appears as a flat, blue-grey or black, painless patch, most often on the gingiva, alveolar mucosa or buccal mucosa near restored teeth, and remains stable in size. Radiographs may show fine radiopaque particles, though small amounts may not be visible. Histology shows black granules along collagen fibres and around vessels, sometimes with a foreign body reaction. A diagnosis can be made clinically when typical; biopsy is needed if there is doubt, to exclude melanoma.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Oral melanoma: Melanoma is usually larger, irregular, may grow or change, and is not associated with radiopaque particles.
  • B. Melanotic macule: A melanotic macule is a small brown patch of melanin, without radiopaque particles.
  • C. Blue naevus: A blue naevus is a deep collection of melanocytes, usually on the palate, without radiopaque particles.
  • E. Kaposi sarcoma: Kaposi sarcoma is purple, may be raised and multifocal, and is associated with HIV or immunosuppression.

Key point: Diagnose an amalgam tattoo from its stable blue-grey appearance near amalgam restorations and radiopaque particles.

Question 9 · Caries, pulp and periapical disease, and oral infections · easy

A 50-year-old man in Lahore with asthma started a high-dose inhaled corticosteroid two months ago. He now has creamy white plaques on his soft palate and tongue that can be wiped off with gauze, leaving red, slightly bleeding areas underneath. He does not rinse his mouth after using the inhaler. He has no other illness.

What is the diagnosis and the best preventive advice?

  1. A. Leukoplakia; stop the inhaler
  2. B. Lichen planus; topical steroids
  3. C. Candidosis; rinse after each use
  4. D. Hairy leukoplakia; antiretroviral treatment
  5. E. White sponge naevus; reassurance
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Candidosis; rinse after each use

Pseudomembranous candidosis (thrush) presents as creamy white plaques that can be wiped off, leaving a red, sometimes bleeding surface. The plaques consist of desquamated epithelial cells, fibrin, inflammatory cells and Candida hyphae and yeasts. Predisposing factors include infancy and old age, inhaled or systemic corticosteroids, broad-spectrum antibiotics, dry mouth, diabetes, HIV infection and other immunodeficiencies. Inhaled steroids deposit in the mouth and pharynx, suppressing local immunity. Treatment is topical antifungals (nystatin or miconazole) or fluconazole; prevention includes rinsing the mouth or brushing after inhaler use and using a spacer device. Recurrent thrush without an obvious cause should prompt testing for diabetes and HIV.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Leukoplakia; stop the inhaler: Leukoplakia cannot be wiped off, and stopping an asthma inhaler is unsafe.
  • B. Lichen planus; topical steroids: Lichen planus gives fixed white striae, and more steroid would worsen candidosis.
  • D. Hairy leukoplakia; antiretroviral treatment: Hairy leukoplakia cannot be wiped off and occurs on the lateral tongue in immunosuppression.
  • E. White sponge naevus; reassurance: White sponge naevus is present from childhood and cannot be wiped off.

Key point: Recognise pseudomembranous candidosis and prevent it in patients using inhaled corticosteroids.

Question 10 · Immunological, skin, blood and metabolic disease; injuries and forensic odontology · easy

A 34-year-old man in Lahore with untreated HIV infection (CD4 count 150 cells/µL) has painless, white, vertically corrugated patches on both lateral borders of his tongue. They cannot be wiped off and do not respond to antifungal treatment.

Which virus causes this lesion?

  1. A. Candida albicans
  2. B. Human papillomavirus
  3. C. Herpes simplex virus
  4. D. Cytomegalovirus
  5. E. Epstein-Barr virus
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Epstein-Barr virus

Oral lesions are common in HIV infection and other immunodeficiencies and may be the first sign. They include candidosis (pseudomembranous, erythematous, angular cheilitis), oral hairy leukoplakia, Kaposi sarcoma, non-Hodgkin lymphoma, necrotising periodontal disease, recurrent herpes, HPV warts and salivary gland disease. Oral hairy leukoplakia is caused by Epstein-Barr virus replicating in the upper epithelium; it appears as asymptomatic, white, corrugated or 'hairy' plaques on the lateral tongue that cannot be wiped off, and histology shows koilocyte-like ballooned cells. It is not premalignant and usually improves with antiretroviral therapy. It also occurs in transplant recipients and others on immunosuppression.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Candida albicans: Candida is a fungus, and these patches did not respond to antifungals.
  • B. Human papillomavirus: HPV causes papillomas and warts, not corrugated lateral tongue patches.
  • C. Herpes simplex virus: HSV causes vesicles and ulcers.
  • D. Cytomegalovirus: CMV causes deep ulcers in severe immunosuppression.

Key point: Identify Epstein-Barr virus as the cause of oral hairy leukoplakia.
