Question 1 · Paediatric dentistry · easy

The mother of a 10-month-old boy in Multan notices a soft, dome-shaped, bluish swelling over the gum where his lower first primary molar is about to erupt. The baby is feeding normally and has no fever.

What is the most appropriate management?

  1. A. Reassure and observe
  2. B. Urgent biopsy
  3. C. Extract the primary molar
  4. D. Prescribe antibiotics
  5. E. Drain under general anaesthesia
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Reassure and observe

An eruption cyst is a soft-tissue variant of a dentigerous cyst over an erupting tooth, seen as a smooth, translucent or bluish swelling on the alveolar ridge; when it contains blood it is called an eruption haematoma. It usually ruptures as the tooth erupts and needs only reassurance; surgical uncovering is reserved for cysts that persist, become infected or are painful. Teething can cause drooling, gum rubbing and irritability, but not high fever or diarrhoea, which suggest illness. Natal teeth (present at birth) and neonatal teeth (in the first month) are usually primary incisors and are kept unless very mobile (aspiration risk) or causing feeding problems or ulceration (Riga-Fede disease).

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Urgent biopsy: This is a typical benign finding needing no biopsy.
  • C. Extract the primary molar: Extraction is not needed; the tooth erupts normally.
  • D. Prescribe antibiotics: There is no infection.
  • E. Drain under general anaesthesia: Drainage under GA is excessive for this self-limiting cyst.

Key point: Manage an eruption cyst in an infant.

Question 2 · Paediatric dentistry · medium

A 6-year-old girl attends a dental teaching hospital in Peshawar for her first dental visit. She allows the dentist to examine her mouth, answers questions in short sentences, and accepts prophylaxis with cautious cooperation. She does not cry, withdraw, or refuse treatment. Her behaviour is cooperative but not enthusiastic.

According to the Frankl behaviour rating scale, how is the behaviour of this child best classified?

  1. A. Frankl 1 – definitely negative
  2. B. Frankl 2 – negative
  3. C. No Frankl rating is possible at the first visit
  4. D. Frankl 3 – positive
  5. E. Frankl 4 – definitely positive
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. Frankl 3 – positive

The Frankl behaviour rating scale classifies cooperation in a child during a dental visit into four categories. Frankl 1 is definitely negative, with refusal of treatment and forceful crying. Frankl 2 is negative, with reluctant acceptance and withdrawal. Frankl 3 is positive, with acceptance of treatment and cautious cooperation. Frankl 4 is definitely positive, with good rapport and obvious enjoyment of treatment. This child allows examination, answers briefly, and accepts prophylaxis cautiously without crying or refusal. That pattern fits Frankl 3 positive rather than the enthusiastic response of Frankl 4 or the refusal of Frankl 1 and 2. The rating can be made during the first visit once behaviour is observed.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Frankl 1 – definitely negative: She cooperates cautiously rather than refusing treatment or crying forcefully, so she does not show a definitely negative response.
  • B. Frankl 2 – negative: She accepts treatment willingly and cooperates with the dentist, so she is not reluctant or withdrawn.
  • C. No Frankl rating is possible at the first visit: Her cooperation during examination allows immediate rating; waiting for treatment is not required to assign a Frankl score.
  • E. Frankl 4 – definitely positive: She is not enthusiastic or clearly enjoying treatment, so she does not meet the criteria for definitely positive behaviour.

Key point: Assess behaviour in a child using the Frankl scale.

Question 3 · Paediatric dentistry · medium

A 5-year-old boy attends a private dental clinic in Mardan for restoration of 74, the lower left first primary molar. He cries when the handpiece is mentioned and refuses to open his mouth. The dentist explains the procedure using simple words, demonstrates the instrument on his fingernail, and then begins treatment.

Which behaviour-guidance technique is being used?

  1. A. Tell-show-do
  2. B. Voice control
  3. C. Non-verbal communication
  4. D. Positive reinforcement
  5. E. Distraction
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Tell-show-do

Tell-show-do is a non-pharmacological behaviour-guidance technique in which the dentist first explains the procedure in age-appropriate language, then demonstrates the instrument or sensation on a safe site such as a fingernail, and finally carries out the treatment. It reduces fear of the unknown and builds trust before local anaesthesia or handpiece use. In this story, the dentist explains with simple words, shows the instrument on the fingernail, and then begins treatment. That sequence is tell-show-do. Voice control, non-verbal communication, positive reinforcement and distraction are different techniques and are not described here. Tell-show-do is especially useful for anxious children who refuse to open their mouth.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Voice control: Voice control uses a sudden change in voice volume to gain attention, which is not described and could frighten this crying boy.
  • C. Non-verbal communication: Non-verbal communication relies on facial expression and posture alone, whereas this dentist uses spoken explanation and demonstration.
  • D. Positive reinforcement: Positive reinforcement rewards desired behaviour after it occurs, but the story shows preparation before treatment, not a reward afterwards.
  • E. Distraction: Distraction diverts attention away from the procedure, but the dentist focuses attention on the instrument and the steps.

Key point: Identify the tell-show-do behaviour-guidance technique.

Question 4 · Paediatric dentistry · medium

A 6-year-old boy attends a dental OPD in Swat. He is rated Frankl 3 positive on his first visit and accepts examination. For restoration of 36, the lower left first permanent molar, the dentist gives an inferior alveolar nerve block. After the injection, the boy becomes tearful and refuses to open his mouth for the preparation.

What is the most likely explanation for the change in behaviour?

  1. A. The Frankl rating is a fixed trait that cannot change between visits
  2. B. The injection caused a negative experience and shifted him to Frankl 2
  3. C. He has developed a true dental phobia after one visit
  4. D. Tell-show-do was not used before the injection
  5. E. He requires general anaesthesia for all future restorative treatment
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. The injection caused a negative experience and shifted him to Frankl 2

Frankl behaviour ratings describe how a child behaves at a particular visit and can change from one appointment to the next. A painful or frightening local anaesthetic injection is a common reason for a child to shift from cautious cooperation to reluctant acceptance and withdrawal. This corresponds to a move from Frankl 3 positive to Frankl 2 negative. One bad experience does not by itself create a true dental phobia, and the rating is not fixed for life. General anaesthesia is not needed for one restoration; the dentist can rebuild trust with tell-show-do, a gentle approach, and careful technique. Recognising that behaviour is dynamic helps the dentist avoid overreacting to a single difficult visit.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. The Frankl rating is a fixed trait that cannot change between visits: Frankl behaviour can vary from visit to visit and is not a fixed personality trait.
  • C. He has developed a true dental phobia after one visit: A single negative experience does not establish a true phobia, which involves persistent irrational fear and avoidance.
  • D. Tell-show-do was not used before the injection: Tell-show-do may help but the immediate change follows the injection, not the absence of a prior demonstration.
  • E. He requires general anaesthesia for all future restorative treatment: General anaesthesia is not indicated for a single restoration; behaviour can be rebuilt with non-pharmacological techniques.

Key point: Recognise that Frankl behaviour can change after a stressful event such as an injection.

Question 5 · Paediatric dentistry · medium

A 3-year-old boy attends a dental teaching hospital in Peshawar with extensive early childhood caries. He needs multiple extractions and restorations but is unable to cooperate and is rated Frankl 1 definitely negative. His medical history is unremarkable.

Which is the most appropriate indication for general anaesthesia in this child?

  1. A. Mild anxiety for a single restoration
  2. B. First dental visit for examination only
  3. C. Parent preference for general anaesthesia
  4. D. A small carious lesion on 51 in a cooperative child
  5. E. Extensive treatment needs with inability to cooperate
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Extensive treatment needs with inability to cooperate

General anaesthesia in children is reserved for situations where comprehensive dental care cannot be provided safely under local anaesthesia with behaviour guidance. Typical indications include extensive treatment needs, very young age, inability to cooperate, and medical or behavioural conditions that make dental care unsafe. This 3-year-old boy has extensive early childhood caries requiring multiple extractions and restorations and is rated Frankl 1 definitely negative, meaning he refuses treatment and is fearful. General anaesthesia allows all necessary treatment to be completed in one session while protecting the airway and preventing psychological trauma. Mild anxiety, a first examination visit, parent preference alone, or a small lesion are not indications for general anaesthesia. In Pakistan, general anaesthesia for children should be provided in a hospital with an anaesthetist and appropriate monitoring.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Mild anxiety for a single restoration: Mild anxiety for one restoration can be managed with tell-show-do and local anaesthesia, not general anaesthesia.
  • B. First dental visit for examination only: A first visit for examination does not require general anaesthesia; behaviour guidance and preventive care are appropriate.
  • C. Parent preference for general anaesthesia: Parent preference alone is not an indication; the decision depends on clinical need, cooperation, and safety.
  • D. A small carious lesion on 51 in a cooperative child: A small lesion on one tooth can be restored with local anaesthesia and behaviour guidance in a cooperative or mildly anxious child.

Key point: Identify an appropriate indication for general anaesthesia in a child.

Question 6 · Paediatric dentistry · medium

A 5-year-old girl attends a dental teaching hospital in Peshawar with multiple dental injuries and burns. The mother discloses that the father inflicts the injuries and asks the dentist not to report it. The child is quiet and avoids eye contact.

What is the dentist's most appropriate immediate action?

  1. A. Respect the request of the mother and provide treatment only without reporting
  2. B. Confront the father directly at the clinic
  3. C. Advise the mother to leave the home without reporting
  4. D. Report the suspected abuse to the child protection authorities
  5. E. Delay treatment until the father gives consent
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. Report the suspected abuse to the child protection authorities

Suspected child abuse must be reported to the appropriate child protection authorities even if a parent asks the dentist not to do so. The duty to protect the child overrides parental wishes, and confronting the alleged abuser may place the child at greater risk. Dental treatment should not be delayed while waiting for consent from the person who may be responsible. Reporting allows the authorities to investigate and arrange safety for the child. In Pakistan, dentists should follow local child protection protocols and document findings clearly and objectively.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Respect the request of the mother and provide treatment only without reporting: Respecting the request would leave the child unprotected and fails the duty to report suspected abuse.
  • B. Confront the father directly at the clinic: Confronting the father may endanger the child and is not the role of the dental practitioner.
  • C. Advise the mother to leave the home without reporting: Advising the mother to leave without reporting does not ensure the safety of the child or involve the authorities.
  • E. Delay treatment until the father gives consent: Delaying treatment harms the child and parental consent is not required to report suspected abuse.

Key point: State the duty to report suspected child abuse.

Question 7 · Paediatric dentistry · medium

A 9-year-old boy with unrepaired cyanotic congenital heart disease and exertional dyspnoea since birth attends a dental teaching hospital in Karachi for extraction of 85, the lower right second primary molar. He has no drug allergies.

Which prophylaxis does current AHA guidance advise before the extraction?

  1. A. No antibiotic prophylaxis
  2. B. Clindamycin orally one hour before
  3. C. Amoxicillin orally one hour before
  4. D. Azithromycin orally one hour before
  5. E. Metronidazole orally one hour before
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Amoxicillin orally one hour before

Unrepaired cyanotic congenital heart disease is a high-risk condition for infective endocarditis, so antibiotic prophylaxis is indicated before dental extraction. Amoxicillin taken one hour before the procedure is the first-line regimen for a patient with no penicillin allergy. Clindamycin is reserved for penicillin-allergic patients, while azithromycin and metronidazole are not recommended for endocarditis prophylaxis. The extraction of a primary tooth in a medically compromised child still requires the same prophylactic principles as in adults. After prophylaxis, the dentist should use atraumatic technique and provide clear postoperative instructions.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. No antibiotic prophylaxis: Unrepaired cyanotic congenital heart disease is a high-risk condition, so prophylaxis is indicated before extraction.
  • B. Clindamycin orally one hour before: Clindamycin is reserved for patients with a penicillin allergy, which this boy does not have.
  • D. Azithromycin orally one hour before: Azithromycin is not the recommended first-line agent for infective endocarditis prophylaxis in dental extraction.
  • E. Metronidazole orally one hour before: Metronidazole does not cover the viridans streptococci responsible for endocarditis and is not used for prophylaxis.

Key point: Choose first-line antibiotic prophylaxis for a child with high-risk cardiac disease.

Question 8 · Paediatric dentistry · medium

A 4-year-old boy is treated at a private dental clinic in Hyderabad. His mother reports night bottle feeding and frequent sweetened milk. He has pain on eating sweets but no spontaneous pain or swelling. Examination shows large cavitated lesions on the occlusal and mesial surfaces of the second primary molars 55, 65, 75 and 85. Radiographs show no periapical pathology or pulp exposure. He has a high caries risk because of frequent sweetened milk and poor brushing.

What is the most appropriate definitive restoration for these molars?

  1. A. Amalgam restoration on all four molars
  2. B. Stainless steel crowns on all four molars
  3. C. Composite restoration on all four molars
  4. D. Glass ionomer restoration on all four molars
  5. E. Extraction of all four molars with a space maintainer
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Stainless steel crowns on all four molars

Early childhood caries frequently affects primary molars with large occlusal and proximal lesions. When caries involves multiple surfaces in a young child at high risk, stainless steel crowns are preferred because they withstand occlusal forces, prevent new caries and last until exfoliation. Amalgam, composite or glass ionomer alone have higher failure rates in such cavities, especially with poor cooperation and diet. Extraction is avoided unless the tooth is non-restorable or has periapical pathology, to preserve arch length. In Pakistani practice, cost and single-visit treatment also favour crowns for extensive lesions.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Amalgam restoration on all four molars: Amalgam is technique-sensitive in young children and has poorer longevity than full coverage for multi-surface primary molar lesions.
  • C. Composite restoration on all four molars: Composite requires strict isolation and has high failure rates in large multi-surface cavities in uncooperative children.
  • D. Glass ionomer restoration on all four molars: Glass ionomer alone lacks sufficient wear resistance for large occlusoproximal cavities in a primary molar.
  • E. Extraction of all four molars with a space maintainer: Extraction is unnecessary for restorable asymptomatic primary molars and risks space loss and arch length problems.

Key point: Select the best restoration for multi-surface carious primary molars in a high-caries-risk child.

Question 9 · Paediatric dentistry · medium

A 7-year-old girl attends a private dental clinic in Islamabad. Her permanent first molars erupted six months ago. She has a history of caries in her primary teeth and eats sugary snacks daily. Examination shows 16 and 26 with deep, retentive fissures and no caries; radiographs show no proximal lesions. Her oral hygiene is fair and she uses a non-fluoridated toothpaste.

What is the most appropriate preventive treatment?

  1. A. Fluoride varnish only every six months
  2. B. Fissure sealants on 16 and 26 only
  3. C. Fissure sealants on 16 and 26 and fluoride varnish every six months
  4. D. No preventive treatment until caries develops
  5. E. Glass ionomer restorations on the occlusal fissures of 16 and 26 as a preventive measure
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Fissure sealants on 16 and 26 and fluoride varnish every six months

Newly erupted permanent first molars have deep, incompletely mineralised fissures that are highly susceptible to occlusal caries. In a child with a history of caries and frequent sugar intake, pit-and-fissure sealants on sound molars are strongly recommended, and fluoride varnish adds protection for smooth surfaces. Sealants are best placed soon after eruption before plaque stagnation causes lesions. Glass ionomer or composite preventive restorations are reserved for early carious lesions, not sound fissures. In Pakistan, where sugary tea and snacks are common, combining sealants with fluoride and diet counselling gives the best outcome.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Fluoride varnish only every six months: Fluoride varnish alone does not protect deep retentive fissures from occlusal caries in a high-caries-risk child.
  • B. Fissure sealants on 16 and 26 only: Sealants alone are good, but this child also needs fluoride reinforcement given her active caries history.
  • D. No preventive treatment until caries develops: Waiting for cavitation allows irreversible damage to newly erupted permanent molars in a high-risk patient.
  • E. Glass ionomer restorations on the occlusal fissures of 16 and 26 as a preventive measure: Preventive resin restorations are for incipient caries, not sound deep fissures, and are more invasive than sealants.

Key point: Choose combined sealant and fluoride prevention for high-caries-risk permanent molars.

Question 10 · Paediatric dentistry · medium

A 4-year-old boy attends a private dental clinic in Karachi with early childhood caries. The four maxillary incisors (51, 52, 61, 62) have extensive cavitated lesions involving the mesial, distal and labial surfaces. Pulpotomies were completed last week and the teeth are asymptomatic. Enough sound crown remains for retention, and his parents are concerned about appearance. He cooperates well enough for isolation.

Which restoration is most appropriate for these incisors?

  1. A. Composite strip crowns
  2. B. Class III amalgam restorations
  3. C. Stainless steel crowns on all four incisors
  4. D. Glass ionomer restorations alone
  5. E. Extraction of all four incisors
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Composite strip crowns

Primary maxillary incisors destroyed by early childhood caries that have been treated with pulp therapy and retain enough sound crown are restored best with composite strip crowns, which give a tooth-coloured full-crown restoration. Success needs good isolation and a cooperative child, because bonding fails in a wet field. Stainless steel crowns on incisors are durable but unaesthetic, amalgam is not suited to anterior teeth, and glass ionomer alone is too weak for large lesions. Extraction is kept for teeth that cannot be restored. Dietary counselling and fluoride varnish are given at the same time.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Class III amalgam restorations: Amalgam is grey, is not used in anterior teeth and gives no retention in lesions this large.
  • C. Stainless steel crowns on all four incisors: Metal crowns are strong but unaesthetic, and the parents are concerned about appearance.
  • D. Glass ionomer restorations alone: Glass ionomer alone lacks the strength and wear resistance for large, multi-surface incisor lesions.
  • E. Extraction of all four incisors: Extraction is not needed when the teeth are restorable after pulp therapy, and it affects appearance and speech.

Key point: Choose a stainless steel crown for a multi-surface carious primary molar.
