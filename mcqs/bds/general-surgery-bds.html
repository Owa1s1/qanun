Question 1 · Surgery of the face, mouth and jaws · medium

A 28-year-old woman from Hyderabad has a 10 mm radiopaque stone in the hilum of the left submandibular gland, confirmed on an occlusal radiograph. She has had three episodes of acute sialadenitis in the past year. Intraoral removal through the submandibular duct is not possible because the stone is too posterior. She has no allergy to penicillin.

Which is the most appropriate definitive treatment?

  1. A. Sialendoscopy alone
  2. B. Marsupialisation of the submandibular gland
  3. C. External lithotripsy
  4. D. Long-term antibiotic therapy
  5. E. Submandibular gland excision
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Submandibular gland excision

When a submandibular calculus is located at the hilum or within the gland and cannot be retrieved through the duct, submandibular gland excision is the definitive treatment, particularly with recurrent sialadenitis. The procedure requires identification of the lingual nerve, hypoglossal nerve, and facial vein, and ligation of the submandibular duct and vessels. Temporary numbness of the anterior tongue may occur from lingual nerve retraction. Sialendoscopy is preferred for anterior ductal stones.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Sialendoscopy alone: Sialendoscopy works well for anterior ductal stones but is less successful for large hilar stones in a gland with recurrent infections.
  • B. Marsupialisation of the submandibular gland: Marsupialisation is used for ranulae in the floor of the mouth, not for stones within the submandibular gland itself.
  • C. External lithotripsy: Extracorporeal shock wave lithotripsy is more useful for parotid stones and is less available in Pakistan.
  • D. Long-term antibiotic therapy: Antibiotics treat acute infection but do not remove the stone or prevent recurrence.

Key point: Select submandibular gland excision for a hilar stone with recurrent sialadenitis.

Question 2 · Principles of surgery · easy

A 40-year-old man is seen at a dental teaching hospital in Karachi with severe vomiting for two days. He has not kept fluids down. His pulse is 110/min, blood pressure 100/70 mmHg, and mucous membranes are dry. Serum sodium is 138 mEq/L (135-145) and potassium 3.9 mEq/L (3.5-5.0).

What type of dehydration is this most likely?

  1. A. Hypotonic dehydration
  2. B. Hypertonic dehydration
  3. C. Isotonic dehydration
  4. D. Mixed dehydration
  5. E. Osmotic dehydration
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Isotonic dehydration

Isotonic dehydration means water and sodium are lost in proportion, so serum sodium remains normal. It commonly follows vomiting, diarrhoea or gastrointestinal suction. The patient is clinically hypovolaemic with tachycardia, dry mucous membranes and normal blood pressure early on. Initial resuscitation uses isotonic crystalloid such as normal saline or balanced salt solution. Electrolytes should be rechecked after fluids because hidden deficits may appear.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Hypotonic dehydration: This would show serum sodium below 135 mEq/L, often from diuretics or adrenal insufficiency.
  • B. Hypertonic dehydration: This would show serum sodium above 145 mEq/L, usually from water loss or diabetes insipidus.
  • D. Mixed dehydration: This is not a standard primary classification based on a single normal sodium value.
  • E. Osmotic dehydration: This term is not used for acute vomiting with normal electrolytes and normal osmolarity.

Key point: To classify dehydration as isotonic when serum sodium is normal after acute fluid loss.

Question 3 · The neck: lumps, thyroid and airway · easy

A 26-year-old man visits a dental teaching hospital in Peshawar with a painless swelling below the body of the mandible, between the anterior and posterior bellies of the digastric muscle. The swelling is soft and mobile. It lies within the lower border of the mandible superiorly and the two bellies of the digastric muscle inferiorly.

Which cervical triangle contains this swelling?

  1. A. Submandibular triangle
  2. B. Submental triangle
  3. C. Carotid triangle
  4. D. Muscular triangle
  5. E. Posterior triangle
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Submandibular triangle

The submandibular triangle is one of the four subdivisions of the anterior triangle of the neck. Its boundaries are the lower border of the mandible above, the anterior belly of the digastric in front, and the posterior belly of the digastric behind. A swelling in this region in a dental patient often relates to the submandibular gland or lymph nodes. Accurate localisation helps the dental student decide whether the lump is likely salivary, nodal or thyroid in origin before planning referral or further tests.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Submental triangle: The submental triangle is midline, bounded by the anterior bellies of both digastric muscles and the hyoid bone, not lateral to the mandible.
  • C. Carotid triangle: The carotid triangle lies below the posterior belly of the digastric, in front of the sternocleidomastoid, and above the superior belly of the omohyoid.
  • D. Muscular triangle: The muscular triangle is bounded by the sternocleidomastoid, the superior belly of the omohyoid and the midline of the neck.
  • E. Posterior triangle: The posterior triangle is bounded by the sternocleidomastoid, trapezius and clavicle, far posterior to this mandibular swelling.

Key point: Identify the submandibular triangle from its anatomical boundaries.

Question 4 · Trauma and maxillofacial injuries · medium

A 6-year-old boy is brought to a general practice in Abbottabad twenty minutes after a stray dog bit his right cheek. The wound is a 2-centimetre laceration with clean edges and no deep tissue exposure. He has not received any rabies vaccine previously. His tetanus immunisation is complete.

What is the most appropriate next step in management?

  1. A. Delayed closure after five days, with full prophylaxis
  2. B. Delayed suturing after five days and rabies immunoglobulin
  3. C. Wound washing, RIG and vaccine, then primary closure
  4. D. Immediate primary closure with antibiotics and no rabies prophylaxis
  5. E. Wound cleansing only; no further intervention needed
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Wound washing, RIG and vaccine, then primary closure

A dog bite on the face is a WHO category III exposure. Wash the wound thoroughly with soap and running water for about 15 minutes and apply an antiseptic such as povidone-iodine, then give rabies immunoglobulin infiltrated into and around the wound and start the rabies vaccine course. Because the face has a good blood supply and scarring matters, a clean facial laceration is closed primarily (loosely) after this cleansing and RIG infiltration; delayed closure is kept for heavily contaminated or late wounds elsewhere. Antibiotic prophylaxis (co-amoxiclav) is often given for facial bites, and tetanus status is checked.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Delayed closure after five days, with full prophylaxis: Delayed closure is for contaminated or late wounds; a clean facial bite is closed primarily after cleansing and RIG.
  • B. Delayed suturing after five days and rabies immunoglobulin: Wrong: rabies immunoglobulin alone is incomplete; vaccine and wound care are also required, and closure should be delayed.
  • D. Immediate primary closure with antibiotics and no rabies prophylaxis: Wrong: immediate closure without rabies prophylaxis leaves an unvaccinated child at risk of rabies from a stray dog.
  • E. Wound cleansing only; no further intervention needed: Wrong: cleansing alone is insufficient because the boy is unvaccinated and needs rabies post-exposure prophylaxis.

Key point: To manage an unvaccinated child with a stray dog bite to the face.

Question 5 · Surgery of the face, mouth and jaws · easy

A 3-day-old newborn is brought to a dental teaching hospital in Peshawar by his mother. The baby was born with a cleft of the soft and hard palate but no cleft lip. He cries vigorously and breathes normally. The mother reports milk comes out of the nose during feeds and the baby tires quickly after a few sucks. Birth weight is 2.8 kg. There is no fever or respiratory distress.

What is the most appropriate initial feeding advice?

  1. A. Breastfeed only in the side-lying position until repair
  2. B. Begin semisolid complementary feeds today
  3. C. Use spoon feeds only until surgical repair at three months
  4. D. Insert a nasogastric tube for all feeds
  5. E. Feed upright with a soft squeezable bottle
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Feed upright with a soft squeezable bottle

Cleft palate creates an opening between the oral and nasal cavities, so the infant cannot generate negative pressure and milk regurgitates into the nose. The first step is to feed the baby upright with a soft squeezable or cleft-specific bottle, burp frequently, and monitor weight gain. Surgery is not an emergency; lip repair is usually at three to six months and palate repair at nine to eighteen months. Feeding support should begin immediately and should not wait for operative repair. In Pakistan, access to cleft bottles may be limited, so a plain soft bottle held by an adult is an acceptable low-cost alternative.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Breastfeed only in the side-lying position until repair: Side-lying feeding may worsen nasal regurgitation in a newborn with an open palate; upright positioning is preferred first.
  • B. Begin semisolid complementary feeds today: A 3-day-old infant should receive only milk; semisolid foods are introduced around six months and risk aspiration with a cleft palate.
  • C. Use spoon feeds only until surgical repair at three months: Spoon-only feeding delays nutrition and is unnecessary; a stable newborn can take adequate volumes with a cleft bottle before repair.
  • D. Insert a nasogastric tube for all feeds: Nasogastric feeding is reserved for infants with respiratory distress or failure to thrive; this baby breathes normally and is stable.

Key point: The student will select the correct initial feeding method for a newborn with isolated cleft palate.

Question 6 · Principles of surgery · medium

A 50-year-old man is reviewed on the evening of the first day after an upper abdominal operation at a hospital in D.I. Khan. He has a temperature of 38.2°C, mild breathlessness and reduced air entry at the right base. He is coughing but producing little sputum.

What is the most likely cause of the fever?

  1. A. Wound infection
  2. B. Atelectasis
  3. C. Urinary tract infection
  4. D. Pneumonia
  5. E. Deep vein thrombosis
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Atelectasis

Atelectasis is the commonest cause of fever in the first 24 to 48 hours after upper abdominal or thoracic surgery. Pain and shallow breathing prevent normal inspiration, causing basal alveolar collapse, reduced air entry and a low-grade fever. Treatment is analgesia, physiotherapy, incentive spirometry and early mobilisation. Wound infection, urinary infection and pneumonia occur later and have different localising signs. Chest X-ray may show basal opacity but is not always needed.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Wound infection: This typically appears on the fourth or fifth postoperative day with redness and purulent discharge.
  • C. Urinary tract infection: This causes dysuria, frequency and suprapubic pain, not basal chest signs after surgery.
  • D. Pneumonia: This causes high fever, productive cough and consolidation, usually later than the first postoperative day.
  • E. Deep vein thrombosis: This causes calf pain and swelling, not fever with reduced air entry at the lung base.

Key point: To attribute early postoperative fever to atelectasis from basal collapse after upper abdominal surgery.

Question 7 · The neck: lumps, thyroid and airway · easy

A 12-year-old boy is seen at a hospital in Peshawar with a painless, matted swelling in the neck that has been present for four months. He has occasional night sweats and has lost weight. A Mantoux test is positive.

What is the most likely diagnosis?

  1. A. Reactive lymphadenitis
  2. B. Lymphoma
  3. C. Metastatic lymph node
  4. D. Tuberculous lymphadenitis
  5. E. Cat scratch disease
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. Tuberculous lymphadenitis

Tuberculous cervical lymphadenitis, or scrofula, commonly affects children and presents as a painless, matted, sometimes fluctuant neck mass with constitutional symptoms such as night sweats and weight loss. A positive Mantoux test supports the diagnosis. In Pakistan, TB is common and should be considered before lymphoma or malignancy in a child with these features.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Reactive lymphadenitis: Reactive nodes are tender, acute and follow a recent throat or dental infection, not a four-month painless matted lump.
  • B. Lymphoma: Lymphoma nodes are rubbery and discrete rather than matted, and usually require biopsy for confirmation.
  • C. Metastatic lymph node: Metastasis is uncommon in a 12-year-old without a primary head and neck tumour or tobacco history.
  • E. Cat scratch disease: Cat scratch disease causes tender nodes after a cat scratch or bite, which is not described here.

Key point: Diagnose tuberculous cervical lymphadenitis from a painless matted neck mass with systemic symptoms.

Question 8 · Trauma and maxillofacial injuries · easy

A 24-year-old male is brought to the emergency department in Peshawar after a road traffic accident. He is unconscious with snoring respirations. His airway is partially obstructed. A cervical collar is in place. His pulse is 110 per minute. There is no obvious facial trauma.

What is the most appropriate initial airway manoeuvre?

  1. A. Head tilt-chin lift
  2. B. Jaw thrust
  3. C. Oropharyngeal airway insertion
  4. D. Nasopharyngeal airway insertion
  5. E. Cricothyroidotomy
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Jaw thrust

In ATLS, the airway is assessed while protecting the cervical spine. If cervical spine injury is possible, head tilt-chin lift is avoided; the jaw thrust displaces the mandible forward without neck movement. The jaw thrust is performed by placing fingers behind the angles of the mandible and pushing forward while the cervical collar remains in place. This avoids neck movement. Basic airway maneuvers precede advanced airways. If the jaw thrust fails, an oropharyngeal or nasopharyngeal airway may be added before considering intubation. Cricothyroidotomy is indicated only when intubation and ventilation fail. Pakistani trauma centres often lack immediate airway equipment, so basic maneuvers are life-saving.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Head tilt-chin lift: Head tilt-chin lift extends the neck and risks worsening an unstable cervical spine injury, which must be assumed in this trauma patient.
  • C. Oropharyngeal airway insertion: Oropharyngeal airway can be used after the airway is opened but is not the first manoeuvre and may cause gagging if the patient is not deeply unconscious.
  • D. Nasopharyngeal airway insertion: Nasopharyngeal airway is useful when trismus or gag reflex is present, but the jaw thrust should be attempted first in cervical spine injury.
  • E. Cricothyroidotomy: Cricothyroidotomy is a surgical airway reserved for failure of basic airway maneuvers and intubation, not the initial step.

Key point: To identify the correct initial airway maneuver in a trauma patient with suspected cervical spine injury.

Question 9 · Surgery of the face, mouth and jaws · easy

A 3-week-old infant is brought to a dental clinic in Chitral because the mother cannot breastfeed effectively. Examination shows the tongue tip is tied to the floor of the mouth by a short, tight frenulum, and the tongue cannot protrude beyond the lower gum. The baby gains weight poorly.

What is the most appropriate management?

  1. A. Wait until age five years before any intervention
  2. B. Speech therapy alone until school age
  3. C. Tongue stretching exercises three times daily
  4. D. Antibiotic therapy for suspected tongue infection
  5. E. Frenotomy now with sterile scissors
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Frenotomy now with sterile scissors

Ankyloglossia, or tongue-tie, restricts tongue protrusion and movement because of a short, tight frenulum. When it interferes with breastfeeding and weight gain, frenotomy with sterile scissors in the neonatal period is safe and effective. Waiting until school age prolongs feeding and speech problems. Speech therapy and stretching exercises do not release the frenulum, and antibiotics have no role unless infection is present. Frenotomy is a simple procedure that can be done in a dental or paediatric clinic.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Wait until age five years before any intervention: Waiting until five years prolongs feeding and speech problems; early release is indicated when breastfeeding is affected.
  • B. Speech therapy alone until school age: Speech therapy does not release a tight frenulum and is reserved for persistent articulation issues after surgical release.
  • C. Tongue stretching exercises three times daily: Stretching exercises may help after release but cannot replace frenotomy when the tongue is functionally tethered.
  • D. Antibiotic therapy for suspected tongue infection: There is no sign of infection; antibiotics do not release a congenital frenulum or improve feeding.

Key point: The student will indicate frenotomy for tongue-tie causing poor breastfeeding and weight gain.

Question 10 · Principles of surgery · easy

A 58-year-old man attends a dental teaching hospital in Peshawar with a painful lower left first molar (36) for two days. He has hypertension controlled with amlodipine and type 2 diabetes controlled with metformin. He smokes ten cigarettes daily. He walks up two flights of stairs without stopping and has no chest pain. Pulse 78/min, blood pressure 134/84 mmHg.

What is his ASA physical status grade?

  1. A. ASA I
  2. B. ASA II
  3. C. ASA III
  4. D. ASA IV
  5. E. ASA V
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. ASA II

The ASA physical status classification grades preoperative risk. ASA I is a healthy patient. ASA II is a patient with mild systemic disease, such as controlled hypertension or controlled diabetes, without functional limitation. This patient climbs two flights of stairs and has no angina, so he is not ASA III. He is not moribund or in constant danger of death, excluding ASA IV and V. Elective dental care can proceed with routine monitoring and stress reduction.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. ASA I: Wrong because he has controlled hypertension and diabetes, so he is not a completely healthy patient.
  • C. ASA III: Wrong because his diseases do not limit his activity; he climbs stairs and has no angina or organ failure.
  • D. ASA IV: Wrong because he is not in constant danger of life; he is ambulant and stable for elective dental care.
  • E. ASA V: Wrong because he is not moribund and is expected to survive well beyond 24 hours without surgery.

Key point: Assign the correct ASA physical status grade for a patient with controlled mild systemic disease.
