Question 1 · Crowns and indirect restorations · easy

A 24-year-old woman visits a private dental clinic in Karachi asking for a smile makeover. She dislikes the shape of her upper lateral incisors and a gap between the upper central incisors. Her smile line displays the full crowns and gingiva of the anterior teeth. The dentist photographs her in natural head position for analysis.

Which smile design element does a line through the centre of the upper central incisors assess?

  1. A. Incisal plane
  2. B. Gingival contour
  3. C. Buccal corridor
  4. D. Dental midline
  5. E. Tooth proportion
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. Dental midline

Smile design analyses several components, including the facial midline, dental midline, incisal plane, gingival contour, tooth proportion, and buccal corridor. The dental midline is determined by a line through the centre of the upper central incisors and should coincide with the facial midline for an aesthetic result. Photographs in natural head position help avoid errors caused by head tilt.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Incisal plane: The incisal plane is assessed by a horizontal line through the incisal edges of the anterior teeth.
  • B. Gingival contour: Gingival contour is evaluated by the architecture and symmetry of the gingival margins.
  • C. Buccal corridor: The buccal corridor is the dark space between the posterior teeth and the corner of the mouth.
  • E. Tooth proportion: Tooth proportion compares the width and length of individual anterior teeth.

Key point: Identify the dental midline as the smile design element assessed through the central incisors.

Question 2 · Endodontics and dental trauma · easy

A 22-year-old male visits a private dental clinic in Quetta with spontaneous pain in his upper right first premolar that wakes him at night. Cold testing produces lingering pain. The tooth, 14, upper right first premolar, has a large occlusal restoration and no periapical radiolucency. The dentist plans root canal treatment.

Which access cavity outline is most appropriate for this tooth?

  1. A. Circular, 3 mm in diameter
  2. B. Oval, wider mesiodistally
  3. C. Triangular, apex toward the buccal
  4. D. Rectangular, centred on the occlusal table
  5. E. Oval, wider buccolingually
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Oval, wider buccolingually

Maxillary first premolars frequently have two canals and two orifices, buccal and palatal, separated by a developmental groove. The access cavity should therefore be oval and wider buccolingually to unroof the chamber fully and allow straight-line access to both canals. A narrow mesiodistal preparation risks leaving the palatal orifice undiscovered and causes instrument binding. Care is needed because the chamber is narrow mesiodistally and the buccal and palatal orifices lie close to the external root surfaces.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Circular, 3 mm in diameter: This is too small to negotiate two canals and achieve straight-line access in this premolar.
  • B. Oval, wider mesiodistally: This would not give straight-line access to the separate buccal and palatal orifices of 14.
  • C. Triangular, apex toward the buccal: This risks buccal perforation and does not follow the palatal canal orifice of this premolar.
  • D. Rectangular, centred on the occlusal table: This is too angular and may miss the lingual orifice of 14.

Key point: The student will select the buccolingual oval access outline for a maxillary first premolar.

Question 3 · Caries management and direct restorations · easy

A 12-year-old female attends a school dental clinic in Peshawar. She has multiple cavitated lesions and reports frequent sugary snacks. Her caries risk is assessed as high.

What is the most appropriate recall interval for this patient?

  1. A. 3 months
  2. B. 6 months
  3. C. 12 months
  4. D. 18 months
  5. E. 24 months
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. 3 months

Recall intervals are individualised according to caries risk. High-risk patients, especially children with active cavitated lesions, are reviewed frequently—commonly every three months—so that new or progressing lesions are detected early and preventive measures are reinforced. Low-risk patients may be recalled at longer intervals, such as annually or longer, depending on local guidance.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. 6 months: Six months is more suitable for moderate caries risk, not a child with multiple cavitated lesions and high risk.
  • C. 12 months: Twelve months is generally for low-risk patients; this patient’s high risk needs earlier review.
  • D. 18 months: Eighteen months is too long for a high-caries-risk child and allows lesions to progress.
  • E. 24 months: Twenty-four months is reserved for very low-risk patients and is unsafe here.

Key point: Select a three-month recall interval for a child with high caries risk.

Question 4 · Crowns and indirect restorations · easy

A 35-year-old man attends a dental OPD in Abbottabad with a small occlusal carious lesion on the upper right first premolar. He wishes to keep as much natural tooth as possible. The dentist plans a conservative cavity that removes only infected dentine and preserves the sound enamel margins.

Which principle is shown by removing only carious tissue?

  1. A. Tooth preservation
  2. B. Retention form
  3. C. Resistance form
  4. D. Durability
  5. E. Marginal integrity
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Tooth preservation

Tooth preservation is a core principle of modern operative dentistry. It means removing only carious or unsound tissue and retaining as much healthy enamel and dentine as possible. This contrasts with older extension-for-prevention concepts. Retention form, resistance form, durability, and marginal integrity are also important, but they address restoration stability and longevity rather than the amount of tooth removed. Minimal intervention is especially relevant when patients request conservative care.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Retention form: Retention form prevents displacement of the restoration but does not describe conserving tooth tissue.
  • C. Resistance form: Resistance form prevents fracture of the restoration and tooth but is not about minimal removal.
  • D. Durability: Durability concerns the lifespan of the restoration, not the amount of tooth removed.
  • E. Marginal integrity: Marginal integrity relates to the seal at the restoration-tooth interface, not conservation.

Key point: Define tooth preservation as the principle of conserving sound tooth structure during cavity preparation.

Question 5 · Endodontics and dental trauma · easy

A 25-year-old man visits a private dental clinic in Larkana with a fractured upper right central incisor and pulp exposure. The tooth (11) has no periapical radiolucency. The dentist is determining the working length for root canal treatment.

The working length should be established at which anatomical point?

  1. A. The anatomical apex
  2. B. The radiographic apex
  3. C. The apical constriction
  4. D. The cementoenamel junction
  5. E. The tip of the root
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. The apical constriction

The working length ends at the apical constriction, also called the minor diameter, which is the narrowest part of the canal and usually lies 0.5 to 1 mm short of the radiographic apex. Cleaning and shaping to this point avoids extruding debris and irrigant into the periapical tissues while still debriding the full canal length. The cementoenamel junction or a cusp tip is used only as the coronal reference from which the measurement is taken.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. The anatomical apex: This is the end of the root and is often apical to the apical foramen; instrumenting here overextends treatment of 11.
  • B. The radiographic apex: Seen on the radiograph, it may not coincide with the apical foramen and can lead to over-instrumentation.
  • D. The cementoenamel junction: This is a coronal reference point for measuring length, not the apical termination point.
  • E. The tip of the root: This is synonymous with the anatomical apex and is too far apically for the working length of 11.

Key point: The student will identify the apical constriction as the working length endpoint.

Question 6 · Caries management and direct restorations · easy

A 12-year-old boy attends a dental teaching hospital in Peshawar with sensitivity in the lower left first molar (36) when eating sweet food for three weeks. He has no spontaneous pain. Examination shows a deep occlusal carious lesion in 36; the radiograph shows radiolucency into the inner third of dentin without pulp exposure. Cold sensitivity stops within seconds.

What is the most appropriate next step?

  1. A. Complete caries removal to hard dentin before any restoration
  2. B. Selective caries removal leaving soft dentin near the pulp
  3. C. No caries removal, apply silver diamine fluoride only
  4. D. Complete caries removal followed by direct pulp capping
  5. E. Selective caries removal followed by extraction
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Selective caries removal leaving soft dentin near the pulp

Selective caries removal targets infected, soft dentin while leaving affected but remineralizable dentin over a vital pulp, especially in deep lesions where complete excavation risks exposure. After removing infected dentin with a slow hand excavator or low-speed bur, a biocompatible liner such as glass ionomer is placed and the cavity is restored. This preserves pulp vitality and avoids unnecessary root canal treatment. In Pakistani practice, hand instruments are widely available and inexpensive, making selective removal feasible in most OPDs. The tooth in the stem is asymptomatic with a vital pulp, so aggressive removal or pulp capping is not required.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Complete caries removal to hard dentin before any restoration: Removing all caries to hard dentin risks pulp exposure in this deep lesion; the pulp is vital and asymptomatic.
  • C. No caries removal, apply silver diamine fluoride only: Silver diamine fluoride stains the cavity and is not definitive treatment for a deep cavitated permanent molar lesion.
  • D. Complete caries removal followed by direct pulp capping: There is no pulp exposure or irreversible pulpitis, so direct pulp capping is not indicated.
  • E. Selective caries removal followed by extraction: Extraction is not indicated for a restorable permanent first molar with a vital, asymptomatic pulp.

Key point: Choose selective caries removal to preserve pulp vitality in a deep carious lesion.

Question 7 · Crowns and indirect restorations · easy

A 40-year-old female attends a dental teaching hospital in Mardan for a porcelain-fused-to-metal crown on her upper right central incisor. She has a high smile line and is concerned about a grey line at the gum. Examination of 11 shows sound tooth structure and healthy periodontal tissues.

Which design feature best prevents metal display at the margin?

  1. A. Metal collar
  2. B. Knife-edge margin
  3. C. Bevelled margin
  4. D. Porcelain labial margin
  5. E. Supragingival metal margin
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. Porcelain labial margin

In the aesthetic zone with a high smile line, a porcelain labial margin covers the metal and prevents a grey line at the gingival margin. A metal collar or supragingival metal margin would be visible when the patient smiles. Knife-edge and bevelled margins do not provide a reliable aesthetic ceramic margin for PFM crowns. Shillingburg Fundamentals of Fixed Prosthodontics: chapter on metal-ceramic crowns.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Metal collar: A metal collar would show a grey line at the gingiva, which is unacceptable in a patient with a high smile line.
  • B. Knife-edge margin: A knife-edge margin gives poor control of the ceramic-to-metal junction and risks visible metal at the margin.
  • C. Bevelled margin: A bevelled margin is used for cast gold margins and does not provide a reliable aesthetic ceramic margin for a PFM crown.
  • E. Supragingival metal margin: A supragingival metal margin would be visible when the patient smiles and is not aesthetic for an anterior crown.

Key point: Use a porcelain labial margin to hide metal in an aesthetic PFM crown.

Question 8 · Endodontics and dental trauma · easy

A 26-year-old woman attends a dental teaching hospital in Peshawar with a necrotic lower left first molar (36) and a periapical radiolucency. The dentist is preparing the irrigation protocol for cleaning and shaping and needs a main irrigant to dissolve necrotic tissue and disinfect the canal.

Which irrigant is the main choice, and why?

  1. A. 17 percent EDTA, because it dissolves necrotic tissue
  2. B. 2 percent chlorhexidine, because it dissolves necrotic tissue
  3. C. Sodium hypochlorite, which dissolves tissue and kills bacteria
  4. D. Normal saline, because it kills biofilm bacteria
  5. E. Hydrogen peroxide, because its bubbling sterilises the canal
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Sodium hypochlorite, which dissolves tissue and kills bacteria

Sodium hypochlorite is the main endodontic irrigant because it dissolves organic tissue and kills bacteria in biofilms, which instruments alone cannot reach in the complex canal system. EDTA is used as a chelating rinse to remove the inorganic smear layer, and chlorhexidine is used as an adjunct or final rinse for its residual action. Saline only flushes debris. Concentrations from about 0.5 to 6 percent are used, and higher concentrations dissolve tissue faster but are more irritant if they are extruded beyond the apex, so safe delivery matters more than strength.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. 17 percent EDTA, because it dissolves necrotic tissue: EDTA chelates calcium and removes the inorganic smear layer; it does not dissolve organic tissue.
  • B. 2 percent chlorhexidine, because it dissolves necrotic tissue: Chlorhexidine is antimicrobial with residual action but cannot dissolve necrotic tissue.
  • D. Normal saline, because it kills biofilm bacteria: Saline only flushes out debris; it has no meaningful antimicrobial or tissue-dissolving action.
  • E. Hydrogen peroxide, because its bubbling sterilises the canal: Bubbling moves debris but does not sterilise; peroxide is not the main irrigant and can trap gas apically.

Key point: Choose the standard sodium hypochlorite concentration for root canal irrigation in an acute apical abscess.

Question 9 · Caries management and direct restorations · easy

A 40-year-old man attends a dental teaching hospital in Peshawar with a carious lesion on the proximal surface of the lower right first molar (46). The dentist plans a Class II cavity preparation. The remaining tooth structure after caries removal is thin over the cusp.

Which feature of the cavity preparation resists fracture of the tooth under occlusal load?

  1. A. Outline form of the cavity
  2. B. Retention form
  3. C. Convenience form
  4. D. Caries removal
  5. E. Resistance form
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Resistance form

Resistance form is the feature of a cavity preparation that enables the tooth and restoration to withstand occlusal forces without fracture. It is achieved by flat pulpal and gingival floors, slightly rounded internal line angles, and sufficient remaining dentin thickness over cusps. Outline form removes caries and sets margins, while retention form keeps the restoration from being displaced. In a Class II preparation in a lower first molar, preserving dentin over the cusp is essential to avoid cuspal fracture. This principle applies in all Pakistani operative clinics whether amalgam or composite is used.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Outline form of the cavity: Outline form defines the cavity boundaries and removes caries; it does not primarily resist occlusal fracture.
  • B. Retention form: Retention form prevents the restoration from being pulled out; it does not resist tooth fracture.
  • C. Convenience form: Convenience form allows operator access and visibility; it is not related to resisting occlusal load.
  • D. Caries removal: Caries removal eliminates infection but does not by itself prevent fracture of the remaining tooth.

Key point: Identify resistance form as the feature that prevents occlusal fracture.

Question 10 · Crowns and indirect restorations · easy

A 50-year-old male attends a dental college in Mirpur for a crown on his lower right first molar. He has a high caries risk and no aesthetic concern. Examination of 46 shows a large MOD restoration with sound cusps and periodontal health.

Which crown is most durable for this posterior tooth?

  1. A. Full cast gold crown
  2. B. Porcelain-fused-to-metal crown with a metal collar
  3. C. All-ceramic crown
  4. D. Partial veneer crown
  5. E. Resin crown
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Full cast gold crown

A full cast gold crown is the most durable and conservative option for a posterior tooth in a patient with high caries risk and no aesthetic demand. It withstands occlusal forces, adapts well to margins, and is kind to opposing teeth. Porcelain-fused-to-metal and all-ceramic crowns require more reduction. Partial coverage leaves cusps vulnerable, and resin is not durable enough for molars. Shillingburg Fundamentals of Fixed Prosthodontics: chapter on full cast crowns.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Porcelain-fused-to-metal crown with a metal collar: A metal collar PFM requires more tooth removal and is not needed when aesthetics are not a priority.
  • C. All-ceramic crown: All-ceramic is less resistant to heavy occlusal load and more expensive than gold for this molar.
  • D. Partial veneer crown: A partial veneer crown does not cover all cusps and is less protective for a heavily restored molar.
  • E. Resin crown: A resin crown is not durable enough for a posterior load-bearing tooth and would fracture under occlusal load.

Key point: Choose a full cast gold crown for a high-caries-risk posterior tooth without aesthetic demand.
