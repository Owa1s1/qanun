Question 1 · Behavioural sciences and medical ethics · medium

During a heatwave, a district hospital in Sindh has one free ventilator. Two patients need it: a 45-year-old labourer with severe heatstroke who is expected to recover with ventilation, and a 70-year-old landowner with terminal cancer whose family has political connections and offers to pay for the bed. The doctors decide using clinical need and the likelihood of benefit only.

Which ethical principle guides this decision?

  1. A. Respect for autonomy
  2. B. Confidentiality
  3. C. Veracity
  4. D. Fidelity
  5. E. Justice
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Justice

Justice in health care concerns the fair distribution of limited resources and treating like cases alike. When resources such as ventilators, ICU beds or organs are scarce, allocation should be based on clinically relevant criteria: medical need, urgency and the likelihood and size of benefit, not wealth, social status, connections or ability to pay. Transparent, consistent policies (triage protocols) protect fairness and support staff making hard decisions. Patients who do not receive the resource must still receive the best available care, including good palliative care where appropriate. Decisions and their reasons should be documented and discussed honestly with families.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Respect for autonomy: Autonomy would let each man refuse ventilation, but neither is refusing; the question is who receives a machine that only one can have.
  • B. Confidentiality: Confidentiality governs who may be told about their illnesses, and plays no part in choosing between two patients for one ventilator.
  • C. Veracity: Veracity requires both families to be told the truth about the shortage, but it does not decide which patient gets the machine.
  • D. Fidelity: Fidelity is about keeping promises made to a patient; no promise of a ventilator has been made to either man.

Key point: Scarce resources should be allocated fairly by need and likely benefit, not by wealth or influence (justice).

Question 2 · Behavioural sciences and medical ethics · medium

A 48-year-old man admitted with chest pain is having an evolving myocardial infarction on serial ECGs and troponins. He wants to go home today because his shop will be robbed if left unattended. He is alert, understands that he could die, can explain the risks back to the doctor and weighs them against his livelihood. He refuses to stay, even for one more night.

What is the most appropriate action?

  1. A. Sedate him and keep him in hospital
  2. B. Explain, respect, document
  3. C. Call security to stop him leaving
  4. D. Discharge him with no treatment at all
  5. E. Ask his wife to sign the refusal
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Explain, respect, document

A competent adult may refuse admission or treatment and leave hospital, even when this is against medical advice and may be life-threatening. The doctor should check that he has capacity for this decision, explain clearly the risks of leaving and the benefits of staying, explore practical solutions to his concern (for example asking a relative to watch the shop), and offer the best alternative care he will accept: medicines to take home, a plan for early review, and clear advice to return immediately or call an ambulance if symptoms recur. The discussion should be documented carefully, often with a signed discharge-against-medical-advice form, though the patient cannot be forced to sign.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Sedate him and keep him in hospital: Sedating a man who understands the risk of death and has chosen to leave is assault and unlawful detention, whatever the cardiac risk.
  • C. Call security to stop him leaving: Security may not hold a capable adult, and force would end any chance of him accepting the treatment that could still help him.
  • D. Discharge him with no treatment at all: Sending him away with nothing abandons him; he may accept tablets, a follow-up appointment and clear advice on when to return.
  • E. Ask his wife to sign the refusal: His wife cannot consent or refuse for a capable adult, and asking her to sign gives the record a false authority.

Key point: A capable adult may leave against medical advice; explain risks, offer alternatives, respect the decision and document it.

Question 3 · Behavioural sciences and medical ethics · medium

A cardiologist explains to a 58-year-old man with severe narrowing of three coronary arteries that he needs coronary artery bypass surgery. She describes the operation, the expected benefits and the risks of stroke, bleeding and death, and he signs the consent form. She does not mention that coronary stenting or medicines alone are other possible options in his case.

Which element of informed consent is missing?

  1. A. Information on the nature of the procedure
  2. B. Information about the risks
  3. C. A signature on the form
  4. D. Alternatives, including no treatment
  5. E. Information about the benefits
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. Alternatives, including no treatment

Valid informed consent requires that the patient has capacity, is given adequate information, understands it and decides voluntarily without coercion. The information must include the diagnosis, the nature and purpose of the proposed treatment, its benefits, its material risks and side effects, the reasonable alternatives (including the option of no treatment) with their own risks and benefits, and the likely outcome without treatment. A signature on a form is only evidence that a discussion took place; it does not make consent valid if information was missing. Patients should have time to ask questions and should know they can change their mind.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Information on the nature of the procedure: The nature of the bypass operation was described to him, so this element of the discussion was complete.
  • B. Information about the risks: Stroke, bleeding and death were all set out, so the risks were covered; what is missing comes before the risks.
  • C. A signature on the form: He signed the form, but a signature records a decision rather than making it valid; the gap is in what he was told.
  • E. Information about the benefits: The expected benefits were explained, so this part of the discussion was not what was left out.

Key point: Informed consent must cover the procedure, benefits, material risks and reasonable alternatives, including no treatment.

Question 4 · Behavioural sciences and medical ethics · medium

On a busy surgical ward, a consultant asks a house officer in her first week to "get consent" from a patient for a laparoscopic cholecystectomy listed for the next morning. She has never seen the operation performed and does not know its specific risks or how often they occur. The patient has many questions about bile duct injury and recovery time.

Who should take consent for this operation?

  1. A. The ward nurse
  2. B. The most junior doctor on the team
  3. C. The operating surgeon
  4. D. The anaesthetist
  5. E. The admitting medical officer
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. The operating surgeon

Consent should be obtained by the doctor carrying out the procedure, or by someone who is suitably trained and has enough knowledge of the procedure and its risks to explain them and answer the patient's questions. A junior doctor who does not understand the operation should not take consent; she should say so and ask a senior colleague. Consent is a process, not a signature: it should ideally begin well before the day of surgery, giving the patient time to think, and be confirmed before the procedure. Written information leaflets help but do not replace discussion.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. The ward nurse: A nurse can witness consent but cannot explain the operation and its risks.
  • B. The most junior doctor on the team: A junior may take consent only if trained and able to explain the procedure; for this operation that is the surgeon.
  • D. The anaesthetist: The anaesthetist takes consent for the anaesthetic, not the operation.
  • E. The admitting medical officer: The admitting doctor does not perform the operation and cannot answer for its risks.

Key point: Consent should be taken by the person doing the procedure or someone with enough knowledge to explain it and answer questions.

Question 5 · Behavioural sciences and medical ethics · medium

An 80-year-old woman with a urinary infection becomes confused at night, pulling at her drip and not recognising her daughter, but is lucid in the mornings. A non-urgent decision is needed about whether she wants to move to her son's home or a care facility after discharge. The team plans to discuss it with her at 10 pm while she is confused.

What is the most appropriate approach?

  1. A. Let her daughter decide for her
  2. B. Decide at night when she is confused
  3. C. Assume she lacks capacity for all decisions
  4. D. Reassess when she is lucid
  5. E. Make the decision for her now
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. Reassess when she is lucid

Capacity can fluctuate, for example with delirium, infection, medication, fatigue or time of day. When a decision can wait, it should be made at a time when the person is most likely to have capacity, such as in the morning when she is lucid, with treatment of the underlying cause (here the infection) first. The assessment should be repeated if needed and the reasons recorded. If a decision is urgent and the person lacks capacity at that time, doctors act in her best interests, taking into account her past and present wishes and the views of family. Delirium should never be assumed to be permanent dementia.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Let her daughter decide for her: Her daughter cannot decide for her while she may still have capacity in the mornings when she is lucid.
  • B. Decide at night when she is confused: A discussion at 10 pm catches her at her most confused, when her answer will not reflect what she actually wants.
  • C. Assume she lacks capacity for all decisions: Her confusion comes and goes, so a blanket judgement that she lacks capacity for everything ignores her lucid mornings.
  • E. Make the decision for her now: Deciding for her now removes a choice she may well be able to make herself a few hours later.

Key point: Capacity can fluctuate; non-urgent decisions should wait until the person is at her best.

Question 6 · Behavioural sciences and medical ethics · medium

A 76-year-old widow who lives alone has a fractured neck of femur after a fall. She refuses surgery, saying she has lived a full life and wants to stay in bed at home with her cat rather than risk an operation. She understands she will probably never walk again and may develop complications. Her test of memory, orientation and reasoning is normal. Her son says her decision is foolish and proves she has lost her mind.

What should the team do?

  1. A. Operate because the choice is unwise
  2. B. Ask her son to consent instead
  3. C. Respect her informed refusal
  4. D. Sedate her for the operation
  5. E. Refer her for psychiatric detention
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Respect her informed refusal

A person is not to be treated as lacking capacity merely because they make a decision that others think unwise. Capacity concerns the process of decision-making (understanding, retaining, weighing and communicating), not the outcome. The team should make sure she has all the relevant information, including the benefits of surgery for pain and mobility, explore her reasons and fears, and offer time and support, but if she still refuses, her decision must be respected. She should receive good pain relief, nursing care and support at home, and be told she can change her mind. Family views are important to hear but cannot override a capable adult.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Operate because the choice is unwise: Her choice looks unwise to her son, but an unwise decision is not evidence of incapacity when she understands the consequences.
  • B. Ask her son to consent instead: Her son cannot consent for her; his view that she is foolish does not transfer the decision to him.
  • D. Sedate her for the operation: Sedating her to operate on a capable woman who has refused is assault, whatever the surgical benefit.
  • E. Refer her for psychiatric detention: She has no mental disorder, so there are no grounds for psychiatric detention; she is simply choosing differently from her son.

Key point: An unwise decision does not by itself show lack of capacity; a capable adult's refusal must be respected.

Question 7 · Behavioural sciences and medical ethics · medium

A 10-year-old boy with recurrent tonsillitis is listed for tonsillectomy. His parents agree to the operation. The boy is frightened and asks the doctor what will happen, whether it will hurt and when he can go back to school. The doctor wants to follow good practice in obtaining agreement for the operation.

What does good practice require here?

  1. A. Parental consent only; ignore the boy
  2. B. Consent plus his assent
  3. C. The boy's consent
  4. D. A court order
  5. E. Consent from a teacher
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Consent plus his assent

For young children, legal consent is usually given by a parent or guardian, but children should be involved in decisions about their care as far as their age and understanding allow. Assent means the child's agreement after an explanation suited to his level: what will happen, whether it will hurt, how pain will be managed and when he can return to normal activities. His fears should be addressed and his questions answered honestly. As children mature, their views carry more weight; older children and adolescents with enough understanding may be able to consent to some treatments themselves, depending on the law and the seriousness of the decision.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Parental consent only; ignore the boy: Parental consent is legally sufficient, but ignoring a frightened 10-year-old who is asking direct questions is poor practice.
  • C. The boy's consent: At 10 he is not usually judged able to consent alone, and his parents' consent is still needed.
  • D. A court order: A court order is for disputes or refusal of necessary treatment; his parents agree and nothing is contested.
  • E. Consent from a teacher: A teacher has no authority to consent for a child, and his parents are available and willing.

Key point: For children, obtain parental consent and the child's assent, explaining at a level he can understand.

Question 8 · Behavioural sciences and medical ethics · medium

A family physician driving on the motorway sees a car overturn in front of him. He stops safely and finds an injured driver who is conscious but bleeding heavily from a leg wound. The ambulance has been called and will take 20 minutes. He worries that he could be blamed if anything goes wrong because he has no equipment and works in a clinic, not an emergency department.

What is his ethical duty?

  1. A. Drive on; it is not his patient
  2. B. Perform complex procedures beyond his skills
  3. C. Help within his competence
  4. D. Wait for police permission
  5. E. Stay with the driver until the ambulance comes, without touching the wound
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Help within his competence

Professional codes, including the PM&DC code of ethics, expect doctors to offer help in an emergency, taking into account their own safety, their competence and the availability of other options. Here, he can make the scene safe, call for help, apply firm pressure to control the bleeding, keep the airway clear and reassure the patient until the ambulance arrives. He should act within his competence and not attempt procedures he is not trained to do. Doctors acting reasonably and in good faith in an emergency are rarely held liable; they should record what they did and hand over clearly to the ambulance crew.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Drive on; it is not his patient: The driver is not his registered patient, but a doctor at the scene of an emergency still has a duty to offer what help he can.
  • B. Perform complex procedures beyond his skills: Attempting procedures he has no training or equipment for would put the driver at greater risk than simple first aid.
  • D. Wait for police permission: No permission is needed to give first aid at the roadside, and waiting for the police wastes the twenty minutes before the ambulance.
  • E. Stay with the driver until the ambulance comes, without touching the wound: Heavy bleeding can kill within minutes; firm pressure on the wound is simple first aid within any doctor's competence.

Key point: Doctors have an ethical duty to help in emergencies within their competence and with regard to safety.

Question 9 · Behavioural sciences and medical ethics · medium

A 41-year-old public bus driver has had two generalised seizures in the past month. His doctor explains that he must stop driving until his seizures are controlled. He refuses, saying he will lose his job and his family will starve, and he is seen driving his bus the next week. Further discussion fails to persuade him.

What should the doctor do?

  1. A. Keep it confidential in all circumstances
  2. B. Tell his employer directly
  3. C. Tell his family and ask them to stop him
  4. D. Wait and see whether he has another seizure
  5. E. Inform the licensing authority
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Inform the licensing authority

Confidentiality is important but not absolute. It may be breached when there is a serious risk of death or harm to others that disclosure can prevent, when required by law, or with the patient's consent. A professional driver with uncontrolled seizures who continues to drive poses a serious risk to passengers and the public. The doctor should first try to persuade him to stop driving and inform the authority himself, explaining the risks and offering support (including with employment). If he refuses and continues to drive, the doctor should tell him she intends to disclose, then inform the licensing authority, sharing only the minimum necessary information, and document the decision.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Keep it confidential in all circumstances: Confidentiality is not absolute, and a bus driver having seizures at the wheel risks the lives of dozens of passengers.
  • B. Tell his employer directly: The employer is not the body responsible for licences; disclosure is limited to the licensing authority.
  • C. Tell his family and ask them to stop him: His family has no power to stop him and no right to his diagnosis without his consent.
  • D. Wait and see whether he has another seizure: Waiting leaves the public at risk; he has already refused to stop driving.

Key point: Confidentiality may be breached to prevent serious harm to others, such as an unfit driver who continues to drive.

Question 10 · Behavioural sciences and medical ethics · medium

A doctor at a private clinic receives a phone call from the human resources manager of a large company. The manager says one of their employees has been on sick leave and asks the doctor to confirm his diagnosis and how long he will be off work. The employee is the doctor's patient but has not given any written consent to share information with his employer.

What is the most appropriate response?

  1. A. Give the diagnosis since the employer pays
  2. B. Give only the diagnosis, not the prognosis
  3. C. Ask the manager to visit and read the notes
  4. D. Decline without the patient's consent
  5. E. Confirm if the manager seems genuine
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. Decline without the patient's consent

Employers, insurers, lawyers and other third parties are not entitled to medical information without the patient's consent, unless a law requires disclosure. The doctor should politely decline to give any information, including confirming that the person is a patient, and explain that written consent from the patient is needed. With consent, the doctor should share only information relevant to the request, such as fitness to work and expected time off, rather than the full diagnosis if that is not necessary. Phone calls also carry a risk of deception about who is calling. Sick leave certificates themselves can be given to the patient to pass on.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Give the diagnosis since the employer pays: Who pays the clinic does not change whose information it is; the diagnosis belongs to the employee.
  • B. Give only the diagnosis, not the prognosis: Withholding the prognosis does not make disclosure of the diagnosis lawful; both need his consent.
  • C. Ask the manager to visit and read the notes: Letting the manager read the notes exposes everything in them and goes far beyond anything he has agreed to.
  • E. Confirm if the manager seems genuine: A genuine manager still has no right to the information; checking identity is not the same as obtaining consent.

Key point: Do not give medical information to employers or other third parties without the patient's consent.
