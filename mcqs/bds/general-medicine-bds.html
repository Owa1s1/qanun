Question 1 · Cardiovascular disease and the dental patient · medium

A 60-year-old female from Abbottabad visits a dental clinic with acute periapical pain from a fractured upper left canine (tooth 23). She had a myocardial infarction 1 year ago and has chronic stable angina. Her current medications are aspirin 75 mg daily, metoprolol 50 mg daily, and atorvastatin 20 mg daily. After pulpectomy, she requires oral analgesia for moderate postoperative pain.

Which analgesic regimen is safest for this patient?

  1. A. Celecoxib 200 mg twice daily
  2. B. Diclofenac potassium 50 mg three times daily
  3. C. Ibuprofen 400 mg three times daily
  4. D. Paracetamol 1000 mg up to four times daily
  5. E. Meloxicam 15 mg once daily
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. Paracetamol 1000 mg up to four times daily

Patients with established ischaemic heart disease should avoid non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) and selective COX-2 inhibitors whenever possible. NSAIDs increase the risk of recurrent myocardial infarction, stroke, heart failure, and cardiovascular death. Furthermore, non-selective NSAIDs like ibuprofen interfere with the antiplatelet effect of low-dose aspirin by competitively binding to the cyclooxygenase-1 active site. Paracetamol (acetaminophen) at doses up to 1000 mg every 6 hours (maximum 4000 mg daily) is the first-line and safest analgesic for mild-to-moderate dental pain in patients with cardiovascular disease.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Celecoxib 200 mg twice daily: Selective COX-2 inhibitors are strictly contraindicated in ischaemic heart disease as they promote vascular thrombosis, stroke, and recurrent myocardial infarction.
  • B. Diclofenac potassium 50 mg three times daily: Diclofenac carries the highest cardiovascular thrombotic hazard among traditional non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs and is contraindicated in established coronary artery disease.
  • C. Ibuprofen 400 mg three times daily: Ibuprofen competitively blocks aspirin's irreversible platelet inhibition, increasing cardiovascular thrombotic risk, and promotes sodium retention and heart failure.
  • E. Meloxicam 15 mg once daily: Meloxicam exhibits preferential COX-2 inhibition and poses significant adverse cardiovascular and atherothrombotic risks in patients with prior myocardial infarction.

Key point: Select the safest oral analgesic for dental pain in a patient with ischaemic heart disease.

Question 2 · Diabetes and endocrine disease · medium

A 62-year-old woman attends a dental hospital in Hyderabad with an extraction socket of lower left first molar (36) that has not healed for three months. She has taken oral alendronate for osteoporosis for five years. Examination shows exposed bone in the socket with no pain or pus.

What is the most likely diagnosis?

  1. A. Chronic osteomyelitis
  2. B. Osteoradionecrosis of the jaw
  3. C. Squamous cell carcinoma
  4. D. Medication-related osteonecrosis
  5. E. Chronic periodontitis
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. Medication-related osteonecrosis

Medication-related osteonecrosis of the jaw (MRONJ) is exposed bone in the maxillofacial region that fails to heal after eight weeks in a patient who has taken antiresorptive medication, with no history of head and neck radiotherapy. Oral bisphosphonates such as alendronate carry a low but real risk, higher after prolonged use and invasive dental surgery. The lesion is often asymptomatic and may be discovered at a routine visit. Management is conservative with chlorhexidine rinses and analgesia; surgery is reserved for infection or pathological fracture. Routine antibiotic prophylaxis is not indicated for extraction in patients on oral bisphosphonates.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Chronic osteomyelitis: Osteomyelitis usually presents with pain, fever, swelling and purulent discharge, which are absent.
  • B. Osteoradionecrosis of the jaw: Osteoradionecrosis occurs after radiotherapy to the jaws, and this patient has no history of head and neck irradiation.
  • C. Squamous cell carcinoma: Carcinoma would show an ulcer, induration or a mass on biopsy, not simply exposed bone in a socket.
  • E. Chronic periodontitis: Periodontitis causes pocketing and bleeding gums, not exposed bone in a healed extraction socket.

Key point: Diagnose medication-related osteonecrosis of the jaw from exposed bone in an extraction socket in a patient taking oral bisphosphonates.

Question 3 · Gastroenterology and hepatology · easy

A 52-year-old man attends a dental clinic in Mardan with irreversible pulpitis in 36, lower left first molar. He has known cirrhosis from chronic hepatitis C and requests analgesia. Examination shows no facial swelling. INR 1.3, platelets 98,000/µL (150,000-400,000).

Which analgesic is most appropriate?

  1. A. Aspirin 300 mg four times daily
  2. B. Diclofenac 50 mg three times daily
  3. C. Ibuprofen 400 mg three times daily
  4. D. Naproxen 500 mg twice daily
  5. E. Paracetamol 500 mg four times daily
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Paracetamol 500 mg four times daily

Cirrhosis reduces synthetic liver function, causing coagulopathy and thrombocytopenia, so drugs that impair platelets or damage the kidney are hazardous. NSAIDs such as ibuprofen, diclofenac and naproxen inhibit platelet function, can precipitate gastrointestinal bleeding and may reduce renal perfusion in chronic liver disease. Aspirin adds irreversible platelet inhibition. Paracetamol is the preferred analgesic because it lacks antiplatelet activity; in stable cirrhosis a short course limited to 2 g daily is generally acceptable. Advise Pakistani patients, who often buy NSAIDs over the counter, to avoid regular use and not to combine products containing paracetamol.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Aspirin 300 mg four times daily: Antiplatelet effect worsens bleeding risk with low platelets and raised INR.
  • B. Diclofenac 50 mg three times daily: NSAID; same bleeding and renal concerns as ibuprofen; avoid in chronic liver disease.
  • C. Ibuprofen 400 mg three times daily: NSAID; increases gastrointestinal bleeding and renal risk in cirrhosis, and platelets are already low.
  • D. Naproxen 500 mg twice daily: NSAID; same bleeding and renal concerns as ibuprofen and diclofenac in cirrhosis.

Key point: Select a safe analgesic for a patient with cirrhosis and dental pain.

Question 4 · Haematological disorders · medium

A 42-year-old woman from Quetta is assessed before dental extraction of 15, upper right second premolar. She has a lifelong history of easy bruising and nosebleeds. Her brother had prolonged bleeding after tooth extraction. Platelet count 200,000/µL (150,000-400,000), PT 12 seconds (11-13), APTT 68 seconds (25-35). A 1:1 mixing study with normal plasma corrects the APTT.

Which tests are most appropriate next?

  1. A. Bleeding time with a template device
  2. B. Repeat prothrombin time and INR
  3. C. Von Willebrand panel with factor VIII
  4. D. Platelet aggregation studies
  5. E. D-dimer and fibrinogen level
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Von Willebrand panel with factor VIII

An isolated prolonged APTT with a normal PT and platelet count points to the intrinsic pathway, and correction on mixing means a factor deficiency rather than an inhibitor. In a woman with lifelong mucocutaneous bleeding (bruising, nosebleeds) and an affected brother, von Willebrand disease is the most likely cause: it is the commonest inherited bleeding disorder, is usually autosomal dominant and lowers factor VIII because VWF carries it. The first tests are VWF antigen, VWF activity and factor VIII together; factor VIII alone would miss the diagnosis. Bleeding time is unreliable and obsolete, platelet aggregation studies come later, and D-dimer has no role.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Bleeding time with a template device: Bleeding time is crude, poorly reproducible and does not identify the cause; it has been replaced by specific assays.
  • B. Repeat prothrombin time and INR: The prothrombin time is already normal; repeating it adds nothing.
  • D. Platelet aggregation studies: Platelet aggregation studies look for platelet function disorders; the VWF panel comes first when the APTT is prolonged.
  • E. D-dimer and fibrinogen level: D-dimer reflects fibrin breakdown in thrombosis or DIC and plays no part in a lifelong bleeding history.

Key point: Order a von Willebrand panel with factor VIII for a woman with mucocutaneous bleeding and a corrected APTT.

Question 5 · Neurological disease · easy

A 60-year-old woman attends a dental clinic in Hyderabad with brief attacks of severe electric-shock pain in her right cheek. The pain is triggered by chewing, talking and light touch, lasts seconds, and recurs several times a day. She has no facial numbness.

What is the most likely diagnosis?

  1. A. Irreversible pulpitis
  2. B. Temporal arteritis
  3. C. Migraine
  4. D. Cluster headache
  5. E. Trigeminal neuralgia
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Trigeminal neuralgia

Trigeminal neuralgia causes sudden, severe, electric-shock-like pain in one division of the trigeminal nerve, classically triggered by chewing, talking or light touch. Attacks last seconds to two minutes and recur frequently. There is usually no sensory loss. Carbamazepine is first-line treatment. Dental causes must be excluded, but the pain is neuropathic rather than odontogenic. Surgical options are considered only when medical treatment fails or side effects are intolerable.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Irreversible pulpitis: Pulpitis causes lingering toothache triggered by hot or cold, not brief cheek pain triggered by touch.
  • B. Temporal arteritis: This causes scalp tenderness and jaw claudication in older patients, not electric-shock cheek pain.
  • C. Migraine: Migraine causes throbbing headache with nausea and lasts hours, not seconds of facial pain.
  • D. Cluster headache: Cluster headache causes severe unilateral orbital pain with tearing and nasal congestion, not trigger-zone facial pain.

Key point: Recognise trigeminal neuralgia from brief trigger-induced electric-shock facial pain.

Question 6 · Respiratory disease · medium

A 72-year-old man is on a neurology ward in Larkana. He had a stroke two weeks ago and has dysphagia. He now has fever, cough and drowsiness. Chest X-ray shows consolidation in the right lower lobe. He is nil by mouth.

What is the most likely type of pneumonia?

  1. A. Community-acquired pneumonia
  2. B. Pulmonary infarction from an embolus
  3. C. Pneumocystis pneumonia
  4. D. Aspiration pneumonia
  5. E. Viral pneumonia
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. Aspiration pneumonia

Aspiration pneumonia results from inhalation of oropharyngeal or gastric contents, commonly in patients with impaired consciousness, stroke or dysphagia. The right lower lobe is the usual site because of bronchial anatomy. Patients may be drowsy, febrile and have a productive cough. Good oral hygiene, upright positioning and suction during dental treatment reduce the risk of further aspiration and are relevant to safe dental care. Elective treatment should be postponed during an acute episode.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Community-acquired pneumonia: He has been in hospital for two weeks, and dysphagia after stroke points to aspiration.
  • B. Pulmonary infarction from an embolus: Embolism gives pleuritic pain and breathlessness; fever with consolidation after dysphagia points to aspiration.
  • C. Pneumocystis pneumonia: Pneumocystis affects the severely immunocompromised and causes diffuse, not lobar, shadowing.
  • E. Viral pneumonia: Viral pneumonia usually gives patchy bilateral change, and the dysphagia makes aspiration far likelier.

Key point: Identify aspiration pneumonia in a post-stroke patient with dysphagia and lobar consolidation.

Question 7 · Infectious diseases, renal disease and medical emergencies · easy

A 40-year-old man with HIV presents to a dental clinic in Hyderabad with painful swallowing and white plaques on the buccal mucosa that can be scraped off. His CD4 count is 95 cells/µL (500-1,600). He has been on antiretroviral therapy for six months.

What does this CD4 count indicate about his immune status?

  1. A. Normal immune competence
  2. B. Early immunosuppression
  3. C. Moderate immunosuppression
  4. D. AIDS (stage 3 HIV infection)
  5. E. Immune reconstitution
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. AIDS (stage 3 HIV infection)

CDC staging classifies HIV infection partly by CD4 lymphocyte count. A count below 200 cells/µL defines stage 3 AIDS even without an AIDS-defining opportunistic infection. Oral candidiasis is common in HIV but scrapable buccal plaques are not themselves AIDS-defining; oesophageal candidiasis would be. The patient is on antiretroviral therapy, yet a persistently low CD4 count shows advanced immune damage and warrants medical review before extensive dental care. Standard precautions apply, and elective surgery may be deferred if the patient is acutely unwell.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Normal immune competence: A CD4 count of 95 cells/µL is far below the normal reference range and indicates significant immune suppression, not normal competence.
  • B. Early immunosuppression: Early immunosuppression corresponds to higher CD4 counts; a value under 200 cells/µL denotes severe immune damage.
  • C. Moderate immunosuppression: Advanced immunosuppression is true, but the specific CDC stage for a CD4 count below 200 cells/µL is AIDS, making this less precise.
  • E. Immune reconstitution: Immune reconstitution occurs after starting antiretroviral therapy and is usually accompanied by a rising CD4 count, not a low value.

Key point: Interpret a CD4 count below 200 cells/µL in the context of HIV staging.

Question 8 · Cardiovascular disease and the dental patient · easy

A 62-year-old male from Peshawar attends the emergency department with severe, crushing retrosternal pain radiating to the left jaw and shoulder that began 45 minutes ago. He is pale, diaphoretic, and nauseated. Blood pressure is 100/65 mmHg and pulse is 96/min. The medical team records an immediate 12-lead electrocardiogram.

Which ECG finding indicates an acute anterior transmural infarction?

  1. A. Symmetrical T-wave inversion in leads II, III, and aVF
  2. B. Pathological Q waves without ST-segment changes
  3. C. New right bundle branch block with normal axis
  4. D. PR-segment depression across multiple leads
  5. E. ST-segment elevation of 2 mm at the J-point in leads V1 to V4
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. ST-segment elevation of 2 mm at the J-point in leads V1 to V4

Acute ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) results from complete, persistent thrombotic occlusion of an epicardial coronary artery, most commonly the left anterior descending artery in anterior infarctions. Diagnosis requires typical ischaemic symptoms and new ST-segment elevation at the J-point in at least two contiguous leads (>=2 mm in men >=40 years in leads V2-V3, and >=1 mm in other leads). Prompt recognition is vital because emergency reperfusion therapy via primary percutaneous coronary intervention or fibrinolytic therapy must be initiated rapidly to salvage ischaemic myocardium and reduce mortality.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Symmetrical T-wave inversion in leads II, III, and aVF: T-wave inversion in inferior leads indicates ischaemia or evolving infarction of the inferior wall rather than acute anterior transmural infarction.
  • B. Pathological Q waves without ST-segment changes: Isolated Q waves reflect previous necrosis or completed infarction from past events rather than hyperacute transmural myocardial injury requiring emergency reperfusion.
  • C. New right bundle branch block with normal axis: Right bundle branch block does not satisfy diagnostic criteria for acute transmural anterior myocardial infarction unless accompanied by concordant ST elevation.
  • D. PR-segment depression across multiple leads: PR-segment depression is characteristic of acute pericarditis rather than localized transmural anterior myocardial infarction.

Key point: Identify the electrocardiographic hallmark of acute ST-elevation myocardial infarction.

Question 9 · Diabetes and endocrine disease · easy

A 45 year old man attends a dental teaching hospital in Peshawar for extraction of 36, lower left first molar. He reports increased thirst and passing urine often for three months. He has no nausea, vomiting or abdominal pain.

Which single result, on its own, establishes the diagnosis of diabetes mellitus?

  1. A. Fasting plasma glucose 110 mg/dL
  2. B. Random plasma glucose 218 mg/dL with classic symptoms
  3. C. HbA1c 6.0 percent
  4. D. Urine dipstick positive for glucose
  5. E. Fasting plasma glucose 126 mg/dL without symptoms
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Random plasma glucose 218 mg/dL with classic symptoms

Diabetes mellitus is diagnosed when fasting plasma glucose is 126 mg/dL or higher, random plasma glucose is 200 mg/dL or more with classic symptoms, or HbA1c is 6.5 percent or higher. In a patient with increased thirst and polyuria, a single random value above 200 mg/dL is sufficient. Fasting values require confirmation on a separate day unless symptoms are unequivocal. Urine glucose is not diagnostic because the renal threshold varies. In Pakistan, fasting glucose is widely available in most laboratories, while HbA1c may cost more and is less accessible in smaller towns.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Fasting plasma glucose 110 mg/dL: Fasting glucose must be 126 mg/dL or higher on two occasions; 110 mg/dL is below the diagnostic threshold.
  • C. HbA1c 6.0 percent: HbA1c 6.0 percent is below the 6.5 percent diagnostic threshold and was not measured.
  • D. Urine dipstick positive for glucose: Glycosuria depends on the renal threshold and is not specific enough to diagnose diabetes mellitus.
  • E. Fasting plasma glucose 126 mg/dL without symptoms: Without classic symptoms, a fasting value of 126 mg/dL or more must be confirmed by a second test.

Key point: To recognize the blood glucose criteria that establish a diagnosis of diabetes mellitus.

Question 10 · Gastroenterology and hepatology · easy

A 62-year-old man visits a private dental clinic in Karachi complaining of progressive difficulty swallowing solid food over three months. He has lost 5 kg and has no regurgitation of undigested food. He is a long-term cigarette smoker. On examination he is thin but has no lymphadenopathy. There is no heartburn.

Which investigation should be done first?

  1. A. Barium swallow
  2. B. Upper gastrointestinal endoscopy with biopsy
  3. C. Oesophageal manometry
  4. D. Twenty-four-hour pH monitoring
  5. E. Computed tomography of the chest, abdomen and pelvis
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Upper gastrointestinal endoscopy with biopsy

In a patient over 50 with progressive dysphagia to solids and weight loss, upper gastrointestinal endoscopy with biopsy is the first investigation because it can both visualise and obtain histology from an oesophageal lesion. Barium swallow lacks tissue diagnosis, manometry is for motility disorders, pH monitoring is for reflux without alarm features, and CT is for staging after histology. Smoking is a major risk factor for oesophageal squamous cell carcinoma in Pakistan.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Barium swallow: A barium swallow can show a lesion but cannot obtain tissue; it is usually reserved when endoscopy is unavailable or unsafe.
  • C. Oesophageal manometry: Manometry assesses motility in achalasia or spasm, where dysphagia affects liquids as well as solids from the outset.
  • D. Twenty-four-hour pH monitoring: PH monitoring confirms reflux disease but is not the first test when alarm features such as weight loss are present.
  • E. Computed tomography of the chest, abdomen and pelvis: CT staging is performed after a biopsy confirms malignancy, not as the initial diagnostic test.

Key point: Select upper GI endoscopy with biopsy as the first investigation for dysphagia with alarm features.
