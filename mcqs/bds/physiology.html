Question 1 · Gastrointestinal system · easy

Recordings from the smooth muscle of the small intestine show regular, rhythmic changes in membrane potential about 12 times a minute, even when no contraction occurs.

Which cells generate these slow waves?

  1. A. Interstitial cells of Cajal
  2. B. Enterochromaffin-like cells
  3. C. Parietal cells
  4. D. Myenteric plexus neurons
  5. E. Goblet cells
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Interstitial cells of Cajal

Interstitial cells of Cajal, between the smooth muscle layers, produce slow waves: rhythmic depolarisations that spread through gap junctions. Slow waves alone do not cause contraction; when they reach threshold, spike potentials (Ca²⁺ entry) occur and the muscle contracts. Their frequency sets the maximum rate of contraction: about 3 per minute in the stomach, 12 in the duodenum, falling to about 8 in the ileum. Nerves and hormones change how often slow waves reach threshold.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Enterochromaffin-like cells: ECL cells are endocrine cells in the stomach wall that release histamine to stimulate acid; they have no electrical pacemaker role.
  • C. Parietal cells: Parietal cells secrete hydrochloric acid and intrinsic factor in the stomach; they do not generate rhythmic electrical activity in the muscle.
  • D. Myenteric plexus neurons: These neurons change the strength and timing of contractions, but slow waves continue even when the nerves are blocked, so they are not the source.
  • E. Goblet cells: Goblet cells are epithelial cells that secrete mucus into the lumen; they lie far from the muscle layers and cannot set its rhythm.

Key point: Interstitial cells of Cajal generate gut slow waves; spike potentials on top of them cause contraction.

Question 2 · Nervous system and special senses · medium

A surgeon operating under local anaesthesia of the abdominal wall can cut, crush or burn the small bowel without the patient feeling pain. Later, when the bowel is stretched by obstruction, the patient feels severe, poorly localised central abdominal cramps.

What explains these features of visceral pain?

  1. A. Sparse, convergent afferents sense stretch, not cutting
  2. B. The bowel has no pain fibres at all
  3. C. Visceral pain travels in the vagus nerve to the cortex
  4. D. Cutting pain is blocked by the local anaesthetic in the bowel wall
  5. E. The bowel pain fibres are large myelinated A-beta fibres
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Sparse, convergent afferents sense stretch, not cutting

Visceral organs are supplied by relatively few nociceptive fibres (mostly unmyelinated C fibres) that travel with sympathetic nerves to the spinal cord. They respond to distension, spasm, ischaemia, inflammation and traction rather than cutting or burning. Because they are sparse and converge with other visceral and somatic inputs on dorsal horn neurons over several segments, the pain is dull, poorly localised and often felt in the midline according to the organ's embryological segment (foregut epigastric, midgut periumbilical, hindgut suprapubic), and may be referred to skin areas. When inflammation reaches the parietal peritoneum, which has somatic innervation, pain becomes sharp and localised.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. The bowel has no pain fibres at all: The bowel does have pain fibres; stretching it is painful.
  • C. Visceral pain travels in the vagus nerve to the cortex: Most visceral pain travels with sympathetic nerves, not the vagus.
  • D. Cutting pain is blocked by the local anaesthetic in the bowel wall: The anaesthetic was in the abdominal wall, not the bowel.
  • E. The bowel pain fibres are large myelinated A-beta fibres: Visceral pain fibres are mainly C fibres, not large A-beta fibres.

Key point: Visceral pain is triggered by stretch and ischaemia, is poorly localised and midline, and is referred by segment.

Question 3 · Renal physiology, body fluids and acid-base · easy

A 60-year-old man has a large stone blocking his left ureter.

Which Starling force changes directly to lower the GFR of his left kidney?

  1. A. Hydrostatic pressure in Bowman's capsule rises
  2. B. Glomerular capillary hydrostatic pressure rises
  3. C. Glomerular capillary oncotic pressure falls
  4. D. Oncotic pressure in Bowman's capsule rises
  5. E. The filtration coefficient increases
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Hydrostatic pressure in Bowman's capsule rises

Net filtration pressure in the glomerulus = glomerular capillary hydrostatic pressure (about 60 mmHg) − Bowman's capsule hydrostatic pressure (about 18 mmHg) − glomerular capillary oncotic pressure (about 32 mmHg on average), which gives about +10 mmHg. A ureteric obstruction raises the pressure in Bowman's capsule and reduces filtration. Prolonged obstruction damages the kidney (obstructive nephropathy), which is why urgent relief is needed when both kidneys or a single kidney are blocked.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Glomerular capillary hydrostatic pressure rises: Higher glomerular capillary pressure would increase GFR, not lower it.
  • C. Glomerular capillary oncotic pressure falls: Lower oncotic pressure in the glomerular capillaries would increase GFR.
  • D. Oncotic pressure in Bowman's capsule rises: Bowman's space normally contains almost no protein, so its oncotic pressure is negligible.
  • E. The filtration coefficient increases: A larger filtration coefficient would increase GFR.

Key point: Ureteric obstruction lowers GFR by raising Bowman's capsule hydrostatic pressure.

Question 4 · Respiratory system · easy

A man breathes 12 times a minute with a tidal volume of 500 mL. His anatomical dead space is 150 mL.

What is his alveolar ventilation?

  1. A. 350 mL/min
  2. B. 1.8 L/min
  3. C. 4.2 L/min
  4. D. 6.0 L/min
  5. E. 7.8 L/min
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. 4.2 L/min

Minute ventilation = tidal volume × rate = 6 L/min. Only the part of each breath beyond the dead space reaches the alveoli: alveolar ventilation = (tidal volume − dead space) × rate = 350 × 12 = 4.2 L/min. Rapid shallow breathing is inefficient: at a tidal volume of 200 mL and rate of 30, minute ventilation is still 6 L/min but alveolar ventilation is only (200 − 150) × 30 = 1.5 L/min. Alveolar ventilation, not minute ventilation, sets the arterial PCO₂.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. 350 mL/min: 350 mL is the fresh air reaching the alveoli in one breath; it must be multiplied by 12 breaths a minute.
  • B. 1.8 L/min: 1.8 L/min is the dead space ventilation (150 x 12), the air that never reaches the alveoli.
  • D. 6.0 L/min: 6.0 L/min is the total minute ventilation (500 x 12), which includes the dead space.
  • E. 7.8 L/min: 7.8 L/min adds the dead space ventilation instead of subtracting it.

Key point: Alveolar ventilation = (tidal volume − dead space) × respiratory rate.

Question 5 · Haematology and immunity · easy

A 60-year-old woman has tiredness, a sore red tongue and tingling in her feet. She has vitiligo and her mother had thyroid disease. Her haemoglobin is 7.5 g/dL and her MCV is 118 fL. Her blood film shows oval macrocytes and neutrophils with six-lobed nuclei. Her bilirubin and LDH are raised. Vitamin B12 is low.

What is the underlying defect in her red cell precursors?

  1. A. Impaired DNA synthesis
  2. B. Impaired haem synthesis
  3. C. Impaired globin chain synthesis
  4. D. Destruction of red cells in the spleen
  5. E. Lack of erythropoietin
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Impaired DNA synthesis

Vitamin B12 and folate are needed to make thymidine for DNA synthesis. In deficiency, DNA synthesis slows while RNA and protein synthesis continue, so precursors grow large with immature nuclei (megaloblasts), and many die in the marrow (ineffective erythropoiesis, raising bilirubin and LDH). The blood shows macrocytes, hypersegmented neutrophils, and sometimes low white cells and platelets. B12 deficiency can also cause neurological damage. Other causes of macrocytosis without megaloblasts include alcohol, liver disease, hypothyroidism and reticulocytosis.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Impaired haem synthesis: Faulty haem synthesis (iron deficiency, lead, sideroblastic anaemia) makes small cells, not large ones with an MCV of 118.
  • C. Impaired globin chain synthesis: Reduced globin chain synthesis is thalassaemia, which makes small red cells.
  • D. Destruction of red cells in the spleen: Destruction in the spleen is haemolysis; it raises bilirubin but does not make cells large with six-lobed neutrophils.
  • E. Lack of erythropoietin: Lack of erythropoietin, as in kidney disease, gives normal-sized red cells, not large ones.

Key point: Megaloblastic anaemia (B12 or folate deficiency) results from impaired DNA synthesis.

Question 6 · Cardiovascular system · easy

Skeletal muscle can be stimulated repeatedly so that its twitches fuse into a sustained (tetanic) contraction. Cardiac ventricular muscle cannot be tetanised in this way.

What property of cardiac muscle explains this?

  1. A. Its effective refractory period lasts almost as long as its contraction
  2. B. It has no sarcoplasmic reticulum
  3. C. Its fibres are connected by gap junctions
  4. D. It does not use calcium for contraction
  5. E. Its resting membrane potential is less negative than skeletal muscle
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Its effective refractory period lasts almost as long as its contraction

The refractory period of ventricular muscle is about 0.25–0.30 s, roughly the length of the action potential and most of the contraction. During this time the fast Na⁺ channels remain inactivated and no new action potential can be fired. As a result, a second contraction cannot be added to the first, so the heart always relaxes and refills between beats. The relative refractory period that follows lasts about another 0.05 s.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. It has no sarcoplasmic reticulum: Cardiac muscle does have a sarcoplasmic reticulum, although smaller than in skeletal muscle, so this is not the reason.
  • C. Its fibres are connected by gap junctions: Gap junctions let the heart act as one unit (a syncytium), but they do not stop summation or tetanus.
  • D. It does not use calcium for contraction: Cardiac contraction depends on calcium, part of it entering from outside the cell during the plateau.
  • E. Its resting membrane potential is less negative than skeletal muscle: The ventricular resting potential (about −90 mV) is similar to skeletal muscle and is not the reason.

Key point: The long effective refractory period of cardiac muscle prevents tetanic contraction.

Question 7 · Endocrine and reproduction · easy

A newborn screening programme measures TSH in a heel-prick blood sample from every baby.

Why is early detection of congenital hypothyroidism so important?

  1. A. Thyroid hormone is essential for brain development in the first years
  2. B. It prevents neonatal jaundice
  3. C. Untreated babies develop thyrotoxicosis
  4. D. It prevents iodine deficiency in the mother
  5. E. Thyroid hormone is needed only for growth, which can be corrected later
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Thyroid hormone is essential for brain development in the first years

Thyroid hormone is needed for neuronal growth, myelination and synapse formation in the fetal brain and the first two to three years of life. Babies with congenital hypothyroidism (most often from an absent or misplaced thyroid) usually look normal at birth because some maternal T4 crosses the placenta. Signs such as prolonged jaundice, a large tongue, umbilical hernia and poor feeding appear later. If levothyroxine is started within the first two weeks, development is usually normal; delayed treatment causes permanent intellectual disability.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. It prevents neonatal jaundice: Prolonged jaundice can be a sign, but preventing brain damage is the main reason for screening.
  • C. Untreated babies develop thyrotoxicosis: Untreated hypothyroid babies do not become thyrotoxic.
  • D. It prevents iodine deficiency in the mother: Screening the baby does not treat iodine deficiency in the mother.
  • E. Thyroid hormone is needed only for growth, which can be corrected later: Brain damage from early deficiency cannot be reversed by later treatment.

Key point: Congenital hypothyroidism must be treated in the first weeks to prevent permanent intellectual disability.

Question 8 · Cell, nerve and muscle · easy

A dentist injects lidocaine around the inferior alveolar nerve before filling a molar. The patient first loses pain and temperature sensation in the lower lip and teeth, then later the ability to feel the dentist's touch and pressure, and the bite still feels normal. His jaw muscles are not affected.

Why are pain fibres blocked first?

  1. A. Small fibres are blocked more easily
  2. B. Pain fibres are the largest and fastest
  3. C. Lidocaine binds pain receptors in the skin
  4. D. Touch fibres have no sodium channels
  5. E. Lidocaine opens potassium channels in pain fibres
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Small fibres are blocked more easily

Local anaesthetics enter the axon in their uncharged form and block voltage-gated Na⁺ channels from the inside, binding best to open and inactivated channels (use-dependent block). Small fibres are blocked first because a shorter length of axon needs to be blocked to stop conduction and they fire more often; the usual order of loss is pain and temperature (Aδ and C), then touch and pressure, then motor (Aα). In inflamed, acidic tissue, more of the drug is charged and cannot cross the membrane, so it works less well. Pressure on a nerve blocks the large fibres first.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Pain fibres are the largest and fastest: Pain fibres are among the smallest and slowest; large fast fibres carry touch, pressure and motor signals.
  • C. Lidocaine binds pain receptors in the skin: Lidocaine blocks sodium channels inside the axon; it does not act on pain receptors in the skin.
  • D. Touch fibres have no sodium channels: All these nerve fibres use voltage-gated sodium channels to conduct; that is why lidocaine eventually blocks all of them.
  • E. Lidocaine opens potassium channels in pain fibres: Lidocaine blocks sodium channels; it does not open potassium channels.

Key point: Local anaesthetics block Na⁺ channels; small pain fibres are blocked before large motor fibres.

Question 9 · Gastrointestinal system · easy

Acid secretion by parietal cells is stimulated by acetylcholine, gastrin and histamine.

How does gastrin mainly increase acid secretion?

  1. A. By releasing histamine from ECL cells
  2. B. By blocking somatostatin receptors
  3. C. By inhibiting the proton pump
  4. D. By raising pH in the antrum
  5. E. By stimulating secretin release
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. By releasing histamine from ECL cells

Gastrin, from G cells in the antrum, is released by peptides and amino acids in the stomach, stomach distension and vagal stimulation (gastrin-releasing peptide). It acts mainly on enterochromaffin-like (ECL) cells to release histamine, which acts on H2 receptors of parietal cells; it also acts directly on parietal cells. Acetylcholine from vagal fibres acts on M3 receptors. Low pH in the antrum releases somatostatin from D cells, which inhibits gastrin and acid: a negative feedback loop. This is why H2 blockers reduce acid stimulated by gastrin and food.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. By blocking somatostatin receptors: Somatostatin is the brake on gastrin and acid; gastrin does not work by blocking its receptors.
  • C. By inhibiting the proton pump: The proton pump is the final step of acid secretion; blocking it would reduce acid, which is the opposite of what gastrin does.
  • D. By raising pH in the antrum: A low antral pH turns gastrin off; gastrin is released when the pH rises after food, so it does not cause the rise.
  • E. By stimulating secretin release: Secretin is released by acid in the duodenum and inhibits gastrin and acid; gastrin does not work through it.

Key point: Gastrin stimulates acid mainly via histamine from ECL cells; low antral pH inhibits gastrin through somatostatin.

Question 10 · Nervous system and special senses · easy

A child who knocks his elbow instinctively rubs it, and the pain eases.

What mechanism explains why rubbing reduces the pain?

  1. A. Touch fibres excite dorsal horn inhibitory cells
  2. B. Rubbing destroys the pain receptors
  3. C. Rubbing releases histamine
  4. D. Rubbing blocks the corticospinal tract
  5. E. Rubbing increases blood flow, washing away pain signals
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Touch fibres excite dorsal horn inhibitory cells

The gate control theory proposes that activity in large, myelinated touch fibres (Aβ) excites inhibitory interneurons in the substantia gelatinosa of the dorsal horn, which reduce transmission from small pain fibres (Aδ and C) to the projection neurons. Rubbing, vibration and transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation (TENS) use this effect. Descending pathways from the brain also control the gate.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Rubbing destroys the pain receptors: Rubbing does not damage the pain receptors.
  • C. Rubbing releases histamine: Histamine causes itch and pain; it would make the pain worse.
  • D. Rubbing blocks the corticospinal tract: The corticospinal tract carries motor commands, not pain.
  • E. Rubbing increases blood flow, washing away pain signals: Washing away chemicals is not how rubbing reduces pain within seconds.

Key point: Touch (Aβ) input activates dorsal horn inhibitory interneurons that reduce pain transmission (gate control).
