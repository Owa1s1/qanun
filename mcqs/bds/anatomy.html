Anatomy MCQs for BDS

10 free anatomy questions for every year of BDS at KMU and UHS, written in the style of the real exam. Try each one, then open the answer: every wrong option is explained too. Qanun has 247 anatomy questions for BDS, covering general anatomy, embryology and histology; head and neck; neuroanatomy.

Question 1 · General anatomy, embryology and histology · easy

A newborn girl delivered at term in Lahore has a large, lobulated mass at the base of her spine, hanging between her buttocks, nearly as big as her head. Her legs move normally and the skin over her lower back is intact. The mass is removed in the first week. It contains hair, teeth and bits of bone, with tissue from all three germ layers.

Remnants of which embryonic structure most likely gave rise to this tumour?

  1. A. Notochord
  2. B. Primitive streak
  3. C. Neural crest
  4. D. Allantois
  5. E. Vitelline duct
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Primitive streak

The primitive streak appears at the caudal end of the epiblast in the third week, and cells passing through it form mesoderm and endoderm. It normally regresses and disappears. If pluripotent remnants persist at its caudal end, they may form a sacrococcygeal teratoma, the commonest tumour of the newborn, more frequent in girls. Teratomas contain tissues from all three germ layers, such as hair, teeth, bone and gut. Most are benign but should be removed early, with the coccyx, to prevent malignant change.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Notochord: Notochord remnants give chordomas, which do not contain tissues of all three layers.
  • C. Neural crest: Neural crest tumours include neuroblastoma, which does not contain hair and teeth.
  • D. Allantois: Allantois remnants give urachal anomalies.
  • E. Vitelline duct: Remnants of the vitelline duct give a Meckel diverticulum.

Key point: A sacrococcygeal teratoma arises from remnants of the primitive streak and contains tissues of all three germ layers.

Question 2 · Head and neck · easy

A 45-year-old woman has a total thyroidectomy for a large multinodular goitre; the right lobe extended low and was hard to free. After the operation, she has a weak, breathy voice and coughs when drinking. Her calcium is normal. Laryngoscopy shows her right vocal fold does not move, lying slightly away from the midline.

During which step of the operation is this nerve most at risk?

  1. A. Dividing the isthmus over the trachea
  2. B. Tying the superior thyroid artery at the upper pole
  3. C. Ligating the inferior thyroid artery near the gland
  4. D. Cutting the strap muscles
  5. E. Opening the pretracheal fascia in front
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Ligating the inferior thyroid artery near the gland

The recurrent laryngeal nerves run up in the groove between the trachea and oesophagus, passing close to (in front of, behind or between) the branches of the inferior thyroid artery near the lower pole of the gland, before entering the larynx behind the cricothyroid joint. They supply all the intrinsic muscles of the larynx except cricothyroid. To protect the nerve, surgeons identify it and tie the inferior thyroid artery branches close to the gland capsule. Damage on one side gives a hoarse or breathy voice; on both sides it can cause stridor and airway obstruction.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Dividing the isthmus over the trachea: The nerves lie further back and to the sides.
  • B. Tying the superior thyroid artery at the upper pole: This endangers the external branch of the superior laryngeal nerve.
  • D. Cutting the strap muscles: These lie in front of the gland.
  • E. Opening the pretracheal fascia in front: The nerve lies deeper, in the groove between the trachea and oesophagus.

Key point: The recurrent laryngeal nerve is at risk where it is related to the inferior thyroid artery during thyroidectomy.

Question 3 · Neuroanatomy · easy

A 70-year-old man with known cervical spondylosis and a stiff neck trips and falls forward, hitting his forehead and bending his neck backwards. He has burning pain in both hands and weakness much worse in his hands and arms than his legs, which he can move well. He has retention of urine. X-ray shows no fracture.

Why are the arms more affected than the legs?

  1. A. The arm fibres lie in the dorsal columns
  2. B. The leg fibres cross over in the cervical cord
  3. C. Arm fibres lie medially, nearest the cord centre
  4. D. The legs are supplied by the posterior spinal arteries
  5. E. The arm fibres have less myelin than the leg fibres
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Arm fibres lie medially, nearest the cord centre

In the lateral corticospinal tract the fibres are arranged in layers: those to the cervical segments (arms) lie medially, and those to the sacral and lumbar segments (legs) lie laterally. A sudden hyperextension injury in a narrow, arthritic cervical canal bruises the centre of the cord. The damage spreads outward, so the arms (especially the hands) are weaker than the legs. There may be patchy sensory loss and bladder problems. Many patients recover partly, legs first.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. The arm fibres lie in the dorsal columns: Motor fibres for the arm are in the corticospinal tract.
  • B. The leg fibres cross over in the cervical cord: Corticospinal fibres cross in the medulla.
  • D. The legs are supplied by the posterior spinal arteries: Blood supply does not explain this pattern.
  • E. The arm fibres have less myelin than the leg fibres: Myelin thickness is not the reason.

Key point: In central cord syndrome the arms are weaker than the legs because arm fibres lie medially in the corticospinal tract.

Question 4 · General anatomy, embryology and histology · easy

A term baby boy did not pass meconium in the first 48 hours. He has abdominal distension and bilious vomiting. When the doctor does a rectal examination, there is an explosive gush of stool and gas. Contrast enema shows a narrow rectum with dilated colon above it. Rectal biopsy shows no ganglion cells in the wall of the bowel, with thick nerve fibres.

The missing cells come from which embryonic tissue?

  1. A. Endoderm
  2. B. Splanchnic mesoderm
  3. C. Surface ectoderm
  4. D. Notochord
  5. E. Neural crest
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Neural crest

Neural crest cells arise at the edges of the neural folds and migrate widely. They form the sensory (dorsal root) and autonomic ganglia, the enteric ganglia, Schwann cells, melanocytes, the adrenal medulla, the pia and arachnoid, odontoblasts, the aorticopulmonary septum of the heart, and most bones and connective tissue of the face. In Hirschsprung disease, neural crest cells fail to reach the distal colon, leaving an aganglionic segment that cannot relax. Other neural crest disorders include neuroblastoma and some congenital heart defects.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Endoderm: Endoderm forms the lining epithelium of the gut, not its nerve cells.
  • B. Splanchnic mesoderm: This forms the smooth muscle and connective tissue of the gut wall.
  • C. Surface ectoderm: This forms the epidermis and the lens.
  • D. Notochord: The notochord forms the nucleus pulposus.

Key point: Enteric ganglia come from the neural crest; their absence causes Hirschsprung disease.

Question 5 · Head and neck · easy

A 65-year-old man with long-standing poorly controlled diabetes has had double vision and a drooping right eyelid since yesterday, with an ache around the eye. His right eye looks down and out, and he cannot move it up, down or in. But the right pupil is normal in size and reacts to light. He has no headache of sudden onset.

Why is the pupil spared?

  1. A. The pupil fibres travel separately in the trochlear nerve
  2. B. The pupil fibres lie on the surface of the nerve
  3. C. The pupil is controlled by the sympathetic system
  4. D. Diabetes damages sensory nerves and spares motor ones
  5. E. The lesion is in the midbrain, at the nucleus
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. The pupil fibres lie on the surface of the nerve

The parasympathetic fibres to the pupil run on the outer surface of the oculomotor nerve, which is supplied by small vessels from the surface. In diabetic (microvascular) third nerve palsy, ischaemia affects the centre of the nerve, so the eye movements and eyelid are affected but the pupil is usually spared. In contrast, an aneurysm compresses the nerve from outside and affects the superficial pupil fibres early, giving a dilated pupil. A pupil-involving third nerve palsy needs urgent imaging.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. The pupil fibres travel separately in the trochlear nerve: The pupil fibres travel in the oculomotor nerve.
  • C. The pupil is controlled by the sympathetic system: The pupil constrictor is parasympathetic, through the oculomotor nerve.
  • D. Diabetes damages sensory nerves and spares motor ones: Diabetes can affect motor and cranial nerves.
  • E. The lesion is in the midbrain, at the nucleus: A midbrain lesion would usually affect the pupil too.

Key point: Diabetic third nerve palsy usually spares the pupil because the pupil fibres run on the outside of the nerve.

Question 6 · Neuroanatomy · easy

A 50-year-old man has a meningioma pressing on the left side of his spinal cord at the T6 segment. He has weakness and brisk reflexes in his left leg, with loss of vibration and position sense in the left foot. Pinprick and temperature are lost on the right side of his body, below a level on his trunk.

Where on the right side does the loss of pinprick usually begin?

  1. A. Two or three segments above the lesion
  2. B. At T6 itself
  3. C. At T10, the level of the umbilicus
  4. D. In the sacral segments
  5. E. One or two segments below the lesion
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. One or two segments below the lesion

Pain and temperature fibres enter the dorsal horn, may run up or down a segment in the tract of Lissauer, synapse, and then cross in the anterior white commissure while rising one or two segments. They then ascend in the opposite lateral spinothalamic tract. A lesion of one side of the cord therefore causes loss of pain and temperature on the other side, starting one or two segments below the lesion. The sensory level found on examination is a useful guide but the true lesion may be higher.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Two or three segments above the lesion: Pain fibres cross upwards, so the level is lower, not higher.
  • B. At T6 itself: The fibres take one or two segments to cross.
  • C. At T10, the level of the umbilicus: The level depends on the lesion; it is not fixed.
  • D. In the sacral segments: Pain loss is not limited to the sacral area here.

Key point: Spinothalamic fibres cross over one or two segments, so the contralateral sensory level is slightly below the lesion.

Question 7 · General anatomy, embryology and histology · easy

A student is listing tissues that come from endoderm.

Which of these is derived from endoderm?

  1. A. Follicular cells of the thyroid gland
  2. B. Anterior pituitary
  3. C. Spleen
  4. D. Adrenal cortex
  5. E. Parafollicular (C) cells of the thyroid
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Follicular cells of the thyroid gland

The thyroid gland begins as a thickening of endoderm in the floor of the pharynx at the foramen caecum, which descends as the thyroglossal duct; its follicular cells are endodermal. The parafollicular C cells (which make calcitonin) come from neural crest. Endoderm also forms the epithelium of the gut and its glands (liver, pancreas), the airways and lungs, the bladder and urethra, the tympanic cavity and auditory tube, the thymus and the parathyroids. The anterior pituitary comes from Rathke pouch (ectoderm) and the posterior pituitary from neuroectoderm.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Anterior pituitary: This comes from Rathke pouch, an ectodermal outgrowth of the roof of the mouth.
  • C. Spleen: The spleen comes from mesoderm.
  • D. Adrenal cortex: The adrenal cortex comes from mesoderm.
  • E. Parafollicular (C) cells of the thyroid: C cells come from neural crest by way of the ultimopharyngeal body.

Key point: Thyroid follicular cells are endodermal; C cells come from neural crest and the anterior pituitary from ectoderm.

Question 8 · Head and neck · easy

A 26-year-old overweight woman with daily headaches, worse lying down, and swollen optic discs from idiopathic intracranial hypertension develops double vision on looking to the left. Her left eye cannot move outwards past the midline. MRI shows no mass, and the lumbar puncture opening pressure is 38 cm of water.

Why is the abducens nerve affected by raised intracranial pressure?

  1. A. It is the thinnest of all the cranial nerves
  2. B. It has a long course and bends over the petrous apex
  3. C. It lies inside the optic canal with the optic nerve
  4. D. It has no myelin
  5. E. It is supplied by the posterior communicating artery
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. It has a long course and bends over the petrous apex

The abducens nerve (VI) leaves the brainstem at the junction of the pons and medulla, climbs a long way up the clivus, and bends sharply over the petrous apex of the temporal bone to enter the cavernous sinus. When intracranial pressure rises, the brainstem is pushed downwards and the nerve is stretched at this bend. This causes lateral rectus weakness, often on both sides, even though the cause is not near the nerve: a "false localising sign". It is also the first nerve affected in cavernous sinus disease.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. It is the thinnest of all the cranial nerves: The trochlear nerve is thinner.
  • C. It lies inside the optic canal with the optic nerve: The optic nerve lies in the optic canal.
  • D. It has no myelin: The abducens nerve is myelinated.
  • E. It is supplied by the posterior communicating artery: The abducens nerve is not supplied by the posterior communicating artery; it is stretched over the petrous apex by raised pressure.

Key point: The abducens nerve has a long intracranial course and is a false localising sign of raised intracranial pressure.

Question 9 · Neuroanatomy · easy

A 45-year-old woman has had neck pain for a month, spreading down the outer arm and forearm to the thumb, worse when she looks up. She has a weak biceps and wrist extension, and a reduced biceps and brachioradialis reflex. The triceps reflex is normal. An MRI shows a prolapsed disc compressing a root.

Which disc has most likely prolapsed?

  1. A. C4-5
  2. B. C5-6
  3. C. C6-7
  4. D. C7-T1
  5. E. T1-2
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. C5-6

In the neck, each nerve root leaves the canal above the vertebra of the same number (C1 to C7), and C8 leaves between C7 and T1. A posterolateral disc prolapse presses on the root leaving at that level, so a C5-6 disc compresses the C6 root. C6 supplies the skin of the thumb and the lateral forearm, helps biceps and brachioradialis, and carries their reflexes. In the lumbar spine the pattern is different: a posterolateral L4-5 disc usually compresses the L5 root, which crosses the disc to leave lower down.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. C4-5: This would compress C5, causing shoulder weakness.
  • C. C6-7: This would compress C7, weakening triceps.
  • D. C7-T1: This would compress C8 and the small hand muscles.
  • E. T1-2: This would affect T2 and give no arm weakness.

Key point: A C5-6 disc prolapse compresses the C6 root: thumb numbness, weak biceps, reduced biceps and supinator reflexes.

Question 10 · General anatomy, embryology and histology · easy

A child is born with a wedge-shaped half vertebra causing scoliosis.

Which part of the somite forms the vertebrae?

  1. A. Dermatome
  2. B. Myotome
  3. C. Sclerotome
  4. D. Intermediate mesoderm
  5. E. Notochord
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Sclerotome

Somites form from paraxial mesoderm on either side of the neural tube from the third week. Each divides into a sclerotome (ventromedial), which migrates around the notochord and neural tube to form the vertebrae and ribs, and a dermomyotome, which forms the dermatome (dermis of the back) and the myotome (skeletal muscle). Each vertebra forms from the lower half of one sclerotome and the upper half of the next, so spinal nerves come to lie between vertebrae. Failure of one half of a vertebra to form gives a hemivertebra and congenital scoliosis.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Dermatome: The dermatome forms the dermis of the back.
  • B. Myotome: The myotome forms skeletal muscle.
  • D. Intermediate mesoderm: This is not part of the somite.
  • E. Notochord: The notochord induces the vertebrae but forms only the nucleus pulposus.

Key point: Vertebrae and ribs come from the sclerotome of the somites; the myotome forms skeletal muscle.
