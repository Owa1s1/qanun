Oral and Maxillofacial Surgery MCQs for BDS

Question 1 · Surgery for jaw pathology, TMJ, deformity and clefts · easy

A 54-year-old man from Bannu attends a general dental practice with a painless ulcerated mass in his lower left buccal vestibule opposite the mandibular left first premolar (34) and second premolar (35) that has progressively enlarged over the past five weeks. He has placed a pinch of naswar in that exact site for 25 years. Intraoral examination reveals a 2.5 cm exophytic ulcer with rolled, everted, indurated margins and a granular floor fixed to the buccinator muscle, accompanied by a firm, non-tender 2 cm left submandibular lymph node.

What is the most appropriate immediate management by the general dental practitioner?

  1. A. A 14-day course of amoxicillin and metronidazole, then review
  2. B. Extract 34 and 35 and watch the socket heal
  3. C. Urgent referral on the suspected-cancer pathway
  4. D. A shave biopsy of the central slough in the surgery
  5. E. Six weeks of topical triamcinolone before deciding
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Urgent referral on the suspected-cancer pathway

Oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) is one of the most common malignancies in Pakistan, strongly driven by regional habits including naswar (widely kept in the labial or buccal sulcus in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa), gutka, paan with betel quid and tobacco, and smoking. According to international and UK NICE/SDCEP guidelines, any unexplained ulceration, red or mixed red-and-white patch (erythroleukoplakia), or indurated lump in the oral cavity persisting for more than 3 weeks requires an urgent suspected cancer referral (two-week-wait pathway) to an oral and maxillofacial surgeon or head and neck oncology unit. Classic malignant features include painless progression, rolled and everted borders, base induration (woody hardness on bimanual palpation from infiltrating tumour cords), fixation to deeper tissues, and firm, non-tender ipsilateral cervical lymphadenopathy. Dentists must never extract teeth in the tumour bed or delay referral with empirical antibiotics or steroids.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. A 14-day course of amoxicillin and metronidazole, then review: Empiric antibiotic therapy has no role in a non-infectious indurated ulcer with rolled margins and delays cancer diagnosis and staging by a full month.
  • B. Extract 34 and 35 and watch the socket heal: Extracting teeth adjacent to a malignant gingival or sulcular lesion spreads tumour cells into the medullary bone of the mandible and causes malignant socket fungation.
  • D. A shave biopsy of the central slough in the surgery: Sampling only the central necrotic slough yields non-diagnostic fibrinous exudate without showing basement membrane invasion by malignant epithelial cells at the margin.
  • E. Six weeks of topical triamcinolone before deciding: Prolonged topical corticosteroid application fails to treat squamous cell carcinoma and introduces an unacceptable six-week delay in oncological workup.

Key point: Recognise the clinical red flags of oral squamous cell carcinoma associated with naswar use and initiate urgent specialist referral for any oral ulcer lasting over 3 weeks.

Question 2 · Maxillofacial trauma · medium

A 26-year-old man attends a dental OPD in Bannu after a road traffic accident. He has swelling over the right mandibular angle, trismus, and malocclusion. An OPG shows a displaced fracture of the right mandibular angle. A partially erupted, horizontally impacted lower right third molar (48) lies directly in the line of fracture. The crown of 48 is grossly carious and unrestorable.

What is the most appropriate management of tooth 48?

  1. A. Extract 48 before fracture reduction
  2. B. Retain 48 and perform closed reduction only
  3. C. Retain 48 and perform open reduction with miniplates
  4. D. Perform root canal treatment on 48 before fixation
  5. E. Leave 48; it will erupt spontaneously after fixation
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Extract 48 before fracture reduction

Teeth in the line of a mandibular fracture that are grossly carious or unrestorable should be extracted at the time of fracture treatment because they can serve as a nidus for infection and compromise healing. Sound teeth may be retained if they do not interfere with reduction. In a young adult with an impacted, carious third molar at the angle fracture site, extraction before or during open reduction and internal fixation is appropriate. This prevents secondary infection and non-union.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Retain 48 and perform closed reduction only: Wrong: leaving an unrestorable carious tooth in the fracture line risks persistent infection and osteomyelitis.
  • C. Retain 48 and perform open reduction with miniplates: Wrong: placing hardware around an unrestorable carious tooth does not remove the infection risk from the fracture site.
  • D. Perform root canal treatment on 48 before fixation: Wrong: root canal treatment does not eliminate the tooth as a focus of infection within a fracture line and delays definitive care.
  • E. Leave 48; it will erupt spontaneously after fixation: Wrong: an impacted, unrestorable third molar will not erupt and may compromise fracture healing.

Key point: Extract an unrestorable tooth lying in the line of a mandibular angle fracture.

Question 3 · Local anaesthesia, exodontia and minor oral surgery · easy

A 30-year-old woman attends a dental teaching hospital in Quetta for crown preparation of a vital upper left permanent first molar (26) and upper left permanent second molar (27). The clinician administers a left posterior superior alveolar nerve block at the height of the mucobuccal fold above 27, advancing a 27-gauge short needle upward, inward, and backward at 45 degrees to each plane to a depth of 16 mm. Preparation of 27 is painless, but cutting the mesial aspect of 26 causes sharp pulpal pain.

Which anatomical structure accounts for the persistent sensitivity in her upper left first molar?

  1. A. Greater palatine nerve fibres entering the apical foramen of the palatal root canal
  2. B. Nasopalatine nerve fibres reaching the molars
  3. C. Long buccal nerve fibres piercing the cortical bone of the zygomaticoalveolar crest
  4. D. The mesiobuccal root of 26, supplied by the middle superior alveolar nerve
  5. E. Anterior superior alveolar nerve supplying the distobuccal root of the first molar
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. The mesiobuccal root of 26, supplied by the middle superior alveolar nerve

The posterior superior alveolar (PSA) nerve block is inserted at the height of the mucobuccal fold above the maxillary second molar and advanced 16 mm upward (45° to the occlusal plane), inward (45° medially toward the midline), and backward (45° to the long axis of the second molar) around the curvature of the maxillary tuberosity. It reliably anaesthetises the maxillary third and second molars and the palatal and distobuccal roots of the maxillary first molar, along with the overlying buccal periodontal tissues. However, in roughly 28% of patients, the mesiobuccal root of the maxillary first molar (16 or 26) is innervated by the middle superior alveolar (MSA) nerve. Therefore, a supraperiosteal infiltration over the apex of the second premolar or mesiobuccal root of the first molar is needed to achieve complete pulpal anaesthesia of 16 or 26.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Greater palatine nerve fibres entering the apical foramen of the palatal root canal: Although the greater palatine nerve supplies the palatal gingiva and mucoperiosteum, pulpal sensory innervation of the palatal root of 26 comes from the posterior superior alveolar plexus.
  • B. Nasopalatine nerve fibres reaching the molars: The nasopalatine nerve innervates only the anterior palatal mucoperiosteum from canine to canine and does not supply molar dental pulps.
  • C. Long buccal nerve fibres piercing the cortical bone of the zygomaticoalveolar crest: The long buccal nerve supplies buccal soft tissues of the cheek and lower molars, playing no role in pulpal innervation of maxillary molars.
  • E. Anterior superior alveolar nerve supplying the distobuccal root of the first molar: The anterior superior alveolar nerve innervates the maxillary central and lateral incisors and canine, not the posterior roots of the first molar.

Key point: Explain why the mesiobuccal root of the maxillary first molar frequently requires supplemental infiltration after a posterior superior alveolar nerve block.

Question 4 · Infections, medically compromised patients, sedation and forensic dentistry · easy

A healthy 26-year-old woman attends a dental clinic in Kohat with a 2-day history of localised swelling in the upper left buccal sulcus opposite a non-vital, restorable upper left second premolar (25). Her temperature is 36.8 °C (36.5-37.5), pulse is 74/min (60-100), and she has no facial cellulitis, trismus, or regional lymphadenopathy. Intraoral examination shows a 1.5 cm fluctuant, tender mucosal swelling confined to the attached gingiva and buccal vestibule over the apex of 25.

What is the most appropriate management under antimicrobial stewardship guidance?

  1. A. Oral amoxicillin 500 mg three times daily for 5 days without surgical drainage
  2. B. Incision and drainage with root canal access, without antibiotics
  3. C. Intravenous co-amoxiclav 1.2 g and hospital admission for inpatient observation
  4. D. Cold compresses, ibuprofen and review in a week
  5. E. Oral metronidazole 400 mg and amoxicillin 500 mg before any local intervention
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Incision and drainage with root canal access, without antibiotics

The fundamental surgical principle in managing any odontogenic infection is rapid elimination of the source of infection and evacuation of accumulated pus. According to Scottish Dental Clinical Effectiveness Programme (SDCEP) and international oral surgery guidelines, a well-localised fluctuant dentoalveolar or vestibular abscess in a healthy, immunocompetent patient with no signs of spreading infection (such as facial cellulitis, trismus, dysphagia) and no systemic toxicity (normal temperature and pulse) does not require systemic antibiotics. Definitive treatment consists of establishing drainage—either through the root canal by pulp extirpation or by intraoral incision and drainage at the point of dependent fluctuation—or extracting the tooth if unrestorable. Antibiotics are added only when there is systemic involvement, diffuse spreading cellulitis, or immunocompromise.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Oral amoxicillin 500 mg three times daily for 5 days without surgical drainage: Prescribing systemic antibiotics without establishing surgical drainage fails to penetrate the avascular abscess cavity and violates antimicrobial stewardship in the absence of systemic spread.
  • C. Intravenous co-amoxiclav 1.2 g and hospital admission for inpatient observation: Intravenous therapy and inpatient admission are reserved for patients with airway compromise, trismus, systemic sepsis, or deep fascial space involvement.
  • D. Cold compresses, ibuprofen and review in a week: Delaying drainage of a fluctuant collection leaves purulent exudate under pressure, risking bone destruction and progression into adjacent fascial spaces.
  • E. Oral metronidazole 400 mg and amoxicillin 500 mg before any local intervention: Pre-treating a localised fluctuant intraoral swelling with dual antibiotics before draining the pus delays definitive source control and promotes antimicrobial resistance.

Key point: Apply antimicrobial stewardship principles by treating a localised fluctuant vestibular abscess with surgical drainage and source control without systemic antibiotics.

Question 5 · Surgery for jaw pathology, TMJ, deformity and clefts · easy

A 46-year-old woman from Swat undergoes surgical resection of a T2 N0 M0 squamous cell carcinoma of the left anterolateral tongue opposite the mandibular left canine (33) and first premolar (34) at a teaching hospital in Peshawar. Although ultrasound and contrast-enhanced computed tomography of the neck show no enlarged cervical lymph nodes, the depth of invasion of the primary tongue tumour is 6 mm, giving an occult micrometastasis risk exceeding 20%. The surgeon performs an elective selective supraomohyoid neck dissection on the left side while preserving the sternocleidomastoid muscle, internal jugular vein, and spinal accessory nerve.

Which nodal levels does a supraomohyoid neck dissection remove?

  1. A. Levels I, II and III, above where the omohyoid crosses the internal jugular vein
  2. B. Levels II, III, and IV only, sparing the submental and submandibular triangles
  3. C. Levels I to V together with sacrifice of the internal jugular vein, sternocleidomastoid, and accessory nerve
  4. D. Levels IV, V, and VI including the pretracheal and paratracheal anterior thyroid compartment
  5. E. Level Ia (submental triangle) and bilateral superficial parotid preauricular lymph nodes
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Levels I, II and III, above where the omohyoid crosses the internal jugular vein

Cervical lymph node metastasis is the most powerful adverse prognostic factor in oral squamous cell carcinoma, reducing 5-year survival by approximately 50%. Even when the neck is clinically and radiologically node-negative (cN0), primary oral cavity tumours—particularly of the tongue and floor of the mouth—with a depth of invasion (DOI) of >=4 mm carry an occult nodal metastasis rate >20% and require an elective selective neck dissection. Lymphatic drainage from the oral cavity follows a predictable pattern into the submental (Level Ia) and submandibular (Level Ib) triangles, the upper jugular chain around the spinal accessory nerve (Level IIa and IIb, from skull base to hyoid bone), and the middle jugular chain (Level III, from hyoid bone to where the superior omohyoid crosses the internal jugular vein). Removing Levels I, II, and III constitutes a supraomohyoid neck dissection.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Levels II, III, and IV only, sparing the submental and submandibular triangles: Dissecting Levels II to IV (lateral neck dissection) is used for laryngeal or hypopharyngeal cancers and leaves behind Level I, which is a primary drainage echelon for the oral cavity.
  • C. Levels I to V together with sacrifice of the internal jugular vein, sternocleidomastoid, and accessory nerve: Removal of all five levels along with all three non-lymphatic structures is a classic radical neck dissection, which is never indicated for a clinically node-negative (N0) neck.
  • D. Levels IV, V, and VI including the pretracheal and paratracheal anterior thyroid compartment: Central compartment (Level VI) and lower posterior triangle (Level V) dissections are indicated for differentiated thyroid carcinoma rather than lateral oral tongue squamous cell carcinoma.
  • E. Level Ia (submental triangle) and bilateral superficial parotid preauricular lymph nodes: Oral tongue carcinomas frequently skip Level Ia to seed Levels Ib, IIa, and III directly, and do not drain primarily to the parotid preauricular nodes.

Key point: Identify cervical lymph node Levels I, II, and III and their anatomical boundaries in a supraomohyoid selective neck dissection for cN0 oral cancer.

Question 6 · Maxillofacial trauma · easy

A 25-year-old motorcyclist without a helmet is brought to the emergency department of a teaching hospital in Peshawar 30 minutes after a high-speed collision. He is supine on a spinal board with gurgling, noisy breathing, profuse bleeding into the mouth, avulsed maxillary central incisors (11 and 21), and mobile midface and mandibular fractures. His oxygen saturation is 88% on room air (normal 95-100%), pulse is 118/min, and blood pressure is 110/70 mmHg.

Under ATLS principles, what is the first action?

  1. A. Clear the oropharynx with rigid suction and jaw thrust with cervical spine immobilisation
  2. B. Obtain an urgent occipitomental and panoramic radiograph in the radiology suite
  3. C. Perform a head-tilt and hyperextension of the neck to open the pharyngeal airway
  4. D. Suture the facial lacerations and wire the mobile mandibular fragments in the resuscitation bay
  5. E. Insert two wide-bore intravenous cannulae and infuse 2 litres of warmed Ringer lactate first
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Clear the oropharynx with rigid suction and jaw thrust with cervical spine immobilisation

Under the Advanced Trauma Life Support (ATLS 10th edition) protocol, the primary survey follows the strict ABCDE sequence: Airway maintenance with restriction of cervical spine motion, Breathing and ventilation, Circulation with haemorrhage control, Disability (neurological status), and Exposure/Environmental control. In maxillofacial trauma, acute upper airway obstruction is the most rapid preventable cause of death and frequently results from blood clots, vomitus, avulsed teeth (such as 11 and 21), broken denture fragments, or posterior prolapse of the tongue in a supine patient. The immediate priority is to inspect and clear the oral cavity and oropharynx using a wide-bore rigid Yankauer suction catheter and Magill forceps, followed by a chin-lift or jaw-thrust manoeuvre while an assistant maintains manual in-line immobilisation of the cervical spine. Head-tilt with neck extension is strictly contraindicated until cervical spine injury is excluded.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Obtain an urgent occipitomental and panoramic radiograph in the radiology suite: Transferring an hypoxic patient with an obstructed airway for plain facial radiographs before completing the primary survey can be fatal.
  • C. Perform a head-tilt and hyperextension of the neck to open the pharyngeal airway: Hyperextending the cervical spine in a high-speed road-traffic trauma patient risks converting an unstable cervical fracture into complete spinal cord transection.
  • D. Suture the facial lacerations and wire the mobile mandibular fragments in the resuscitation bay: Definitive soft-tissue repair and fracture fixation belong to the secondary phase after life-threatening airway, breathing, and circulatory threats are controlled.
  • E. Insert two wide-bore intravenous cannulae and infuse 2 litres of warmed Ringer lactate first: Circulatory resuscitation (C) follows airway maintenance with cervical spine restriction (A) and breathing assessment (B) in the ATLS primary survey.

Key point: Apply the ATLS primary survey priority of airway clearance and jaw thrust with manual in-line cervical spine immobilisation in acute maxillofacial trauma.

Question 7 · Local anaesthesia, exodontia and minor oral surgery · easy

A 45-year-old man attends a dental teaching hospital in Muzaffarabad requiring extraction of three periodontally mobile teeth: the upper right permanent first premolar (14), upper right permanent second premolar (15), and upper right permanent first molar (16). Instead of giving three painful palatal infiltrations, the operator decides to administer a single right greater palatine nerve block using pressure anaesthesia with a cotton-tipped applicator over the target foramen.

Where is the anatomical depression for the greater palatine foramen located in most adults?

  1. A. Midline of the hard palate immediately posterior to the maxillary central incisors
  2. B. Palatal to the maxillary canine halfway toward the median palatine raphe
  3. C. Posterior to the hard-soft palate junction medial to the pterygoid hamulus
  4. D. Within the palatal interdental papilla between the first and second premolars
  5. E. Medial to the upper second molar, at the junction of the palate and alveolus
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Medial to the upper second molar, at the junction of the palate and alveolus

The greater palatine nerve exits the greater palatine foramen and runs anteriorly in a groove between the vertical maxillary alveolar process and the horizontal palatine process to supply the palatal mucoperiosteum and hard palate bone from the third molar anteriorly as far as the first premolar. In most adults, the greater palatine foramen is located approximately 1 cm medial to the palatal gingival margin adjacent to the distal half of the maxillary second molar (17 or 27) or between the second and third molars. Approaching from the opposite side of the mouth at a right angle to the palatal bone, the clinician applies firm pressure with a cotton swab over the foramen until tissues blanch, inserts the needle just anterior to the foramen to a depth under 10 mm, and slowly deposits 0.45 to 0.6 mL.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Midline of the hard palate immediately posterior to the maxillary central incisors: The midline depression just behind the central incisors is the incisive foramen, which transmits the nasopalatine nerves supplying the premaxilla from canine to canine.
  • B. Palatal to the maxillary canine halfway toward the median palatine raphe: The canine palatal region is the junction zone where terminal fibres of the nasopalatine and greater palatine nerves overlap, not the location of the foramen.
  • C. Posterior to the hard-soft palate junction medial to the pterygoid hamulus: The lesser palatine foramen lies posterior to the greater palatine foramen in the pyramidal process of the palatine bone; injecting into the soft palate causes gagging and dysphagia.
  • D. Within the palatal interdental papilla between the first and second premolars: No palatine foramen opens between the premolars; injecting there only produces localized papillary blanching rather than a regional posterior palatal block.

Key point: Locate the greater palatine foramen and define the distribution of the greater palatine nerve block.

Question 8 · Infections, medically compromised patients, sedation and forensic dentistry · easy

An 11-year-old boy is brought to a dental clinic in D.I. Khan with a 2-month history of a painless, bony-hard swelling along the inferior and buccal aspect of the left body of the mandible. He has no fever, no trismus, and normal sensation in the left lower lip. Intraoral examination reveals a grossly carious, non-vital lower left first molar (36). A lower standard occlusal radiograph demonstrates concentric, laminated 'onion-skin' layers of subperiosteal new bone deposited parallel to the buccal cortex opposite 36.

What is the most appropriate treatment for this condition?

  1. A. Surgical decortication and immediate recontouring of the buccal cortex
  2. B. Extraction or root canal treatment of the offending lower left first molar
  3. C. Six weeks of intravenous benzylpenicillin and metronidazole without extraction
  4. D. En-bloc marginal resection of the left mandibular body with 1 cm margins
  5. E. Hyperbaric oxygen therapy for 30 dives followed by sequestrectomy
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Extraction or root canal treatment of the offending lower left first molar

Garrè's chronic non-suppurative sclerosing osteomyelitis, more accurately termed proliferative periostitis, occurs predominantly in children and young adolescents whose periosteum possesses high osteogenic potential. When a low-grade chronic periapical infection—most commonly from a carious mandibular first molar (36 or 46)—penetrates the thin cortex and mildly irritates the highly active periosteum without causing acute suppuration, the periosteum lifts and deposits successive parallel layers of reactive new bone. On an occlusal or panoramic radiograph, these alternating radiopaque and radiolucent laminations produce the pathognomonic 'onion-skin' appearance. Because there is no necrotic sequestrum or systemic sepsis, neither aggressive surgical recontouring nor prolonged systemic antibiotic therapy is required. Simply removing the chronic inflammatory stimulus by extracting the unrestorable 36 (or performing root canal therapy if restorable) allows osteoclasts to remodel the facial contour over 6 to 12 months.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Surgical decortication and immediate recontouring of the buccal cortex: Early surgical chiselling of the reactive periosteal bone in a child is unnecessary because the mandible naturally remodels over 6 to 12 months once the source is removed.
  • C. Six weeks of intravenous benzylpenicillin and metronidazole without extraction: Proliferative periostitis is a chronic non-suppurative reaction to low-grade dental infection; prolonged intravenous antibiotics without treating tooth 36 fail to resolve the stimulus.
  • D. En-bloc marginal resection of the left mandibular body with 1 cm margins: Radical bony resection is reserved for aggressive neoplasms such as osteosarcoma or ameloblastoma, whereas this lesion is a benign reactive periostitis.
  • E. Hyperbaric oxygen therapy for 30 dives followed by sequestrectomy: Hyperbaric oxygen and sequestrectomy apply to irradiated or necrotic suppurative bone, whereas this child has vital, reactive subperiosteal bone formation.

Key point: Recognize that proliferative periostitis (Garrè's osteomyelitis) in children is managed by extracting or endodontically treating the causal tooth, allowing spontaneous bony remodelling.

Question 9 · Surgery for jaw pathology, TMJ, deformity and clefts · easy

A 29-year-old teacher presents to a dental hospital in Rawalpindi with a painless buccal swelling over the anterior right maxilla. The maxillary right lateral incisor (12) is darkened and non-vital on electric pulp testing, whereas the maxillary right central incisor (11) and canine (13) are vital. A periapical radiograph shows a well-defined 1.8 cm unilocular radiolucency with a corticated margin around the apex of 12. After orthograde root canal therapy of 12, the surgeon raises a mucoperiosteal flap, shells out the entire cystic sac intact, performs a 3 mm apicectomy with retrograde filling, and sutures the flap closed.

What is this procedure, and what is its main advantage over marsupialisation?

  1. A. Partsch I (marsupialisation); it requires daily home irrigation with an acrylic obturator plug for 9 months
  2. B. Carnoy solution chemical cauterisation; it devitalises the adjacent roots of 11 and 13 intentionally to prevent recurrence
  3. C. Marginal block osseous resection; it removes the crowns and roots of 11, 12, and 13 in one segment
  4. D. Tube decompression; it leaves the entire epithelial lining permanently inside the anterior maxilla without histology
  5. E. Partsch II (enucleation); it provides the entire cyst lining for histopathology with primary healing
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Partsch II (enucleation); it provides the entire cyst lining for histopathology with primary healing

Enucleation (the Partsch II operation) is the surgical shelling out of an entire cystic lesion cleanly from its surrounding bony cavity along the cleavage plane between the fibrous cyst capsule and the osseous wall, allowing the bone defect to fill with a blood clot that organises into new bone under a primarily closed mucoperiosteal flap. It is the treatment of choice for most small-to-moderate odontogenic cysts—such as this inflammatory radicular cyst on a non-vital maxillary lateral incisor (12)—where removal does not endanger adjacent vital structures or risk jaw fracture. The two major advantages of primary enucleation over marsupialisation (Partsch I) are that the entire cystic lining is submitted for histopathological examination (ensuring that an odontogenic keratocyst, unicystic ameloblastoma, or mural carcinoma is not missed), and primary closure spares the patient months of open cavity irrigation.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Partsch I (marsupialisation); it requires daily home irrigation with an acrylic obturator plug for 9 months: Partsch I is marsupialisation (deroofing the cyst into an open pouch), whereas shelling out the entire cyst wall with primary mucoperiosteal closure is Partsch II.
  • B. Carnoy solution chemical cauterisation; it devitalises the adjacent roots of 11 and 13 intentionally to prevent recurrence: Chemical fixation with Carnoy solution is an adjunct historically used for aggressive odontogenic keratocysts rather than a 1.8 cm inflammatory radicular cyst.
  • C. Marginal block osseous resection; it removes the crowns and roots of 11, 12, and 13 in one segment: Block osseous resection is reserved for locally aggressive odontogenic tumours such as ameloblastoma and is destructive overkill for a benign radicular cyst.
  • D. Tube decompression; it leaves the entire epithelial lining permanently inside the anterior maxilla without histology: Tube decompression drains intraluminal fluid to shrink large lesions over months before secondary enucleation and does not remove the cyst capsule immediately.

Key point: Define cyst enucleation (Partsch II) and explain its diagnostic and healing advantages over marsupialisation.

Question 10 · Maxillofacial trauma · easy

A 28-year-old cricketer presents to a dental teaching hospital in Peshawar after being struck on the left cheek by a fast ball. Examination reveals flattening of the left malar prominence, left subconjunctival haemorrhage with no visible posterior border, a palpable step at the left infraorbital margin, and numbness of the left upper lip and maxillary left canine and premolar gingiva (23, 24, and 25). Computed tomography is temporarily unavailable, and the surgeon requests a plain extraoral radiograph.

Which plain view best shows the midface buttresses and sinuses?

  1. A. Lateral skull cephalometric projection
  2. B. Reverse Towne (posteroanterior 30-degree) projection
  3. C. Occipitomental (Waters) projection at 10 and 30 degrees
  4. D. Mandibular true lower 90-degree occlusal radiograph
  5. E. Transcranial temporomandibular joint projection
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Occipitomental (Waters) projection at 10 and 30 degrees

The occipitomental (Waters) radiograph—typically taken at 10° and 30° (or with the radiographic baseline at 45° to the receptor)—is the single most informative plain radiograph for assessing fractures of the middle third of the facial skeleton, particularly the zygomaticomaxillary complex (ZMC), orbital rims, nasal bones, and maxillary sinuses. By tilting the patient's chin upward against the vertical detector so that the orbitomeatal line forms a 45° angle with the film, the dense radiopaque petrous ridges of the temporal bone are projected inferiorly below the floors of the maxillary sinuses. Clinicians systematically trace Campbell's five curvilinear lines (or McGregor's three lines) from superior to inferior across both sides to identify cortical discontinuities at the zygomaticofrontal suture, infraorbital rim, lateral antral wall, and zygomatic arch, as well as fluid levels (haemosinus) or the 'hanging drop' sign of orbital floor blow-out.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Lateral skull cephalometric projection: A lateral skull view superimposes the left and right zygomatic complexes upon each other, obscuring unilateral malar displacement.
  • B. Reverse Towne (posteroanterior 30-degree) projection: The reverse Towne projection is designed to show the mandibular condylar necks rather than the infraorbital rims and zygomatic body.
  • D. Mandibular true lower 90-degree occlusal radiograph: An intraoral mandibular occlusal film demonstrates the mediolateral cortex of the mandibular body and floor of the mouth, not the midface.
  • E. Transcranial temporomandibular joint projection: Transcranial oblique views image only the lateral pole of the mandibular condyle and glenoid fossa rather than the zygomaticomaxillary buttresses.

Key point: Select the occipitomental (Waters) view as the primary plain radiograph for midface and zygomatic complex fractures and explain how it avoids petrous bone overlap.
