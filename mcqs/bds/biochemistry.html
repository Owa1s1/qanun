Question 1 · Enzymes, bioenergetics and free radicals · medium

In an experiment on isolated mitochondria, a drug stops oxygen use when NADH-linked substrates are given, but oxygen use resumes when succinate is added.

Where does the drug act?

  1. A. Complex IV
  2. B. Complex III
  3. C. ATP synthase
  4. D. Complex I
  5. E. Cytochrome c on the outer face
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. Complex I

Electrons from NADH enter at complex I; electrons from succinate enter at complex II (succinate dehydrogenase) and join at coenzyme Q. A complex I inhibitor (rotenone, a fish poison and pesticide; amobarbital; metformin weakly) blocks NADH oxidation but not succinate oxidation. Antimycin A blocks complex III, and cyanide, carbon monoxide, azide and hydrogen sulfide block complex IV; these stop oxygen use from both substrates. Oligomycin blocks ATP synthase. Such inhibitor experiments showed the order of the carriers in the chain.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Complex IV: Blocking complex IV stops oxygen use with any substrate, including succinate.
  • B. Complex III: Electrons from succinate also pass through complex III, so oxygen use would not resume.
  • C. ATP synthase: ATP synthase block slows oxygen use with all substrates in coupled mitochondria, including succinate.
  • E. Cytochrome c on the outer face: Cytochrome c carries electrons from both NADH and succinate, so blocking it stops all oxygen use.

Key point: Rotenone and amobarbital block complex I; succinate bypasses complex I through complex II.

Question 2 · Lipid metabolism and lipoproteins · medium

A 38-year-old man from Gilgit eats a fatty meal. His gallbladder releases bile into the duodenum. Bile acids emulsify fat and are then absorbed further down the small intestine before returning to the liver. This return pathway conserves bile acids for repeated use.

Where are most bile acids absorbed after acting in the intestine?

  1. A. In the duodenum
  2. B. In the jejunum
  3. C. In the colon
  4. D. In the ileum
  5. E. In the rectum
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. In the ileum

Bile acids are made from cholesterol in hepatocytes and secreted into the duodenum to emulsify dietary fat. After helping fat digestion, most bile acids are actively reabsorbed in the terminal ileum by a specific transporter. They enter the portal vein and return to the liver, where hepatocytes take them up again. This enterohepatic circulation means each bile acid molecule is reused many times. The duodenum and jejunum absorb only small amounts; the colon and rectum are not major sites. This matters because losing bile acids in the ileum forces the liver to use more cholesterol to make new ones.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. In the duodenum: The duodenum receives bile but absorbs only a little; most bile acid uptake happens much farther down.
  • B. In the jejunum: The jejunum absorbs nutrients and some bile acids, but the dedicated bile acid transporter is concentrated in the ileum.
  • C. In the colon: The colon absorbs water and salts and only a small fraction of remaining bile acids.
  • E. In the rectum: The rectum stores faeces and is not a site of significant bile acid absorption.

Key point: Identify the terminal ileum as the main site of bile acid reabsorption in enterohepatic circulation.

Question 3 · Cell and molecular biology, genetics · easy

A 35-year-old woman from Lahore has a malignant tumour in the breast whose cells make too many copies of a growth-factor receptor. The receptor signals continuous division even when its ligand is absent. A monoclonal antibody binds the receptor and blocks its action.

Which gene is amplified when excess growth-factor receptors drive breast cancer?

  1. A. RAS
  2. B. BCR-ABL
  3. C. BRCA1
  4. D. HER2 (ERBB2)
  5. E. TP53
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. HER2 (ERBB2)

HER2, also called ERBB2, codes for a receptor tyrosine kinase on the cell surface. When the HER2 gene is amplified, the cell displays too many receptors, which signal division even without the normal growth factor. This drives some breast cancers. A monoclonal antibody that binds HER2 can block these signals and slow tumour growth. BCR-ABL is a fusion gene in leukaemia, BRCA1 and p53 are tumour suppressors, and RAS is an intracellular signal carrier. In patients, testing HER2 status identifies cancers likely to respond to HER2-directed antibody therapy. This helps avoid giving ineffective treatment.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. RAS: RAS is an intracellular signalling protein, not the cell-surface receptor amplified here.
  • B. BCR-ABL: This fusion gene drives chronic myeloid leukaemia, not breast cancer.
  • C. BRCA1: BRCA1 is a tumour-suppressor gene involved in DNA repair, not a growth receptor.
  • E. TP53: P53 is a tumour-suppressor gene that stops damaged cells from dividing.

Key point: Name the gene amplified in HER2-positive breast cancer and explain how targeted antibody therapy works.

Question 4 · Nutrition, vitamins and minerals · medium

A baby girl who was well on breast milk develops, a few weeks after weaning at 6 months, a red, crusted rash around her mouth, the nappy area and her fingers. She also has diarrhoea, patchy hair loss and irritability, and is slow to gain weight. Her parents are cousins. Her plasma zinc is 30 µg/dL (normal 70-120).

What is the most likely cause and treatment?

  1. A. Candida infection; topical antifungal
  2. B. Inherited zinc malabsorption; oral zinc
  3. C. Eczema; topical steroids
  4. D. Biotin deficiency; biotin
  5. E. Niacin deficiency; nicotinamide
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Inherited zinc malabsorption; oral zinc

Zinc is needed by over 300 enzymes and for growth, immunity, taste, wound healing and the skin. In acrodermatitis enteropathica (autosomal recessive defect of the zinc transporter ZIP4), zinc absorption is poor; breast milk contains a binding factor that helps, so symptoms appear after weaning: a red, scaly, crusted rash around body openings and on the hands and feet, diarrhoea, alopecia, irritability and infections. Acquired zinc deficiency occurs with malnutrition, chronic diarrhoea, alcohol and long-term parenteral nutrition. Oral zinc corrects it rapidly.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Candida infection; topical antifungal: Candida causes a red rash with satellite spots in the nappy area but not hair loss, diarrhoea or low zinc.
  • C. Eczema; topical steroids: Eczema causes itchy patches on the face and skin creases, not a rash around body openings with low zinc.
  • D. Biotin deficiency; biotin: Biotin deficiency can cause a similar rash but does not lower plasma zinc.
  • E. Niacin deficiency; nicotinamide: Niacin deficiency (pellagra) affects sun-exposed skin with diarrhoea and dementia.

Key point: Acrodermatitis enteropathica (zinc malabsorption) causes periorificial and acral dermatitis, diarrhoea and alopecia after weaning.

Question 5 · Amino acids, haem and nucleotides · medium

A 3-day-old boy, born at term and fed breast milk, becomes lethargic, feeds poorly and breathes fast. His mother's brother died in the first week of life. He has no fever. Blood ammonia is 766 µg/dL (normal under 170), blood glucose is normal and there is no acidosis; the blood gas shows a respiratory alkalosis. Plasma citrulline is low. Urine orotic acid is very high.

Which enzyme is deficient?

  1. A. Carbamoyl phosphate synthetase I
  2. B. Ornithine transcarbamylase
  3. C. Arginase
  4. D. UMP synthase
  5. E. Propionyl-CoA carboxylase
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Ornithine transcarbamylase

Ornithine transcarbamylase deficiency is the commonest urea cycle disorder and is X-linked, so boys are often severely affected in the first days of life, while carrier girls may have milder episodes after protein loads or illness. Carbamoyl phosphate cannot enter the urea cycle; it builds up, leaves the mitochondria and enters pyrimidine synthesis, so orotic acid rises in the urine (unlike CPS I deficiency). Ammonia rises with respiratory alkalosis (ammonia stimulates breathing), lethargy, vomiting, seizures and coma. Treatment is stopping protein, glucose, ammonia scavengers, arginine and dialysis if needed.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Carbamoyl phosphate synthetase I: CPS I deficiency causes the same newborn hyperammonaemia, but carbamoyl phosphate is not made, so orotic acid stays normal.
  • C. Arginase: This causes spastic diplegia with high arginine, and rarely severe newborn hyperammonaemia.
  • D. UMP synthase: UMP synthase deficiency causes orotic aciduria with megaloblastic anaemia but normal ammonia.
  • E. Propionyl-CoA carboxylase: Propionic acidaemia causes ketoacidosis with a high anion gap; this baby has no acidosis and a respiratory alkalosis.

Key point: OTC deficiency (X-linked) causes newborn hyperammonaemia with high urinary orotic acid.

Question 6 · Carbohydrate metabolism · easy

The beta cells of the pancreas and the liver both need to take up glucose in proportion to the blood glucose level.

Which feature of GLUT2 suits this role?

  1. A. It has a high Km, so uptake rises steadily with blood glucose
  2. B. It has a very low Km, so it is saturated at fasting glucose
  3. C. It needs insulin to reach the membrane
  4. D. It carries glucose with sodium against a gradient
  5. E. It carries only fructose
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. It has a high Km, so uptake rises steadily with blood glucose

GLUT2 has a high Km for glucose, so it is never saturated at normal levels, and glucose moves in or out in proportion to the blood level. Together with glucokinase, it lets beta cells sense blood glucose and release insulin accordingly, and lets the liver take up glucose after meals and release it during fasting (it is bidirectional). GLUT2 also moves glucose out of the gut and kidney tubule cells into the blood. Mutations cause Fanconi-Bickel syndrome, with liver glycogen build-up, kidney tubule dysfunction and abnormal glucose levels.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. It has a very low Km, so it is saturated at fasting glucose: GLUT1 and GLUT3 have a low Km and stay saturated at fasting glucose, giving the brain and red cells a steady supply.
  • C. It needs insulin to reach the membrane: Only GLUT4, in muscle and fat, needs insulin to move to the membrane; GLUT2 sits on the surface all the time.
  • D. It carries glucose with sodium against a gradient: Sodium-coupled uptake against a gradient is done by SGLT1 in the gut and SGLT2 in the kidney, not by GLUT2.
  • E. It carries only fructose: GLUT5 is the intestinal fructose transporter; GLUT2 carries glucose, galactose and fructose.

Key point: GLUT2 has a high Km and is bidirectional; it lets beta cells and liver respond to blood glucose.

Question 7 · Chemistry of biomolecules · easy

A 10-day-old baby girl, breastfed, is jaundiced and vomiting after feeds, with a large liver and poor weight gain. She has developed cloudy lenses. Her blood culture grows E. coli. A urine sample turns Benedict reagent orange, but a glucose oxidase dipstick on the same urine is negative. Her blood glucose is 40 mg/dL.

What does this result suggest?

  1. A. Diabetes mellitus
  2. B. Sucrose in the urine
  3. C. A normal result in all newborns
  4. D. Ketones in the urine
  5. E. A non-glucose reducing sugar
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. A non-glucose reducing sugar

Benedict test detects any reducing substance: sugars with a free aldehyde or keto group reduce blue copper(II) to red copper(I) oxide. Glucose oxidase strips are specific for glucose. A positive Benedict test with a negative glucose strip means another reducing sugar is present, such as galactose (galactosaemia), fructose (fructose intolerance or fructosuria), lactose or pentoses. In a newborn with jaundice, vomiting and poor feeding, galactosaemia must be considered and milk stopped until it is ruled out.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Diabetes mellitus: Diabetes puts glucose in the urine, which turns the glucose oxidase strip positive; her strip is negative.
  • B. Sucrose in the urine: Sucrose is not a reducing sugar, so it would not turn Benedict reagent orange.
  • C. A normal result in all newborns: Reducing sugars in a newborn's urine are abnormal, especially with jaundice, vomiting and cataracts.
  • D. Ketones in the urine: Ketones are detected by the nitroprusside test, not by Benedict reagent.

Key point: A positive Benedict test with a negative glucose oxidase strip means a non-glucose reducing sugar, such as galactose.

Question 8 · Hormone action, metabolism in health and disease, clinical biochemistry · medium

A 26-year-old man from Chitral uses hydrocortisone cream for itchy skin. The hormone crosses the cell membrane and binds its receptor in the cytoplasm, and the heat shock proteins attached to the receptor are released.

Why does a hormone of this type take hours to act, while adrenaline acts within seconds?

  1. A. Because it is absorbed slowly through the skin
  2. B. Because it must first be converted into a second messenger in the cytoplasm
  3. C. It acts by changing gene transcription and protein synthesis
  4. D. Because it is bound to albumin and released slowly
  5. E. Because it acts only during sleep, when transcription is highest
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. It acts by changing gene transcription and protein synthesis

Hormones fall into two broad mechanistic classes. Water-soluble hormones, including the peptides and catecholamines, cannot cross the membrane and bind receptors on the cell surface: G protein-coupled receptors acting through cyclic AMP, inositol trisphosphate and calcium, or diacylglycerol; receptor tyrosine kinases, as for insulin; or receptors with intrinsic guanylyl cyclase activity, as for atrial natriuretic peptide. Their actions begin in seconds and stop quickly, and they are amplified by cascades of kinases. Lipid-soluble hormones, the glucocorticoids, mineralocorticoids, sex steroids, thyroid hormone, vitamin D and retinoids, enter the cell and bind receptors of the nuclear receptor family, which dimerise and bind response elements to activate or repress transcription. This explains the slow onset and long duration of steroid effects, the delay before thyroxine takes effect, and why a steroid given for an acute asthma attack takes several hours to help.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Because it is absorbed slowly through the skin: Absorption is not the rate-limiting step.
  • B. Because it must first be converted into a second messenger in the cytoplasm: Steroid receptors do not use second messengers.
  • D. Because it is bound to albumin and released slowly: Protein binding affects the free fraction, not the mechanism of action.
  • E. Because it acts only during sleep, when transcription is highest: Transcription is not confined to sleep.

Key point: Contrast the time course of nuclear receptor and membrane receptor hormone action.

Question 9 · Enzymes, bioenergetics and free radicals · easy

Two enzymes act on the same substrate. Enzyme A has a Km of 0.1 mM and enzyme B has a Km of 10 mM.

What does this tell you?

  1. A. Enzyme B is faster at all substrate levels
  2. B. Enzyme B has a higher affinity for the substrate
  3. C. Both enzymes have the same Vmax
  4. D. Enzyme A is inhibited by its substrate
  5. E. Enzyme A has the higher affinity
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Enzyme A has the higher affinity

In Michaelis-Menten kinetics, the reaction velocity rises with substrate concentration and then levels off at the maximum velocity (Vmax), when all the enzyme is busy. Km is the substrate concentration at which the velocity is half of Vmax. A low Km means the enzyme reaches half speed at a low substrate level, so it has a high affinity. Hexokinase (low Km for glucose) works well even when glucose is low, while glucokinase (high Km) works mainly when glucose is high after meals. Km is a constant for an enzyme and its substrate and does not depend on how much enzyme is present.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Enzyme B is faster at all substrate levels: Km describes affinity, not speed; speed at saturation is Vmax, which is not given.
  • B. Enzyme B has a higher affinity for the substrate: A higher Km means more substrate is needed for half Vmax, so enzyme B has the lower affinity.
  • C. Both enzymes have the same Vmax: Km and Vmax are independent; two enzymes with different Km can have any Vmax.
  • D. Enzyme A is inhibited by its substrate: Substrate inhibition shows as a falling rate at high substrate levels, which Km values cannot show.

Key point: Km is the substrate concentration at half Vmax; low Km means high affinity.

Question 10 · Lipid metabolism and lipoproteins · medium

A 4-year-old boy from Skardu, Gilgit-Baltistan, has a distended abdomen and poor growth. His liver is enlarged and his spleen is palpable 6 cm below the costal margin. A bone marrow aspirate shows large foamy macrophages with a soap-bubble appearance. Chest X-ray shows fine shadowing in both lungs.

What would a bone marrow or splenic aspirate show, and which form of the disease does this child have?

  1. A. Macrophages with a wrinkled, tissue-paper cytoplasm, indicating the adult form
  2. B. Foam cells and sea-blue histiocytes; the infantile type A
  3. C. Ring sideroblasts, indicating iron loading of the marrow
  4. D. Hypersegmented neutrophils, indicating a megaloblastic marrow
  5. E. Reed-Sternberg cells, indicating a lymphoma
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Foam cells and sea-blue histiocytes; the infantile type A

Acid sphingomyelinase deficiency causes Niemann-Pick disease types A and B. Sphingomyelin, derived largely from cell membranes, accumulates in macrophages, so the liver and spleen enlarge greatly and the marrow and lungs are infiltrated, giving foam cells and sea-blue histiocytes. Type A appears in the first months with failure to thrive, massive hepatosplenomegaly, loss of milestones and a cherry-red macula in about half, and is fatal by three years; type B has little or no neurological disease and presents later with organomegaly, thrombocytopenia and progressive interstitial lung disease, with survival into adulthood. Type C is a different disease, caused by defective intracellular cholesterol transport through NPC1 or NPC2, and is recognised by vertical supranuclear gaze palsy and ataxia. Enzyme replacement is now available for the non-neurological disease.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Macrophages with a wrinkled, tissue-paper cytoplasm, indicating the adult form: Wrinkled, tissue-paper macrophages are the Gaucher cell, from a different enzyme deficiency.
  • C. Ring sideroblasts, indicating iron loading of the marrow: Ring sideroblasts belong to sideroblastic anaemia.
  • D. Hypersegmented neutrophils, indicating a megaloblastic marrow: Hypersegmented neutrophils belong to folate or B12 deficiency.
  • E. Reed-Sternberg cells, indicating a lymphoma: Reed-Sternberg cells belong to Hodgkin lymphoma.

Key point: Recognise foam cells and sea-blue histiocytes in sphingomyelinase deficiency and distinguish type A from type B.
