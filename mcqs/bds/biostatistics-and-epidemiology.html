Question 1 · Biostatistics, epidemiology and research · easy

A 40-year-old man enrolled in a diabetes drug trial at a teaching hospital in Lahore tells the research nurse after 3 months that he no longer wishes to take part, but he is worried that the doctors will stop treating his diabetes if he leaves. He gave written informed consent at the start.

What should he be told?

  1. A. He can withdraw without affecting his care
  2. B. He must complete the trial because he signed consent
  3. C. He must pay for the trial drugs taken
  4. D. He can withdraw only with the sponsor's permission
  5. E. He will lose access to the hospital
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. He can withdraw without affecting his care

Informed consent for research must be voluntary and given by a person with capacity, after adequate information about the purpose, procedures, risks, benefits, alternatives and confidentiality, without coercion or undue inducement. A fundamental part of consent is that participants may withdraw at any time, without giving a reason, and without any penalty or effect on their usual medical care. Researchers may ask whether they can keep using data already collected or continue follow-up, but must respect refusal. These principles come from the Declaration of Helsinki and are enforced by research ethics committees, including the National Bioethics Committee of Pakistan.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. He must complete the trial because he signed consent: Consent can be withdrawn.
  • C. He must pay for the trial drugs taken: Participants cannot be charged for trial drugs they received.
  • D. He can withdraw only with the sponsor's permission: Participants may withdraw at any time without the sponsor's permission.
  • E. He will lose access to the hospital: Withdrawing must not affect his normal care at the hospital.

Key point: Research participants may withdraw consent at any time without penalty or effect on their care.

Question 2 · Biostatistics, epidemiology and research · medium

A survey of 2,000 adults in Karachi, carried out over one month, asks each person about their current employment and screens them for depression with a validated questionnaire. Depression is found in 22% of unemployed people and 9% of employed people. A newspaper reports that "losing your job causes depression".

What is the main weakness of this study for showing cause?

  1. A. It cannot show which came first
  2. B. It is too small to find a difference
  3. C. It cannot measure prevalence
  4. D. It suffers from recall bias
  5. E. It cannot include unemployed people
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. It cannot show which came first

In a cross-sectional study, exposure (unemployment) and outcome (depression) are measured at the same time, so the study cannot establish temporality, a key requirement for causation. Unemployment may cause depression, but depression may equally cause people to lose or fail to find work (reverse causation), and other factors such as poverty or chronic illness may cause both (confounding). Cross-sectional studies are good for estimating prevalence and generating hypotheses. To test whether unemployment leads to depression, a cohort study following employed people without depression over time would be needed.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. It is too small to find a difference: 2,000 people is a reasonable sample.
  • C. It cannot measure prevalence: Measuring prevalence is its strength.
  • D. It suffers from recall bias: Not its main weakness here.
  • E. It cannot include unemployed people: Unemployed people were included; the weakness is that exposure and outcome were measured at the same time.

Key point: Cross-sectional studies cannot establish temporality, so they cannot separate cause from reverse causation.

Question 3 · Biostatistics, epidemiology and research · medium

In a case-control study, 60 of 100 patients with oral cancer used naswar regularly, and 40 did not. Among 200 controls without cancer, 40 used naswar regularly, and 160 did not. The investigators want a single number that summarises the strength of the association between naswar use and oral cancer.

What is the odds ratio?

  1. A. 1.5
  2. B. 3.0
  3. C. 4.0
  4. D. 6.0
  5. E. 9.0
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. 6.0

In a 2 × 2 table for a case-control study, a = exposed cases (60), b = exposed controls (40), c = unexposed cases (40) and d = unexposed controls (160). The odds of exposure among cases are a/c = 60/40 = 1.5, and among controls b/d = 40/160 = 0.25. The odds ratio is (a/c) ÷ (b/d) = ad/bc = (60 × 160)/(40 × 40) = 9,600/1,600 = 6.0. People with oral cancer had 6 times the odds of having used naswar compared with controls. An odds ratio of 1 means no association; above 1, a positive association; below 1, a protective one. Its confidence interval shows whether the association could be due to chance.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. 1.5: This is the odds of exposure in cases only.
  • B. 3.0: This compares the proportions 60% and 20%.
  • C. 4.0: Not the correct calculation.
  • E. 9.0: Not the correct calculation.

Key point: The odds ratio in a case-control study is ad/bc: the odds of exposure in cases divided by the odds in controls.

Question 4 · Biostatistics, epidemiology and research · medium

A case-control study finds an odds ratio of 3.2 for the link between a medicine taken in pregnancy and a rare birth defect seen in 1 in 5,000 births. A student asks whether this means the medicine triples the risk of the defect, since only a cohort study or trial can measure relative risk directly. The lecturer says that in this situation the odds ratio is a good estimate of the relative risk.

Why is the odds ratio a good estimate of the relative risk here?

  1. A. Because the sample was large
  2. B. Because the study was blinded
  3. C. Because the controls were matched
  4. D. Because the outcome is rare
  5. E. Because the exposure is common
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. Because the outcome is rare

Risk is the number of people with an outcome divided by all those at risk, while odds are the number with the outcome divided by those without it. When an outcome is rare (usually under about 10%), the number without the outcome is almost the same as the total, so odds and risks are nearly equal, and the odds ratio closely approximates the relative risk ("rare disease assumption"). When the outcome is common, the odds ratio exaggerates the relative risk, moving further from 1. Because case-control studies fix the numbers of cases and controls, they cannot measure risk directly, but with a rare disease the odds ratio gives a good estimate of it.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Because the sample was large: Size does not make the two measures equal.
  • B. Because the study was blinded: Blinding does not explain it; the odds ratio approximates relative risk because the defect is rare.
  • C. Because the controls were matched: Matching does not make them equal.
  • E. Because the exposure is common: The rarity of the outcome is what matters.

Key point: The odds ratio approximates the relative risk when the outcome is rare.

Question 5 · Biostatistics, epidemiology and research · medium

In a case-control study of birth defects, mothers of babies born with a heart defect and mothers of healthy babies are interviewed about medicines taken during early pregnancy. Mothers of affected babies report taking a common antacid much more often. Pharmacy records, checked later, show that both groups actually bought the antacid at similar rates.

Which bias best explains the difference in reported use?

  1. A. Lead-time bias
  2. B. Healthy worker effect
  3. C. Recall bias
  4. D. Loss to follow-up
  5. E. Confounding by age
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Recall bias

Recall bias is a form of information bias in which cases and controls remember or report past exposures differently. Parents of a child with a birth defect, or patients with a serious disease, often think hard about what might have caused it and so report exposures more completely (or over-report them), while controls may forget. This creates a false association or exaggerates a true one. It is a particular problem in case-control studies, which rely on memory. It can be reduced by using records made before the outcome (pharmacy or antenatal records), structured questionnaires, blinding interviewers to case status, and choosing controls with another illness, who also have a reason to search their memories.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Lead-time bias: Lead-time bias applies to screening programmes, not to mothers recalling past medicines.
  • B. Healthy worker effect: A selection effect in occupational studies.
  • D. Loss to follow-up: Applies to cohort studies.
  • E. Confounding by age: Does not explain different reporting.

Key point: Recall bias in case-control studies occurs when cases recall exposures differently from controls; records reduce it.

Question 6 · Biostatistics, epidemiology and research · medium

In a 10-year cohort study of 5,000 factory workers, 2,000 were smokers and 3,000 were non-smokers at the start. During follow-up, 60 smokers and 30 non-smokers developed tuberculosis. Nobody was lost to follow-up. The researchers want to know how many times more likely smokers were to develop tuberculosis.

What is the relative risk of tuberculosis in smokers?

  1. A. 0.33
  2. B. 1.5
  3. C. 2.0
  4. D. 3.0
  5. E. 6.0
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. 3.0

Relative risk (risk ratio) is the incidence in the exposed divided by the incidence in the unexposed. Incidence in smokers = 60/2,000 = 0.03 (3%); in non-smokers = 30/3,000 = 0.01 (1%). Relative risk = 0.03/0.01 = 3.0, so smokers were three times as likely to develop TB over 10 years. Comparing case counts (60 versus 30) is wrong because the groups are different sizes. The risk difference (attributable risk) = 3% - 1% = 2%, or 20 extra cases per 1,000 smokers over 10 years. A relative risk of 1 means no association.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. 0.33: 0.33 is the inverse; smokers' risk (60/2,000) divided by non-smokers' (30/3,000) is 3.0.
  • B. 1.5: Not the correct calculation.
  • C. 2.0: This compares the numbers of cases, not risks.
  • E. 6.0: Not the correct calculation.

Key point: Relative risk = incidence in the exposed ÷ incidence in the unexposed; it is measured directly in cohort studies.

Question 7 · Biostatistics, epidemiology and research · medium

A health department wants to know whether working at a particular asbestos cement factory, which has employed 3,000 people over 25 years, increases the risk of several diseases, including lung cancer, mesothelioma and asbestosis. Very few people in the general population have ever worked with asbestos. Employment records for all past and present workers are available.

Which study design is most suitable?

  1. A. Case-control study
  2. B. Cross-sectional survey of the town
  3. C. Randomised controlled trial
  4. D. Cohort study of the workers
  5. E. Case report
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. Cohort study of the workers

The choice of design depends on what is rare. When an exposure is rare (such as work at one factory), a cohort study that selects people because of their exposure, here all workers on the employment records, ensures enough exposed people, and it can look at several outcomes at once. Because records go back 25 years, a retrospective (historical) cohort design can be used, tracing workers to the present and comparing their disease rates with an unexposed group or the general population (for example with standardised mortality ratios). When a disease is rare, a case-control study is more efficient because it starts by collecting people with the disease.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Case-control study: Better for a rare disease, not a rare exposure.
  • B. Cross-sectional survey of the town: Few people would be exposed.
  • C. Randomised controlled trial: Unethical to assign asbestos exposure.
  • E. Case report: A case report describes a single patient and cannot measure risk in an exposed group.

Key point: Cohort studies suit rare exposures and multiple outcomes; case-control studies suit rare diseases.

Question 8 · Biostatistics, epidemiology and research · medium

In a trial of a new antihypertensive drug, the doctor recruiting patients knows that the next patient will receive the new drug because allocation alternates strictly between drug and placebo. He tends to enrol healthier-looking patients when he knows the new drug is next and to delay enrolling sicker patients until a placebo slot comes up.

Which feature of trial design would have prevented this problem?

  1. A. Larger sample size
  2. B. Intention-to-treat analysis
  3. C. Concealed allocation
  4. D. Longer follow-up
  5. E. Using a placebo
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Concealed allocation

Randomisation only protects against bias if the person enrolling patients cannot know or predict the next allocation. Allocation concealment hides the sequence until a patient is irreversibly enrolled, for example through a central telephone or web-based randomisation service, or sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes. Predictable schemes such as alternation, birth dates or record numbers allow investigators, consciously or not, to steer certain patients into one group, causing selection bias that can exaggerate treatment effects. Concealment is different from blinding: concealment protects the allocation before enrolment, while blinding hides the treatment received after allocation.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Larger sample size: Does not stop biased enrolment.
  • B. Intention-to-treat analysis: Addresses drop-outs, not enrolment.
  • D. Longer follow-up: Longer follow-up does not stop selection bias; concealed random allocation does.
  • E. Using a placebo: A placebo was already used.

Key point: Allocation concealment prevents selection bias at enrolment by hiding the next assignment from recruiters.

Question 9 · Biostatistics, epidemiology and research · medium

A trial compares two treatments for diabetic foot ulcers in 400 patients, assigned by computer-generated random numbers with concealed allocation. At baseline, the two groups have similar ages, HbA1c, smoking rates and ulcer sizes. A student asks why randomisation is better than letting doctors choose the treatment and then adjusting for these measured factors in the analysis.

What is the main advantage of randomisation?

  1. A. It balances unknown confounders too
  2. B. It guarantees the treatment works
  3. C. It removes the need for a control group
  4. D. It prevents loss to follow-up
  5. E. It makes blinding unnecessary
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. It balances unknown confounders too

Randomisation gives each participant the same chance of receiving each treatment, so, with enough participants, the groups are similar in all characteristics, both measured ones (age, glucose control) and unmeasured or unknown ones (genetics, wound care at home, attitudes). Any difference in outcomes can then be attributed to the treatment rather than to confounding. Statistical adjustment in observational studies can only deal with confounders that were measured and measured accurately. Randomisation also prevents doctors choosing treatments based on prognosis (selection bias), especially when combined with allocation concealment.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. It guarantees the treatment works: It does not affect efficacy.
  • C. It removes the need for a control group: A control group is still needed.
  • D. It prevents loss to follow-up: Randomisation does not prevent loss to follow-up; it balances known and unknown confounders.
  • E. It makes blinding unnecessary: Blinding is still useful.

Key point: Randomisation balances both known and unknown confounders between groups, which statistical adjustment cannot do.

Question 10 · Biostatistics, epidemiology and research · medium

In a trial of a new cream for chronic eczema, the outcome is a severity score judged by a dermatologist looking at the skin. In the first version of the protocol, the dermatologist knows which patients are using the new cream. A reviewer warns that the dermatologist may score those patients as more improved because she expects the cream to work.

Which bias does blinding the assessor reduce?

  1. A. Recall bias
  2. B. Selection bias
  3. C. Lead-time bias
  4. D. Publication bias
  5. E. Observer bias
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Observer bias

Blinding (masking) hides which treatment a person receives. Blinding participants reduces performance and reporting bias, and placebo effects. Blinding those who deliver care reduces differences in other care. Blinding outcome assessors reduces observer (detection or ascertainment) bias, where an assessor's expectations influence measurement, especially for subjective outcomes such as symptom scores, skin severity or X-ray interpretation. In a double-blind trial, neither participants nor investigators know the allocation. Where blinding participants is impossible (for example surgery versus tablets), outcome assessors can often still be blinded, and objective outcomes (death, laboratory values) are less vulnerable.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Recall bias: A problem in case-control studies.
  • B. Selection bias: Reduced by randomisation and concealment.
  • C. Lead-time bias: Lead-time bias applies to screening; an unblinded assessor causes observer bias.
  • D. Publication bias: Affects which studies are published.

Key point: Blinding outcome assessors reduces observer (detection) bias, especially for subjective outcomes.
