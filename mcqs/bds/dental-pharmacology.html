Question 1 · Local anaesthetics, pain control, sedation and emergency drugs · easy

A 24-year-old dental student in Peshawar administers 2% lidocaine with 1:100,000 adrenaline for an inferior alveolar nerve block prior to restoring an extensive carious lesion in tooth 47 (lower right second molar). The student observes reduced bleeding and prolonged pulpal anaesthesia.

Which receptor mediates adrenaline's vasoconstriction at the injection site?

  1. A. Alpha-1 adrenergic receptor
  2. B. Beta-1 adrenergic receptor
  3. C. Beta-2 adrenergic receptor
  4. D. V1 vasopressin receptor
  5. E. Muscarinic M3 receptor
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Alpha-1 adrenergic receptor

Adrenaline is an agonist at alpha-1, alpha-2, beta-1, and beta-2 adrenergic receptors. At the concentrations used in dental local anaesthetic solutions (1:80,000 to 1:200,000), local vasoconstriction is predominantly mediated by alpha-1 adrenergic receptors on vascular smooth muscle. This vasoconstriction slows systemic absorption of the local anaesthetic, prolongs the depth and duration of pulpal and soft tissue anaesthesia, reduces peak blood levels (minimising systemic toxicity), and provides local surgical haemostasis.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Beta-1 adrenergic receptor: Beta-1 adrenergic receptors are located predominantly in the myocardium and increase heart rate and contractility, not peripheral vasoconstriction.
  • C. Beta-2 adrenergic receptor: Beta-2 adrenergic receptor stimulation causes vasodilation in skeletal muscle vascular beds and bronchodilation rather than local vasoconstriction.
  • D. V1 vasopressin receptor: V1 receptors mediate vasoconstriction induced by vasopressin analogues like felypressin, not adrenergic catecholamines like adrenaline.
  • E. Muscarinic M3 receptor: Muscarinic M3 receptors mediate parasympathetic glandular secretion and smooth muscle contraction, inducing endothelium-dependent vasodilation through nitric oxide.

Key point: Identify the alpha-1 adrenergic receptor as the mediator of adrenaline-induced local vasoconstriction.

Question 2 · Antimicrobials, antiseptics and drugs used in the mouth · easy

A 64-year-old female presents to a dental clinic in Peshawar complaining of burning and redness under her complete maxillary acrylic denture for 3 weeks. Intraoral examination reveals diffuse erythema corresponding strictly to the denture-bearing area of the hard palate. She is prescribed nystatin oral suspension 100,000 units/mL to rinse and retain in the mouth four times daily.

Which biochemical mechanism accounts for the antifungal activity of this prescribed agent?

  1. A. Inhibition of fungal squalene epoxidase
  2. B. Binding to membrane ergosterol to form transmembrane pores
  3. C. Inhibition of fungal lanosterol 14-alpha-demethylase
  4. D. Inhibition of beta-(1,3)-D-glucan synthase in the cell wall
  5. E. Inhibition of fungal thymidylate synthase and DNA synthesis
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Binding to membrane ergosterol to form transmembrane pores

Nystatin is a classic polyene antifungal widely used topically for oral candidiasis (denture-related stomatitis). It binds avidly to ergosterol, an essential sterol constituent of fungal cell membranes. This binding disrupts membrane architecture and forms aqueous transmembrane pores or channels. Intracellular cations, primarily potassium and magnesium, leak out rapidly, collapsing cellular electrochemical gradients and leading to fungal cell lysis and death. Because mammalian membranes contain cholesterol rather than ergosterol, polyenes exhibit selective fungal toxicity, though systemic amphotericin B retains some affinity for mammalian cells. Nystatin is not absorbed across the intact gastrointestinal tract, making it exceptionally safe for topical oral administration.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Inhibition of fungal squalene epoxidase: Squalene epoxidase inhibition is the mechanism of allylamines such as terbinafine, not polyene antifungals like nystatin.
  • C. Inhibition of fungal lanosterol 14-alpha-demethylase: Inhibition of 14-alpha-demethylase is the primary mechanism of azole antifungals such as fluconazole and miconazole, not polyenes.
  • D. Inhibition of beta-(1,3)-D-glucan synthase in the cell wall: Glucan synthase inhibition is the antifungal mechanism of echinocandins such as caspofungin, not topical polyenes.
  • E. Inhibition of fungal thymidylate synthase and DNA synthesis: Thymidylate synthase and nucleic acid synthesis inhibition is the mechanism of flucytosine, not nystatin.

Key point: Identify the mechanism of action of nystatin as binding to fungal ergosterol to create lethal membrane pores.

Question 3 · Local anaesthetics, pain control, sedation and emergency drugs · easy

A 7-year-old child in Hyderabad is anxious about receiving an inferior alveolar nerve block injection. To minimise needle puncture discomfort, the dental surgeon decides to place a topical anaesthetic gel on dry mucosa over the injection site for two minutes.

Which ester local anaesthetic is most commonly formulated as a 20% topical gel in dentistry?

  1. A. Benzocaine
  2. B. Articaine
  3. C. Bupivacaine
  4. D. Procaine
  5. E. Prilocaine
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Benzocaine

Benzocaine is an ester-type local anaesthetic with poor water solubility and low systemic absorption when applied to intact mucous membranes. It is standardly formulated at a concentration of 20% as a flavoured gel, ointment, or spray in dental practice. When applied to dried oral mucosa for 1 to 2 minutes, it achieves effective surface anaesthesia of the outer 2 to 3 mm of tissue, significantly decreasing the discomfort of needle penetration. Because it is poorly soluble, systemic toxicity is rare, though prolonged or excessive mucosal application can precipitate methaemoglobinaemia.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Articaine: Articaine is formulated as an injectable 4% amide solution with adrenaline and is not used as a mucosal surface anaesthetic gel.
  • C. Bupivacaine: Bupivacaine is an injectable amide used for long-acting regional blocks and is not formulated as a 20% topical gel.
  • D. Procaine: Procaine (Novocain) is an injectable ester with very poor mucosal penetration that is obsolete as a surface topical agent.
  • E. Prilocaine: Prilocaine is used in topical EMLA cream at 2.5% combined with lidocaine, but is not formulated alone as a 20% dental gel.

Key point: Identify benzocaine 20% as the most widely used topical mucosal anaesthetic in dentistry.

Question 4 · Antimicrobials, antiseptics and drugs used in the mouth · easy

A 32-year-old female presents to a dental surgery in Mirpur, Azad Kashmir, with severe primary herpetic gingivostomatitis presenting as painful oral ulcerations, cervical lymphadenopathy, and low-grade fever. The dentist considers prescribing an oral antiviral and selects valaciclovir rather than oral acyclovir.

What is the principal pharmacokinetic advantage of the chosen agent over oral acyclovir?

  1. A. Complete absence of renal excretion
  2. B. Substantially higher oral bioavailability
  3. C. Direct antiviral action without intracellular phosphorylation
  4. D. Marked activity against acyclovir-resistant thymidine kinase-deficient strains
  5. E. Selective accumulation within peripheral sensory nerve cell nuclei
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Substantially higher oral bioavailability

Valaciclovir is the L-valyl ester prodrug of acyclovir. Oral acyclovir has poor and erratic gastrointestinal absorption, resulting in a low oral bioavailability of only 15% to 20%, requiring inconvenient dosing of 200 mg five times daily for herpes simplex. In contrast, valaciclovir is actively absorbed from the intestine via human peptide transporter 1 (PEPT1) and rapidly undergoes extensive first-pass hepatic conversion to acyclovir and L-valine. This mechanism achieves an oral bioavailability of approximately 55% to 65%, yielding plasma acyclovir concentrations comparable to intravenous administration. Consequently, valaciclovir allows less frequent dosing (e.g., 500 mg twice daily or 1000 mg twice daily), significantly improving patient adherence.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Complete absence of renal excretion: Valaciclovir is hydrolysed to acyclovir, which is primarily excreted unchanged by the kidneys via glomerular filtration and tubular secretion.
  • C. Direct antiviral action without intracellular phosphorylation: Valaciclovir is inactive itself; it must be converted to acyclovir and triphosphorylated before inhibiting viral DNA synthesis.
  • D. Marked activity against acyclovir-resistant thymidine kinase-deficient strains: Valaciclovir produces acyclovir and remains completely ineffective against viral strains lacking functional viral thymidine kinase.
  • E. Selective accumulation within peripheral sensory nerve cell nuclei: Valaciclovir distributes throughout body water without preferential targeting or selective sequestration within neuronal cell nuclei.

Key point: Explain the enhanced oral bioavailability and improved dosing regimen of valaciclovir compared to oral acyclovir.

Question 5 · Local anaesthetics, pain control, sedation and emergency drugs · easy

A 21-year-old university student in Dera Ismail Khan presents with moderate inflammatory pain following crown preparation of tooth 11 (upper right central incisor). She has normal liver and kidney function, weighs 55 kg, and takes no regular medications. The dental surgeon writes a prescription for oral paracetamol tablets.

What is the maximum daily dose of paracetamol for a healthy adult?

  1. A. 2 g
  2. B. 3 g
  3. C. 4 g
  4. D. 6 g
  5. E. 8 g
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. 4 g

The standard therapeutic dose of paracetamol (acetaminophen) for an adult with normal hepatic and renal function is 500 mg to 1000 mg every 4 to 6 hours as needed, up to a strict maximum of 4000 mg (4 g) within any 24-hour period. Doses exceeding 4 g/day in adults can saturate standard glucuronidation and sulfation pathways, leading to excess production of the hepatotoxic intermediate NAPQI and risking acute centrilobular hepatic necrosis. In patients with chronic alcoholism, hepatic cirrhosis, severe malnutrition, or body weight under 50 kg, the maximum daily dose should be reduced to 2-3 g/day.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. 2 g: A daily limit of 2 g is often advised for patients with chronic alcoholic liver disease, malnutrition, or mild hepatic impairment, but is below the standard adult maximum.
  • B. 3 g: While 3 g is increasingly suggested as a conservative voluntary ceiling in some outpatient guidelines, 4 g remains the established BNF and standard maximum.
  • D. 6 g: A cumulative dose of 6 g per day exceeds safe metabolic conjugation capacity and substantially increases the risk of glutathione depletion and liver injury.
  • E. 8 g: An 8 g daily dose is well within the hepatotoxic overdose range where severe centrilobular hepatic necrosis can occur.

Key point: Identify 4 grams as the maximum daily therapeutic dose of paracetamol in a healthy adult.

Question 6 · Antimicrobials, antiseptics and drugs used in the mouth · easy

A 26-year-old woman in Islamabad visits a dental clinic requesting at-home vital bleaching for generalised aesthetic tooth yellowing. The dentist fabricates custom-fitted bleaching trays and dispenses a 10% carbamide peroxide gel to be applied nightly. The patient reads the product label and asks how much active hydrogen peroxide is delivered by this gel during treatment.

Approximately what concentration of active hydrogen peroxide is released by this formulation?

  1. A. 1.5%
  2. B. 2.0%
  3. C. 3.5%
  4. D. 6.5%
  5. E. 10.0%
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. 3.5%

Carbamide peroxide (urea hydrogen peroxide) is a stable organic adduct used in at-home nightguard vital bleaching. In the presence of oral saliva and moisture, carbamide peroxide undergoes hydrolytic dissociation in a 1:3 stoichiometric ratio, breaking down into approximately 3.35% to 3.5% hydrogen peroxide and 6.5% to 6.65% urea. Hydrogen peroxide serves as the active oxidising agent, breaking down into free radicals (perhydroxyl and oxygen radicals) that cleave conjugated double bonds of complex organic chromophores in enamel and dentine. The urea byproduct decomposes into ammonia and carbon dioxide; ammonia elevates the local pH above 7, which optimizes peroxide bleaching efficiency while reducing the risk of enamel demineralisation.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. 1.5%: A 1.5% concentration would correspond to approximately 4% to 5% carbamide peroxide, far below the standard 10% gel release.
  • B. 2.0%: A 2% yield corresponds to roughly 6% carbamide peroxide, which is lower than the stoichiometric breakdown of a 10% preparation.
  • D. 6.5%: Urea accounts for approximately 6.5% of the breakdown product of a 10% carbamide peroxide formulation, not hydrogen peroxide.
  • E. 10.0%: Carbamide peroxide is an adduct; 10% carbamide peroxide does not yield 10% hydrogen peroxide, but approximately 3.35% to 3.5%.

Key point: State the stoichiometric degradation products and hydrogen peroxide yield of 10% carbamide peroxide.

Question 7 · Local anaesthetics, pain control, sedation and emergency drugs · easy

A 34-year-old woman attends a dental clinic in Quetta for follow-up of erosive oral lichen planus. She has been applying 0.1% triamcinolone acetonide in oral paste to her buccal mucosa three times daily for the past six weeks. She reports a burning sensation across her tongue and palate. Examination shows curd-like white plaques on the hard palate and dorsum of the tongue that wipe off with gauze, leaving raw, erythematous bleeding surfaces.

What is the most likely diagnosis for this secondary mucosal complication?

  1. A. Erythema multiforme
  2. B. Geographic tongue
  3. C. Oral leukoplakia
  4. D. Hairy leukoplakia
  5. E. Pseudomembranous candidiasis
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Pseudomembranous candidiasis

Topical corticosteroids (such as triamcinolone acetonide, betamethasone, or clobetasol) are primary agents for managing immune-mediated oral conditions including erosive lichen planus and aphthous ulceration. However, their local immunosuppressive and anti-inflammatory properties disrupt the resident oral microbiota and impair mucosal cell-mediated immunity. This permits the opportunistic overgrowth of Candida albicans, resulting in acute pseudomembranous candidiasis (oral thrush), characterized by wipeable, curd-like pseudomembranes overlying inflamed, bleeding mucosa. Management involves topical antifungals such as miconazole oral gel or nystatin suspension.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Erythema multiforme: Erythema multiforme gives erosions and crusted lips, often with target skin lesions, not wipeable white plaques.
  • B. Geographic tongue: Geographic tongue features moving areas of smooth red depapillation bordered by white margins, which do not scrape off.
  • C. Oral leukoplakia: Leukoplakia presents as a white plaque that cannot be wiped off with gauze and cannot be characterized clinically as another disease.
  • D. Hairy leukoplakia: Oral hairy leukoplakia presents as corrugated, non-wipeable lateral tongue lesions driven by Epstein-Barr virus in immunocompromised patients.

Key point: Identify pseudomembranous candidiasis as a common adverse effect of prolonged topical oral corticosteroid application.

Question 8 · Antimicrobials, antiseptics and drugs used in the mouth · easy

A BDS student at a dental teaching hospital in Peshawar performs root canal treatment on tooth 46 (lower right first molar). After debriding the canals with 3% sodium hypochlorite, the student immediately flushes the canals with 2% chlorhexidine gluconate without an intervening saline rinse. A thick, dark orange-brown precipitate forms instantly in the pulp chamber, coating the canal walls.

What is the primary chemical product formed by this adverse irrigant combination?

  1. A. Chloramine gas
  2. B. Para-chloroaniline
  3. C. Calcium hydroxide
  4. D. Hydrochloric acid
  5. E. Sodium chlorate
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Para-chloroaniline

When sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl) directly contacts chlorhexidine gluconate (CHX), an acid-base oxidation-reduction reaction occurs that produces an insoluble orange-brown precipitate containing para-chloroaniline (PCA) and related chlorinated aromatic compounds. This precipitate adheres tenaciously to root canal dentine walls, forming a chemical smear layer that occludes dentinal tubules and significantly impairs the seal and bond strength of endodontic sealers. Furthermore, PCA is known to possess haematotoxic, cytotoxic, and potential carcinogenic properties. Clinicians should never use chlorhexidine directly after sodium hypochlorite without copious intermediate flushing and drying using sterile physiological saline or sterile water to prevent precipitate formation.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Chloramine gas: Chloramine is a gaseous byproduct of hypochlorite reacting with organic matter or ammonia, not the insoluble brown precipitate.
  • C. Calcium hydroxide: Calcium hydroxide is a white alkaline paste used as a medicament, not a precipitate formed from biguanide-hypochlorite reactions.
  • D. Hydrochloric acid: Hydrochloric acid does not form as an insoluble coloured precipitate in the root canal space.
  • E. Sodium chlorate: Sodium chlorate is an oxidising salt formed by hypochlorite decomposition, not the orange-brown precipitate from chlorhexidine mixing.

Key point: Identify the formation and clinical hazards of para-chloroaniline from combined NaOCl and chlorhexidine use.

Question 9 · Local anaesthetics, pain control, sedation and emergency drugs · easy

A 24-year-old apprehensive man undergoes surgical removal of 18 and 28 under intravenous conscious sedation in Islamabad. The dental surgeon slowly titrates intravenous midazolam until slurred speech and Verrill sign (ptosis of half the upper eyelid) are achieved. The patient remains responsive to verbal commands throughout the procedure.

What is the primary molecular mechanism of midazolam at the GABA-A receptor complex?

  1. A. Direct opening of chloride channels in the absence of GABA
  2. B. Prolonging the duration of chloride channel opening
  3. C. Competitive antagonism of glycine-gated ion channels
  4. D. Increasing the frequency of chloride channel opening
  5. E. Blocking the voltage-dependent sodium channels
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. Increasing the frequency of chloride channel opening

Midazolam is a short-acting, water-soluble benzodiazepine that acts as a positive allosteric modulator at the pentameric GABA-A receptor-chloride channel complex in the central nervous system. It binds selectively to the interface between alpha and gamma-2 subunits. This allosteric binding enhances the receptor affinity for the inhibitory neurotransmitter gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA), increasing the frequency of chloride ion channel opening. Increased intracellular influx of chloride hyperpolarizes the neuronal membrane, depressing neuronal excitability and generating anxiolysis, sedation, anterograde amnesia, and muscle relaxation.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Direct opening of chloride channels in the absence of GABA: Barbiturates at high doses directly open GABA-A channels; benzodiazepines require GABA to be present.
  • B. Prolonging the duration of chloride channel opening: Barbiturates increase the duration of channel opening, whereas benzodiazepines increase channel opening frequency.
  • C. Competitive antagonism of glycine-gated ion channels: Benzodiazepines act on GABA-A receptors, not inhibitory glycine receptors.
  • E. Blocking the voltage-dependent sodium channels: Local anaesthetics block voltage-gated sodium channels; benzodiazepines modulate ligand-gated chloride channels.

Key point: Describe the molecular mechanism of midazolam as an allosteric modulator that increases the opening frequency of GABA-A chloride channels.

Question 10 · Antimicrobials, antiseptics and drugs used in the mouth · easy

A 29-year-old woman at 34 weeks of gestation presents to a dental clinic in Abbottabad with throbbing pain from acute apical periodontitis affecting tooth 36 (lower left first molar). Endodontic access is performed, and she requests medication for post-treatment pain. She has no known drug allergies. The dentist explains why ibuprofen is strictly avoided in this stage of pregnancy and instead prescribes paracetamol.

What is the primary fetal complication avoided by withholding ibuprofen?

  1. A. Enamel hypoplasia of primary tooth germs
  2. B. Severe intrauterine neural tube defects
  3. C. Sensorineural auditory apparatus damage
  4. D. Premature closure of the ductus arteriosus
  5. E. Accelerated craniosynostosis of cranial sutures
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. Premature closure of the ductus arteriosus

During fetal development, patency of the ductus arteriosus is maintained by continuous local production of vasodilatory prostaglandins, primarily prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) and prostacyclin (PGI2). Administration of non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) such as ibuprofen during the third trimester (especially beyond 28 to 30 weeks of gestation) inhibits cyclo-oxygenase enzymes, curtailing PGE2 production. This promotes premature functional constriction or closure of the ductus arteriosus in utero, leading to persistent pulmonary arterial hypertension, tricuspid regurgitation, and fetal right-sided heart failure. In addition, third-trimester NSAID exposure impairs fetal renal perfusion (causing oligohydramnios) and inhibits myometrial contractility (delaying labour). Paracetamol is the safest first-line analgesic across all stages of pregnancy.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Enamel hypoplasia of primary tooth germs: Tooth discolouration and enamel hypoplasia result from tetracycline exposure during odontogenesis, not from maternal cyclo-oxygenase inhibition.
  • B. Severe intrauterine neural tube defects: Neural tube closure anomalies occur during first-trimester embryogenesis (weeks 3 to 8) and correlate with folate antagonism, not late third-trimester NSAID use.
  • C. Sensorineural auditory apparatus damage: Eighth cranial nerve toxicity and congenital deafness occur with aminoglycosides like gentamicin or streptomycin, not non-steroidal anti-inflammatory agents.
  • E. Accelerated craniosynostosis of cranial sutures: Premature cranial suture closure is a genetic or teratogenic malformation associated with retinoid toxicity, not with prostaglandin synthesis inhibitors.

Key point: Explain why NSAIDs are contraindicated in the third trimester due to premature closure of the fetal ductus arteriosus.
