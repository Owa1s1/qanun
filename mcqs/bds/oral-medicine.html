Question 1 · Oral medicine in systemic disease · easy

A 28-year-old man attends a dental teaching hospital in Peshawar with painless white patches on the side of the tongue for three weeks. He is HIV positive and not taking antiretrovirals. Examination shows a white corrugated patch on the lateral border of the tongue that cannot be scraped off with gauze. CD4 count is 90 cells/µL (500-1500).

What is the most likely diagnosis?

  1. A. Pseudomembranous candidosis
  2. B. Oral hairy leukoplakia
  3. C. Erythematous candidosis
  4. D. Kaposi sarcoma
  5. E. Oral squamous cell carcinoma
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Oral hairy leukoplakia

Oral hairy leukoplakia is strongly associated with HIV and Epstein-Barr virus. It appears as a white, corrugated patch on the lateral tongue that cannot be scraped off and is usually painless. Its presence signals immunosuppression, but the lesion itself needs no specific treatment; manage the underlying HIV and review. Pseudomembranous candidiasis would be scrapable, and Kaposi sarcoma would be vascular and violaceous.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Pseudomembranous candidosis: White plaques that wipe off leaving erythema; this patch is fixed and corrugated.
  • C. Erythematous candidosis: Presents as red atrophic areas, often on the palate or tongue dorsum, not a white patch.
  • D. Kaposi sarcoma: Appears as violaceous macules or nodules, usually on the palate, not a white tongue lesion.
  • E. Oral squamous cell carcinoma: Usually an indurated ulcer in an older tobacco user; this is a white patch in a young HIV patient.

Key point: Identify oral hairy leukoplakia in an HIV patient from its non-scrapable lateral tongue patch.

Question 2 · Oral radiology · easy

A 24-year-old woman attends a dental teaching hospital in Peshawar for a routine check-up. She asks how the dental X-ray machine creates the image. The machine uses a tungsten filament heated by an electric current, and electrons strike a metal target inside a vacuum tube.

Which radiation is produced when high-speed electrons strike the target in a dental X-ray tube?

  1. A. Visible light
  2. B. X-rays
  3. C. Gamma rays
  4. D. Ultraviolet rays
  5. E. Infrared rays
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. X-rays

In a dental X-ray tube, a heated tungsten filament releases electrons that are accelerated across a vacuum and strike a metal target, usually tungsten. The sudden deceleration produces bremsstrahlung radiation, and inner-shell electron displacement produces characteristic radiation. Together these form the polychromatic X-ray beam used for intraoral and extraoral imaging. The beam is invisible, penetrates tissues differentially, and records on film or a digital sensor. Gamma rays originate from nuclear decay, while visible, ultraviolet and infrared rays do not have enough energy to pass through teeth and bone for diagnostic radiography.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Visible light: Visible light is not energetic enough to penetrate soft tissue or expose dental film; the story describes electron bombardment of a metal target.
  • C. Gamma rays: Gamma rays come from radioactive decay of unstable nuclei, not from electron strikes on a tungsten target in a dental tube.
  • D. Ultraviolet rays: Ultraviolet rays lack the penetrating power needed for intraoral radiographs and are not generated by an X-ray tube.
  • E. Infrared rays: Infrared rays are thermal radiation and cannot form a radiographic image of tooth structure.

Key point: Identify the radiation produced by electron-target interaction in a dental X-ray tube.

Question 3 · Principles of oral medicine: assessment, investigations and therapy · easy

A 10-year-old boy is brought to a dental OPD in Swat for a routine check-up. He is otherwise well and has no oral lesions. On extraoral examination, several small nodes are felt in the submandibular region on both sides.

Which finding would make these submandibular nodes most likely normal in this child?

  1. A. The nodes are rubbery and matted together
  2. B. Each node is fixed to underlying muscle
  3. C. Each node is tender and warm
  4. D. Each node measures three centimetres
  5. E. Each node is under 1 cm, soft and mobile
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Each node is under 1 cm, soft and mobile

In children, small cervical or submandibular lymph nodes are frequently palpable and represent reactive responses to minor upper respiratory or dental infections. Normal nodes are generally under one centimetre, soft, mobile and non-tender. Fixed, hard, irregular, tender or markedly enlarged nodes are abnormal and require further assessment. In Pakistan, tuberculosis is an important cause of persistent cervical lymphadenopathy, so duration and associated symptoms must always be recorded.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. The nodes are rubbery and matted together: Matted, rubbery nodes suggest lymphoma or tuberculosis and need investigation.
  • B. Each node is fixed to underlying muscle: Fixation suggests malignancy or tuberculosis and is not a normal feature.
  • C. Each node is tender and warm: Tenderness and warmth indicate acute infection rather than a normal reactive node.
  • D. Each node measures three centimetres: Nodes larger than one centimetre in a child warrant further investigation.

Key point: Recognise the features of normal reactive cervical lymph nodes in a child.

Question 4 · Diseases of the oral mucosa, tongue and lips · easy

A 58-year-old woman attends a dental clinic in Mardan with a burning mouth and white patches on the inner cheeks for ten days. She has completed a seven-day course of amoxicillin for a dental abscess and uses a beclometasone inhaler for asthma. Examination reveals white plaques on both buccal mucosae that can be wiped off with gauze, leaving a red base. The dorsum of the tongue is normal. Temperature 36.8 °C.

What is the most likely diagnosis?

  1. A. Oral lichen planus
  2. B. Pseudomembranous oral candidosis
  3. C. Leukoplakia
  4. D. Oral hairy leukoplakia
  5. E. Chronic mucocutaneous candidosis
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Pseudomembranous oral candidosis

Pseudomembranous candidosis presents as white plaques that can be wiped off, leaving a red base. It commonly follows broad-spectrum antibiotics or inhaled corticosteroid use, and denture wearers are also at risk. Management includes topical nystatin or miconazole gel, good denture hygiene, leaving the denture out at night, and rinsing the mouth after steroid inhaler use. Underlying diabetes or immunosuppression should be checked. In Pakistan, nystatin is widely available and inexpensive, making it a practical first-line choice in most dental clinics.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Oral lichen planus: White lacy streaks that do not wipe off; the plaques here are removable and appeared after antibiotics.
  • C. Leukoplakia: A white patch that cannot be scraped off and is not linked to recent antibiotic or steroid use.
  • D. Oral hairy leukoplakia: A white corrugated patch on the side of the tongue, usually in HIV, that cannot be wiped off.
  • E. Chronic mucocutaneous candidosis: Persistent candida from early childhood with skin and nail involvement, not a short episode after medication.

Key point: To recognise pseudomembranous candidosis by its wipeable plaques and association with antibiotics or inhaled steroids.

Question 5 · Orofacial pain, the temporomandibular joint and facial palsy · easy

A 58-year-old man attends a private dental clinic in Swabi with brief, severe electric-shock pain on the right side of his face, triggered by chewing and brushing, lasting seconds, for three weeks. He has no numbness. Examination shows normal cranial nerves, no rash, and no dental caries.

What is the most likely diagnosis?

  1. A. Cluster headache
  2. B. Dental abscess
  3. C. Migraine
  4. D. Trigeminal neuralgia
  5. E. Temporomandibular disorder
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. Trigeminal neuralgia

Trigeminal neuralgia presents as sudden, severe, electric-shock-like pain in one or more divisions of the trigeminal nerve, lasting seconds to two minutes, triggered by light touch, chewing, or brushing. It is uncommon before age 40 and is usually in the maxillary or mandibular division. Examination between attacks is normal. Carbamazepine is the first-line drug and should be started at a low dose and titrated. Secondary causes should be considered if there are sensory deficits.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Cluster headache: Cluster headache causes severe orbital pain with tearing and congestion, not brief trigger-related shocks.
  • B. Dental abscess: A dental abscess causes continuous throbbing pain with a carious tooth, not triggered seconds-long shocks.
  • C. Migraine: Migraine causes hours of throbbing headache with nausea and light sensitivity, not facial electric shocks.
  • E. Temporomandibular disorder: Temporomandibular disorder causes dull joint or muscle pain with clicking, not brief lancinating triggers.

Key point: To diagnose classic trigeminal neuralgia from brief trigger-related electric-shock facial pain.

Question 6 · Salivary glands, dry mouth and neck swellings · easy

A 35-year-old woman with dry eyes and dry mouth has positive anti-Ro/SSA antibodies and a lip biopsy showing lymphocytic aggregates.

Which process best explains her salivary gland dysfunction?

  1. A. Obstruction of the duct by a calculus
  2. B. Chronic bacterial infection of the parotid gland
  3. C. Autoimmune lymphocytic infiltration of exocrine glands
  4. D. Excessive sympathetic nervous system activity
  5. E. Age-related atrophy of salivary acini
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Autoimmune lymphocytic infiltration of exocrine glands

Sjogren syndrome is an autoimmune disease in which CD4 positive T lymphocytes infiltrate salivary and lacrimal glands, damaging acinar tissue and reducing secretion. Anti-Ro/SSA and anti-La/SSB antibodies are common serological markers. Obstruction, infection, sympathetic overactivity, and simple ageing do not produce the characteristic biopsy pattern or sicca complex.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Obstruction of the duct by a calculus: A duct calculus causes obstructive swelling and pain, not bilateral sicca symptoms with autoantibodies.
  • B. Chronic bacterial infection of the parotid gland: Chronic bacterial parotitis presents with fever and pus, not autoantibodies and lip biopsy findings.
  • D. Excessive sympathetic nervous system activity: Sympathetic activity reduces flow transiently but does not cause permanent lymphocytic infiltration.
  • E. Age-related atrophy of salivary acini: Age-related atrophy is mild and would not explain anti-Ro/SSA positivity or a biopsy infiltrate.

Key point: State the underlying mechanism of Sjogren syndrome.

Question 7 · Oral medicine in systemic disease · easy

A 35-year-old woman attends a private dental clinic in Karachi for extraction of 46, the lower right first molar, because of irreversible pulpitis. She is HIV positive and taking antiretroviral therapy. Her CD4 count is 180 cells/µL (500-1500). She has no fever or oral lesions.

What is the most appropriate management of her extraction?

  1. A. Defer extraction until the CD4 count rises above 200 cells/µL
  2. B. Prescribe amoxicillin 500 mg three times daily for five days before extraction
  3. C. Prescribe metronidazole 400 mg three times daily for three days before extraction
  4. D. Proceed with extraction under local anaesthesia
  5. E. Refer for extraction under general anaesthesia
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. Proceed with extraction under local anaesthesia

Patients with HIV who are medically stable can receive routine dental treatment under local anaesthesia. Antibiotic prophylaxis is not recommended solely because of HIV infection. Extraction should not be delayed merely because the CD4 count is below 200 cells/µL unless there is an active opportunistic infection. Use strict cross-infection precautions, as hepatitis B and C are also common in Pakistan.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Defer extraction until the CD4 count rises above 200 cells/µL: Unnecessary delay; elective dental care can proceed in a medically stable HIV patient.
  • B. Prescribe amoxicillin 500 mg three times daily for five days before extraction: Antibiotic prophylaxis is not indicated for HIV without a high-risk cardiac condition and promotes resistance.
  • C. Prescribe metronidazole 400 mg three times daily for three days before extraction: Metronidazole is not indicated as prophylaxis for a simple extraction and does not replace HIV care.
  • E. Refer for extraction under general anaesthesia: Not required for a routine extraction in a stable patient; local anaesthesia is sufficient.

Key point: Manage a routine dental extraction in a stable HIV patient without unnecessary antibiotics or referral.

Question 8 · Oral radiology · easy

A 19-year-old woman attends a dental teaching hospital in Quetta for extraction of an impacted lower right third molar (48). Before the radiograph, she asks whether X-rays can harm living cells. The dentist explains that ionising radiation can break chemical bonds in DNA and cause cell death or mutation.

Which radiation effect has no safe threshold and may occur after very low doses?

  1. A. Acute radiation syndrome
  2. B. Cataract formation
  3. C. Tissue reaction
  4. D. Deterministic effect
  5. E. Stochastic effect
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Stochastic effect

Stochastic effects arise from random molecular damage, such as DNA strand breaks, and the probability of occurrence rises with dose while severity does not. There is no threshold below which the risk is zero, so even very low dental exposures carry a theoretical, very small risk of cancer or hereditary effects. Deterministic effects, including tissue reactions, cataracts and acute radiation syndrome, only appear after a threshold dose and become more severe as the dose increases. Dental X-ray doses are far below thresholds for deterministic injury, but the ALARA principle still applies to keep stochastic risk as low as reasonably achievable.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Acute radiation syndrome: Acute radiation syndrome follows very high whole-body exposure and is not seen with routine dental radiography.
  • B. Cataract formation: Cataract formation is a deterministic tissue reaction with a threshold dose to the lens of the eye.
  • C. Tissue reaction: Tissue reactions are another name for deterministic effects and require a minimum dose before they appear.
  • D. Deterministic effect: Deterministic effects have a threshold dose and increase in severity above it, unlike the random cellular change described.

Key point: Distinguish stochastic from deterministic radiation effects.

Question 9 · Principles of oral medicine: assessment, investigations and therapy · easy

A 55-year-old man attends a private dental clinic in Mansehra for a check-up. He has no symptoms in his mouth. On examination of the underside of the tongue, several soft, compressible, bluish-purple swellings are seen on both sides. They disappear when pressed and refill when pressure is released.

What is the most likely nature of these lingual swellings?

  1. A. Oral melanotic macule
  2. B. Traumatic neuroma
  3. C. Lymphangioma
  4. D. Squamous cell carcinoma
  5. E. Varix
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Varix

Lingual varices are dilated veins on the ventral tongue and floor of mouth, common after age 50. They appear as soft, bluish-purple, compressible swellings that blanch and refill with pressure. They are asymptomatic and require no treatment. They must be distinguished from varicose veins that are thrombosed or painful, and from vascular lesions such as lymphangioma or malignancy, which are not bilateral and compressible in this manner. Reassurance is all that is needed.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Oral melanotic macule: A melanotic macule is flat and brown or black, not compressible or bluish.
  • B. Traumatic neuroma: A neuroma is a firm painful nodule, usually after trauma, and does not blanch or refill.
  • C. Lymphangioma: A lymphangioma is a compressible swelling but is usually present from childhood and does not look like a simple varix.
  • D. Squamous cell carcinoma: Carcinoma is an indurated ulcer or mass, not a bilateral compressible bluish swelling.

Key point: Identify normal lingual varices as a benign age-related variant.

Question 10 · Diseases of the oral mucosa, tongue and lips · easy

A 22-year-old man attends a dental OPD in Peshawar with fever and malaise for three days. He has multiple small vesicles on the attached gingiva and palate that have ruptured, leaving painful ulcers. Examination also shows bilateral cervical lymphadenopathy and inflamed, bleeding gums. He has no previous history of similar lesions.

What is the most likely diagnosis?

  1. A. Recurrent herpes labialis
  2. B. Recurrent aphthous stomatitis
  3. C. Primary herpetic gingivostomatitis
  4. D. Acute necrotising ulcerative gingivitis
  5. E. Oral candidiasis
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Primary herpetic gingivostomatitis

Primary herpetic gingivostomatitis is the initial manifestation of HSV-1 infection, typically in children and young adults. It presents with fever, malaise, cervical lymphadenopathy, and multiple vesicles on keratinised mucosa that rupture to form painful ulcers. The condition is self-limiting but aciclovir can reduce severity if started early. Dental treatment should be deferred until lesions heal. Supportive care includes analgesia, hydration, and antiseptic mouthwash. Cross-infection control is important in the clinic.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Recurrent herpes labialis: Labialis affects the lip vermilion unilaterally without fever, malaise or widespread gingival ulceration.
  • B. Recurrent aphthous stomatitis: Aphthous ulcers occur on non-keratinised mucosa and are not preceded by fever and vesicles.
  • D. Acute necrotising ulcerative gingivitis: ANUG causes painful bleeding gums with necrotic papillae, not multiple intraoral vesicles and fever.
  • E. Oral candidiasis: Candidiasis presents as white plaques, not painful ruptured vesicles with fever and lymphadenopathy.

Key point: To diagnose primary herpetic gingivostomatitis from fever, malaise, and multiple ruptured oral vesicles.
