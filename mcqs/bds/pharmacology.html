Question 1 · Haematological drugs: anticoagulants, antiplatelets and haematinics · easy

A 30-year-old woman from Swabi is 8 weeks pregnant. Six months ago she had a deep vein thrombosis in the left leg after a long bus journey, and she has been taking warfarin since. She does not have a mechanical heart valve. She is anxious about harming her baby and asks the doctor which anticoagulant to use for the rest of the pregnancy.

Which is the most appropriate anticoagulant?

  1. A. Warfarin continued at the same dose
  2. B. Subcutaneous enoxaparin
  3. C. Rivaroxaban 20 mg once daily
  4. D. Dabigatran 150 mg twice daily
  5. E. Aspirin 75 mg daily
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Subcutaneous enoxaparin

Warfarin is a small lipophilic molecule that crosses the placenta. Exposure between about 6 and 12 weeks can cause warfarin embryopathy (nasal hypoplasia and stippled epiphyses), and later exposure can cause central nervous system abnormalities and fetal haemorrhage. Direct oral anticoagulants also cross the placenta and are not recommended. Heparins are large, highly charged molecules that do not cross the placenta and are safe for the fetus. Low-molecular-weight heparin (for example enoxaparin, which binds antithrombin and so mainly inhibits factor Xa) is preferred to unfractionated heparin because of predictable dose response, no routine monitoring, once- or twice-daily subcutaneous injection and lower risks of heparin-induced thrombocytopenia and osteoporosis. It is stopped about 24 hours before planned delivery so that neuraxial anaesthesia is safe, and treatment continues for at least six weeks after delivery. Warfarin and heparins are both compatible with breastfeeding. Women with mechanical valves need specialist advice.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Warfarin continued at the same dose: Warfarin crosses the placenta and causes embryopathy and fetal bleeding, especially at 6-12 weeks.
  • C. Rivaroxaban 20 mg once daily: Direct oral anticoagulants cross the placenta and are contraindicated in pregnancy.
  • D. Dabigatran 150 mg twice daily: It also crosses the placenta and is avoided in pregnancy.
  • E. Aspirin 75 mg daily: Aspirin does not give adequate protection after a recent deep vein thrombosis.

Key point: Choose low-molecular-weight heparin for anticoagulation in pregnancy and explain why warfarin is avoided.

Question 2 · CNS, psychiatric and anaesthetic drugs · medium

A 24-year-old woman from Swat with schizophrenia has been stable for eight months on olanzapine 20 mg daily. She has gained 14 kg and her body mass index is now 31 kg/m² (18.5-24.9). Fasting glucose is 124 mg/dL (70-110), HbA1c is 6.4% (4.0-5.6), triglycerides are 260 mg/dL (below 150) and waist circumference is 98 cm. Her psychosis remains controlled and prolactin is normal. She is distressed by the weight and considers stopping tablets. She has no cardiac disease and does not smoke.

What is the most appropriate change?

  1. A. Switch to clozapine
  2. B. Switch to quetiapine
  3. C. Increase olanzapine to 30 mg with a diet plan
  4. D. Switch to risperidone and add bromocriptine
  5. E. Switch to aripiprazole with lifestyle support
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Switch to aripiprazole with lifestyle support

Second-generation antipsychotics differ in metabolic risk. Olanzapine and clozapine cause the most weight gain, dyslipidaemia and insulin resistance, through H1, 5-HT2C and muscarinic M3 blockade that increases appetite and impairs insulin secretion; quetiapine and risperidone are intermediate; aripiprazole, lurasidone, ziprasidone and cariprazine carry the least risk. Monitor weight, waist, blood pressure, glucose or HbA1c and lipids at baseline, at 12 weeks and yearly. When a patient develops significant weight gain with controlled psychosis, switching to a metabolically neutral drug such as aripiprazole, alongside diet and exercise, is the best strategy, and metformin is a useful adjunct. Switch gradually by cross-tapering to avoid relapse or withdrawal. Aripiprazole is a partial D2 agonist and can cause akathisia, insomnia and nausea. Clozapine is reserved for resistant illness, and increasing the dose or adding high-risk drugs would worsen her metabolic state. Her HbA1c of 6.4% is in the prediabetic range and needs follow-up.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Switch to clozapine: Carries the highest metabolic risk alongside olanzapine and is reserved for resistant illness.
  • B. Switch to quetiapine: Still causes significant weight gain and dyslipidaemia, so it is a modest improvement at best.
  • C. Increase olanzapine to 30 mg with a diet plan: Higher doses worsen weight and glucose and exceed the licensed maximum.
  • D. Switch to risperidone and add bromocriptine: Risperidone raises prolactin and still causes weight gain, and bromocriptine could worsen her psychosis.

Key point: Switch to a metabolically neutral antipsychotic when weight gain and glucose intolerance develop.

Question 3 · Cardiovascular drugs · medium

A 49-year-old woman from Abbottabad has hypertension and a persistent dry cough that wakes her at night. She has taken enalapril 10 mg twice daily for five months. Blood pressure is 154/96 mmHg, creatinine is 0.9 mg/dL (0.6-1.1) and potassium is 4.2 mEq/L (3.5-5.0). The cough has no other cause. Her doctor stops enalapril and starts losartan 50 mg daily. A medical student asks which pattern of plasma changes is expected two weeks after the switch, compared with her state on enalapril.

Which pattern of plasma changes is expected on losartan?

  1. A. Renin lowered, angiotensin II lowered, bradykinin raised
  2. B. Renin raised, angiotensin II lowered, bradykinin raised
  3. C. Renin unchanged, angiotensin II lowered, bradykinin unchanged
  4. D. Renin raised, angiotensin II raised, bradykinin unchanged
  5. E. Renin raised, angiotensin II unchanged, bradykinin raised
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. Renin raised, angiotensin II raised, bradykinin unchanged

Losartan, valsartan and other ARBs block the AT1 receptor, so angiotensin II, however it is made, cannot cause vasoconstriction, aldosterone release or sodium retention. Because angiotensin II normally inhibits renin secretion, blockade removes this negative feedback and plasma renin and angiotensin II rise. The AT2 receptor remains exposed to the extra angiotensin II. In contrast, an ACE inhibitor lowers angiotensin II and raises renin and bradykinin. ARBs do not inhibit kininase II, so cough is rare and angioedema much less common. The patient therefore gets similar blood pressure lowering without the cough. ARBs share the other class effects: hyperkalaemia, fall in glomerular filtration rate in renal artery stenosis or volume depletion, and fetal toxicity, so they must be avoided in pregnancy. Check creatinine and potassium one to two weeks after starting.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Renin lowered, angiotensin II lowered, bradykinin raised: Renin falls with aliskiren or beta-blockers, not with an AT1 blocker; losartan removes negative feedback so renin rises, and it does not raise bradykinin.
  • B. Renin raised, angiotensin II lowered, bradykinin raised: Is the pattern of the ACE inhibitor she has just stopped; losartan does not block formation of angiotensin II, so its level does not fall.
  • C. Renin unchanged, angiotensin II lowered, bradykinin unchanged: Losartan blocks the receptor, not the enzyme, so angiotensin II is not lowered, and the loss of feedback changes renin release.
  • E. Renin raised, angiotensin II unchanged, bradykinin raised: Bradykinin rises only when ACE is inhibited; she has stopped enalapril, and losartan does not act on kinin breakdown.

Key point: Compare the hormonal effects of an ARB with those of an ACE inhibitor.

Question 4 · Endocrine and reproductive drugs · medium

A 71-year-old woman from Skardu has severe osteoporosis with two new vertebral fractures despite three years of weekly alendronate with good adherence. Her spine T-score is -3.4. Calcium is 9.4 mg/dL (8.5-10.5), 25-hydroxyvitamin D is 34 ng/mL (30-100) and creatinine is 0.8 mg/dL (0.6-1.1). Her specialist starts teriparatide 20 micrograms by subcutaneous injection once daily. A trainee points out that excess parathyroid hormone in primary hyperparathyroidism causes bone loss, and asks why this drug builds bone.

Which feature of its use best explains the anabolic effect?

  1. A. Continuous exposure that keeps osteoclast formation switched off
  2. B. Binding and inhibition of RANK ligand
  3. C. Blockade of the calcium-sensing receptor on the parathyroid gland
  4. D. Brief daily peaks of the hormone
  5. E. Inhibition of sclerostin by a monoclonal antibody
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. Brief daily peaks of the hormone

Teriparatide is recombinant human parathyroid hormone (1-34). Continuous high levels, as in hyperparathyroidism, raise RANKL expression by osteoblast-lineage cells and stimulate osteoclasts, causing net bone loss. Intermittent exposure from a daily injection gives a brief rise that favours osteoblast activity, so formation outpaces resorption and trabecular bone, especially in the vertebrae, increases. It is used for severe osteoporosis after failure of antiresorptive drugs or with multiple fractures, for up to 24 months, and is followed by an antiresorptive to hold the gains. Adverse effects are nausea, dizziness, leg cramps, transient hypercalcaemia and hyperuricaemia. It is contraindicated with hypercalcaemia, Paget disease, previous skeletal radiotherapy, bone metastases and unexplained high alkaline phosphatase, because of an osteosarcoma signal in rats. Romosozumab is avoided after recent heart attack or stroke.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Continuous exposure that keeps osteoclast formation switched off: Continuous parathyroid hormone exposure raises RANKL and osteoclast activity, producing bone loss as in hyperparathyroidism.
  • B. Binding and inhibition of RANK ligand: Is the mechanism of denosumab, not of a parathyroid hormone fragment.
  • C. Blockade of the calcium-sensing receptor on the parathyroid gland: Describes cinacalcet, which lowers parathyroid hormone and is not an anabolic bone drug.
  • E. Inhibition of sclerostin by a monoclonal antibody: Describes romosozumab, a different anabolic agent given monthly.

Key point: Explain why intermittent parathyroid hormone exposure is anabolic for bone.

Question 5 · General pharmacology and toxicology · medium

A 62-year-old man with metastatic prostate cancer in Dera Ismail Khan has taken oral morphine for bone pain for eight months. His dose has been increased from 30 mg to 240 mg daily to keep the pain controlled. He is alert and not drowsy, his respiratory rate is 14/min and he still feels pain relief with each dose. His pupils are 2 mm and he has not passed stool for four days despite eating normally. His family worry that the high dose will make him stop breathing.

Which effect of morphine shows the least tolerance in this patient?

  1. A. Respiratory depression
  2. B. Sedation
  3. C. Constipation
  4. D. Euphoria
  5. E. Nausea and vomiting
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Constipation

Tolerance is a reduced response to a drug after repeated exposure, so a higher dose is needed for the same effect. With opioids it involves receptor desensitisation and uncoupling from G proteins, receptor internalisation and adaptive changes in cyclic AMP signalling, and it develops for analgesia, euphoria, sedation, nausea and respiratory depression. This is why doses can safely rise far above those that would stop breathing in an opioid-naive patient. Tolerance is slow or absent for miosis and for constipation, which are mediated by receptors in the pupillary nucleus and in the gut wall, so these effects persist indefinitely and a regular stimulant or osmotic laxative should be started with the opioid. Cross-tolerance between opioids is incomplete. Physical dependence develops alongside tolerance, so abrupt withdrawal or giving naloxone precipitates a withdrawal syndrome. Doses should be titrated to pain relief, and fear of respiratory depression should not lead to under-treatment of cancer pain.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Respiratory depression: Tolerance to respiratory depression develops with repeated dosing, which is why his respiratory rate is a normal 14/min on a dose that would depress breathing in an opioid-naive patient.
  • B. Sedation: He is alert on 240 mg daily, showing that tolerance to the sedative effect has developed.
  • D. Euphoria: Tolerance to the mood effect develops, and he reports pain relief rather than a high from each dose.
  • E. Nausea and vomiting: Nausea usually fades within days to weeks as tolerance develops, and he has no vomiting after eight months of treatment.

Key point: Identify which opioid effects show tolerance and which persist during long-term use.

Question 6 · Gastrointestinal drugs · easy

A 38-year-old shopkeeper in Lahore has burning chest pain after meals and at night. He was prescribed omeprazole 20 mg daily, which he takes at bedtime after his last cup of tea. After four weeks his symptoms are only slightly better. Endoscopy shows mild oesophagitis, and he has no alarm features. His doctor changes only the timing of his tablet.

When should he take omeprazole?

  1. A. At bedtime
  2. B. After meals
  3. C. 30-60 minutes before breakfast
  4. D. With an antacid
  5. E. When pain starts
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. 30-60 minutes before breakfast

Omeprazole is a prodrug that is activated in the acid space of the parietal cell canaliculus and binds covalently to H+/K+ ATPase pumps that are active at that time. Pumps are recruited to the membrane after a meal, especially after the overnight fast, so taking the drug 30-60 minutes before breakfast lets the peak blood level coincide with the most active pumps. Because binding is irreversible, acid secretion returns only as new pumps are made, which is why once-daily dosing works despite a short plasma half-life of about an hour. Full effect takes 3-5 days. Taken at bedtime with no meal, much of the dose meets inactive pumps.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. At bedtime: Proton pumps are active only when stimulated by food; at bedtime few are active, so less drug binds.
  • B. After meals: After a meal the pumps have already peaked, and the capsule is already enteric-coated.
  • D. With an antacid: An antacid does not speed absorption and is not needed with it.
  • E. When pain starts: Proton pump inhibitors need several days of regular dosing for full effect, so on-demand use is weaker for oesophagitis.

Key point: Proton pump inhibitors work best taken 30-60 minutes before the first meal, when pumps are activated.

Question 7 · Autacoids, NSAIDs, gout and immunosuppressants · easy

A 62-year-old woman with open-angle glaucoma has used latanoprost drops in her right eye only for two years, with good control of her eye pressure. Her husband notices that her right iris has become browner than the left, and her right eyelashes have grown longer and thicker. Her vision and eye pressure are stable.

How does latanoprost lower eye pressure?

  1. A. It reduces aqueous production by beta blockade
  2. B. It inhibits carbonic anhydrase
  3. C. It increases uveoscleral outflow
  4. D. It constricts the pupil to open the angle
  5. E. It is an alpha-2 agonist
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. It increases uveoscleral outflow

Latanoprost, bimatoprost and travoprost are prostaglandin F2-alpha analogues. They lower intraocular pressure mainly by increasing uveoscleral outflow of aqueous humour (through the ciliary muscle), and are often first-line because they are given once daily at night and work well. Local side effects include permanent darkening of the iris (more melanin in melanocytes, most noticeable in hazel or mixed-colour eyes), darkening of the eyelid skin, longer and thicker eyelashes, red eyes, and rarely macular oedema or uveitis. Other glaucoma drugs reduce aqueous production (beta-blockers, carbonic anhydrase inhibitors, alpha-2 agonists) or increase trabecular outflow (pilocarpine).

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. It reduces aqueous production by beta blockade: Reducing aqueous production by beta blockade is the action of timolol.
  • B. It inhibits carbonic anhydrase: Inhibiting carbonic anhydrase is the action of dorzolamide.
  • D. It constricts the pupil to open the angle: Constricting the pupil to open the angle is the action of pilocarpine.
  • E. It is an alpha-2 agonist: Alpha-2 agonism is the action of brimonidine.

Key point: Prostaglandin analogues such as latanoprost increase uveoscleral outflow; they darken the iris and lengthen eyelashes.

Question 8 · Anticancer and immunomodulating drugs · medium

A 45-year-old man from Mardan with acute myeloid leukaemia receives high-dose cytarabine 3 g/m² every 12 hours on days 1, 3 and 5. After the first dose his creatinine had risen from 0.9 to 1.8 mg/dL (0.7-1.3). On day 4 he develops an unsteady gait, slurred speech, nystagmus and an intention tremor, though he is alert and oriented. His eyes are red and gritty. CT of the head is normal and platelets are 45 × 10⁹/L (150-400).

What is the most likely cause of his neurological signs?

  1. A. Cytarabine cerebellar toxicity, worse in renal impairment
  2. B. Wernicke encephalopathy from poor intake and thiamine deficiency
  3. C. Leukaemic infiltration of the cerebellum and meninges
  4. D. Delayed neurotoxicity from earlier intrathecal methotrexate
  5. E. Cerebellar haemorrhage caused by severe thrombocytopenia
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Cytarabine cerebellar toxicity, worse in renal impairment

Cytarabine is a pyrimidine nucleoside analogue that is phosphorylated to ara-CTP, which inhibits DNA polymerase and is incorporated into DNA, killing cells in S phase. It is deaminated to inactive uracil arabinoside by cytidine deaminase, and renal clearance of the metabolite is reduced in kidney impairment. High-dose cytarabine can cause severe cerebellar toxicity (ataxia, dysarthria, nystagmus, tremor) that may be permanent; the risk rises with age over 50, renal dysfunction and cumulative dose, and cytarabine must be stopped at the first sign. Other effects are myelosuppression, fever and rash (cytarabine syndrome), hepatotoxicity and keratoconjunctivitis, prevented by corticosteroid eye drops during treatment. Check creatinine before every dose and reduce the dose when it is raised. Cytarabine has no oral formulation because of first-pass deamination.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Wernicke encephalopathy from poor intake and thiamine deficiency: Typically brings confusion, ophthalmoplegia and ataxia; he is alert and there is no ophthalmoplegia.
  • C. Leukaemic infiltration of the cerebellum and meninges: Would not appear abruptly on day 4 of treatment, and the CT scan is normal.
  • D. Delayed neurotoxicity from earlier intrathecal methotrexate: He has not received intrathecal methotrexate, so this does not apply.
  • E. Cerebellar haemorrhage caused by severe thrombocytopenia: A haemorrhage would be visible on a CT scan, which is normal.

Key point: Recognise cytarabine cerebellar toxicity and its renal risk factor.

Question 9 · Diuretics and renal drugs · easy

A 34-year-old man is trekking from Askole towards K2 base camp in Gilgit-Baltistan. At 3,500 metres he develops headache, nausea and poor sleep. He has no breathlessness at rest, no confusion and no unsteadiness, and his symptoms ease a little after rest. He plans to continue to 4,600 metres and asks a doctor in his group for a tablet to help him acclimatise. He is advised to take acetazolamide, 125 mg twice daily, and to ascend slowly.

Which effect of acetazolamide mainly explains its benefit?

  1. A. Mild metabolic acidosis from bicarbonate loss, driving breathing
  2. B. Metabolic alkalosis from chloride loss, which improves oxygen delivery
  3. C. Direct dilatation of cerebral vessels by retained carbon dioxide
  4. D. A rise in erythropoietin that quickly increases the haemoglobin
  5. E. Contraction of plasma volume that reduces brain swelling
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Mild metabolic acidosis from bicarbonate loss, driving breathing

Carbonic anhydrase in the proximal tubule converts filtered bicarbonate and hydrogen ions to carbon dioxide and water so that bicarbonate can be reabsorbed. Acetazolamide inhibits the enzyme, so bicarbonate, sodium and water are excreted, producing an alkaline urine, a mild hyperchloraemic metabolic acidosis and a weak diuresis that wanes after a few days as acid-base balance adjusts. The acidosis stimulates central and peripheral chemoreceptors and increases ventilation, which raises arterial oxygen at altitude and counters the respiratory alkalosis that otherwise blunts the drive to breathe, especially at night. Acetazolamide therefore prevents and treats acute mountain sickness, and it also reduces aqueous humour and cerebrospinal fluid formation. Adverse effects include paraesthesia, altered taste with fizzy drinks, hypokalaemia, renal stones and, rarely, sulphonamide hypersensitivity. Slow ascent is the basis of prevention; descent and dexamethasone treat severe cases.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Metabolic alkalosis from chloride loss, which improves oxygen delivery: Acetazolamide causes acidosis, not alkalosis, and alkalosis would reduce rather than stimulate his breathing at altitude.
  • C. Direct dilatation of cerebral vessels by retained carbon dioxide: It does not act by retaining carbon dioxide; increased ventilation lowers carbon dioxide and its main benefit is acid-base driven.
  • D. A rise in erythropoietin that quickly increases the haemoglobin: Erythropoietin-driven haemoglobin rise takes days to weeks and is not the action of acetazolamide.
  • E. Contraction of plasma volume that reduces brain swelling: Its diuresis is weak and short-lived, and volume contraction is not the main mechanism of benefit at altitude.

Key point: Explain how carbonic anhydrase inhibition produces metabolic acidosis and increases ventilation at altitude.

Question 10 · Respiratory drugs · easy

A 30-year-old man with asthma uses his salbutamol inhaler three or four times every day and has needed a new one each month. He wakes at night with cough twice a week. He has a steroid inhaler at home but rarely uses it. His hands shake after using salbutamol.

What does his reliever use indicate?

  1. A. His asthma is poorly controlled
  2. B. His asthma is well controlled
  3. C. He has developed COPD
  4. D. Salbutamol has stopped working
  5. E. He needs a higher salbutamol dose
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. His asthma is poorly controlled

Short-acting beta-2 agonists (salbutamol, terbutaline) relieve bronchospasm within minutes and last 4-6 hours. Needing them more than twice a week, waking at night, or using more than one inhaler a month means asthma is poorly controlled and the risk of severe attacks is high. The next steps are checking adherence and inhaler technique, avoiding triggers, and starting or stepping up regular inhaled corticosteroid, often as a combination inhaler. Side effects of beta-2 agonists: tremor, palpitations, tachycardia, hypokalaemia and headache. Regular overuse of SABA alone is linked to more asthma deaths.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. His asthma is well controlled: Daily reliever use means poor control.
  • C. He has developed COPD: Nothing here shows COPD; heavy reliever use means his asthma is poorly controlled.
  • D. Salbutamol has stopped working: It still relieves symptoms.
  • E. He needs a higher salbutamol dose: More reliever is not the answer.

Key point: Frequent reliever (SABA) use signals poorly controlled asthma: check adherence and technique, and step up inhaled steroids.
