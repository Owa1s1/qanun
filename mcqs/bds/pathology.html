Question 1 · Genetic, paediatric and environmental disease · medium

A 25-year-old woman has a massive spleen reaching her pelvis, easy bruising, platelets of 58 x10⁹/L and episodes of severe bone pain in her thighs. X-ray shows flaring of the lower femurs. Her parents are cousins. Marrow shows large macrophages with cytoplasm like crumpled tissue paper. Glucocerebrosidase activity is low.

What is the diagnosis and treatment?

  1. A. Niemann-Pick disease; liver transplant
  2. B. Tay-Sachs disease; no organomegaly
  3. C. Gaucher disease; enzyme replacement therapy
  4. D. Hurler syndrome; bone marrow transplant
  5. E. Chronic myeloid leukaemia; imatinib
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Gaucher disease; enzyme replacement therapy

Gaucher disease (autosomal recessive, GBA gene; commonest in Ashkenazi Jews): glucocerebroside accumulates in macrophages (Gaucher cells with "crumpled tissue paper" cytoplasm) in spleen, liver and marrow, causing hepatosplenomegaly, cytopenias, bone pain and crises, Erlenmeyer flask femurs, and avascular necrosis. Type 1 spares the brain. Treatment: recombinant enzyme (imiglucerase) or substrate reduction (eliglustat). GBA carriers have higher Parkinson disease risk.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Niemann-Pick disease; liver transplant: Niemann-Pick disease involves sphingomyelinase and foamy cells, often with brain disease.
  • B. Tay-Sachs disease; no organomegaly: Tay-Sachs disease affects neurons without a large spleen.
  • D. Hurler syndrome; bone marrow transplant: Hurler syndrome is a mucopolysaccharidosis with coarse features and corneal clouding in early childhood.
  • E. Chronic myeloid leukaemia; imatinib: Chronic myeloid leukaemia shows a high white count; her marrow shows storage macrophages.

Key point: Gaucher disease: glucocerebrosidase deficiency, crumpled-paper macrophages, big spleen, bone crises.

Question 2 · Haemodynamic disorders · medium

Three patients in an emergency room are compared. Patient A has vomited a large amount of blood from an ulcer; B has an anterior myocardial infarction with crackles to both lung bases; C has a urinary infection with fever. All have a systolic pressure of 75 mmHg. A and B have cold, clammy skin and slow capillary refill; C has warm, flushed skin and bounding pulses.

Which statement is correct?

  1. A. All three have high cardiac output
  2. B. C has low systemic vascular resistance
  3. C. B has a low jugular venous pressure
  4. D. A has a high central venous pressure
  5. E. All three have high vascular resistance
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. C has low systemic vascular resistance

Shock is systemic hypoperfusion from low cardiac output or low effective circulating volume. Hypovolaemic shock (bleeding, burns, vomiting): low volume, low CVP, low output, high systemic vascular resistance (cold peripheries). Cardiogenic (infarction, arrhythmia) and obstructive (tamponade, massive PE): low output with high filling pressures and high SVR. Distributive shock (septic, anaphylactic, neurogenic): vasodilatation with low SVR, often high output and warm peripheries in early sepsis. Septic shock is driven by microbial products activating cytokines (TNF, IL-1), nitric oxide, endothelial injury, DIC and metabolic failure.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. All three have high cardiac output: A and B have low cardiac output, with cold clammy skin.
  • C. B has a low jugular venous pressure: Pump failure in B raises venous pressure, with crackles and distended neck veins.
  • D. A has a high central venous pressure: Blood loss in A lowers central venous pressure.
  • E. All three have high vascular resistance: C is vasodilated, with warm skin and bounding pulses, so resistance is low.

Key point: Hypovolaemic and cardiogenic shock have low output and high SVR; septic (distributive) shock has low SVR with warm peripheries.

Question 3 · Immunopathology · medium

A 3-day-old baby has seizures and twitching. He was born blue and a loud murmur was heard. Calcium is 6.1 mg/dL and parathyroid hormone undetectable. He has a cleft palate, low-set ears, a small jaw and a heart defect (truncus arteriosus). Chest X-ray shows no thymic shadow. Lymphocyte counts are low.

What is the underlying defect?

  1. A. Defective ADA enzyme
  2. B. Absent B cells
  3. C. 22q11.2 deletion
  4. D. WAS gene mutation
  5. E. ATM gene mutation
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. 22q11.2 deletion

DiGeorge syndrome (22q11.2 deletion) is a failure of development of the third and fourth pharyngeal pouches: thymic hypoplasia (T cell deficiency, viral and fungal infections), parathyroid hypoplasia (hypocalcaemia), conotruncal heart defects (truncus arteriosus, tetralogy of Fallot) and facial anomalies; psychiatric illness is common later. SCID: severe T and B cell failure (X-linked IL-2 receptor gamma chain, or ADA deficiency) with infections from birth. Wiskott-Aldrich: eczema, thrombocytopenia with small platelets, infections (WAS gene, X-linked). Ataxia telangiectasia: ataxia, telangiectasia, IgA deficiency, and cancer (ATM, DNA repair).

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Defective ADA enzyme: ADA deficiency causes SCID without heart defects, cleft palate or low calcium.
  • B. Absent B cells: Absent B cells (Bruton disease) cause infections after 6 months, not neonatal seizures and heart defects.
  • D. WAS gene mutation: WAS mutations cause eczema, small platelets and infections in boys.
  • E. ATM gene mutation: ATM mutations cause ataxia and telangiectasia in childhood.

Key point: DiGeorge (22q11.2): thymic and parathyroid hypoplasia, conotruncal heart defects and facial anomalies.

Question 4 · Inflammation and repair · medium

A 38-year-old woman notices a firm, slightly tender lump in her right iliac fossa, at the site of an appendix scar from surgery 10 years ago. Excision shows multinucleated giant cells with scattered nuclei surrounding refractile, birefringent fragments under polarised light, with fibrosis around them. There is no necrosis, and stains for organisms are negative.

What is this lesion?

  1. A. A foreign-body granuloma
  2. B. A caseating tuberculous granuloma
  3. C. A keloid
  4. D. A sarcoid granuloma
  5. E. An abscess
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. A foreign-body granuloma

Foreign-body granulomas form around material too large to be phagocytosed by a single macrophage and inert enough not to provoke a strong immune response: sutures, talc (in intravenous drug users), splinters, silica, keratin from ruptured cysts, and urate crystals (tophi). Macrophages fuse into foreign-body giant cells with nuclei scattered through the cytoplasm, and the material can often be seen with polarised light. They differ from immune granulomas (tuberculosis, sarcoid) which are T cell-mediated and often contain Langhans giant cells (nuclei arranged in a horseshoe).

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. A caseating tuberculous granuloma: Tuberculous granulomas show caseation and organisms, both absent here.
  • C. A keloid: Keloids are dense collagen without giant cells around foreign material.
  • D. A sarcoid granuloma: Sarcoid granulomas are tight, non-caseating and contain no foreign material.
  • E. An abscess: An abscess contains neutrophils and pus, which are absent.

Key point: Foreign-body granulomas contain giant cells around inert, often birefringent, material without a T cell response.

Question 5 · Neoplasia · medium

A 64-year-old man with metastatic colorectal cancer is treated with chemotherapy plus an intravenous antibody that binds a growth factor made by hypoxic tumour cells to draw in new vessels. His liver metastases shrink. Afterwards, his blood pressure rises to 165/100, protein appears in his urine, and a small wound from a port insertion is slow to heal.

What does the drug target?

  1. A. EGFR
  2. B. HER2
  3. C. VEGF
  4. D. CD20
  5. E. PD-1
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. VEGF

Tumours larger than about 1-2 mm need new blood vessels. Hypoxia stabilises HIF-1alpha, which raises VEGF; oncogenes (RAS, MYC) also increase it, and loss of p53 reduces inhibitors (thrombospondin-1). Tumour vessels are leaky, tortuous and dilated. Bevacizumab (anti-VEGF) and VEGF receptor tyrosine kinase inhibitors (sunitinib) are used in colorectal, renal and other cancers. Side effects reflect VEGF's role in normal vessels: hypertension, proteinuria, impaired wound healing, bleeding and bowel perforation. In von Hippel-Lindau disease, loss of VHL stabilises HIF, causing vascular tumours.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. EGFR: Anti-EGFR antibodies (cetuximab) cause an acne-like rash, not hypertension and proteinuria.
  • B. HER2: HER2 is targeted in breast and gastric cancer.
  • D. CD20: Anti-CD20 is used in B cell lymphoma.
  • E. PD-1: Anti-PD-1 causes immune side effects such as colitis and thyroiditis.

Key point: Hypoxia drives VEGF via HIF-1alpha; anti-VEGF drugs cause hypertension, proteinuria and poor wound healing.

Question 6 · Cell injury, adaptation and death · easy

A 30-year-old motorcyclist fractured his tibia and spent 8 weeks in a long-leg plaster without bearing weight. When the plaster is removed, his calf on that side is visibly thinner and 4 cm smaller in circumference than the other, and the leg feels weak. Nerve conduction is normal. A research biopsy shows that his muscle fibres are smaller but the number of fibres is unchanged, with no necrosis or inflammation.

Which process mainly reduces muscle size?

  1. A. Necrosis of muscle fibres
  2. B. Proteasome breakdown and autophagy
  3. C. Apoptosis of whole fibres
  4. D. Replacement of muscle by fat cells
  5. E. Metaplasia of muscle into fibrous tissue
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Proteasome breakdown and autophagy

Atrophy is shrinkage of cells through loss of structural components. Causes: disuse, denervation, reduced blood supply, poor nutrition, loss of endocrine stimulation (endometrium after menopause), pressure and ageing. Mechanisms: reduced protein synthesis and increased protein breakdown through the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway, plus autophagy (self-eating of organelles in autophagic vacuoles, which can leave lipofuscin, "brown atrophy"). Disuse atrophy is reversible with rehabilitation; prolonged denervation leads to fibre loss and fibrosis.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Necrosis of muscle fibres: Necrosis would show dead fibres with inflammation and a raised CK; his fibres are intact.
  • C. Apoptosis of whole fibres: Apoptosis of whole fibres would reduce their number; the count is unchanged.
  • D. Replacement of muscle by fat cells: Fat replaces muscle late in denervation or dystrophy, not after 8 weeks of disuse.
  • E. Metaplasia of muscle into fibrous tissue: Muscle fibres do not change into another cell type; damaged muscle heals by regeneration or scarring.

Key point: Atrophy is cell shrinkage through ubiquitin-proteasome protein breakdown and autophagy.

Question 7 · Genetic, paediatric and environmental disease · easy

A newborn boy is floppy and feeds slowly. He has upslanting palpebral fissures, a flat facial profile with a small nose, a single palmar crease, a gap between the first and second toes, and a heart murmur; echo shows an atrioventricular septal defect. The mother is 43 years old and had no antenatal screening.

Which chromosomal finding is most likely?

  1. A. Trisomy 21
  2. B. Trisomy 18
  3. C. Trisomy 13
  4. D. 45,X
  5. E. 47,XXY
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Trisomy 21

Down syndrome (trisomy 21) is the commonest chromosomal disorder; most cases arise from maternal meiotic non-disjunction, linked to maternal age; a few are due to Robertsonian translocation (which can recur in families) or mosaicism. Features: learning disability, flat face, epicanthic folds, single palmar crease, atrioventricular septal defects, duodenal atresia and Hirschsprung disease, a raised risk of acute leukaemia (especially megakaryoblastic in early childhood), early Alzheimer disease, hypothyroidism and immune defects. Trisomy 18 (Edwards): rocker-bottom feet, clenched fists, micrognathia. Trisomy 13 (Patau): holoprosencephaly, cleft lip and palate, polydactyly. Klinefelter (47,XXY): tall, small testes, infertility. Turner (45,X): short stature, webbed neck, coarctation, streak ovaries.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Trisomy 18: Trisomy 18 gives clenched fists with overlapping fingers, rocker-bottom feet and a small jaw.
  • C. Trisomy 13: Trisomy 13 gives cleft lip and palate, extra fingers and scalp defects.
  • D. 45,X: 45,X gives short stature, neck webbing and puffy hands and feet in girls.
  • E. 47,XXY: 47,XXY is usually not obvious at birth; it shows later as tall stature and small testes.

Key point: Trisomy 21 is linked to maternal age and causes AV septal defects, duodenal atresia, leukaemia and early Alzheimer disease.

Question 8 · Haemodynamic disorders · easy

A 7-year-old boy from Quetta has had puffy eyes in the morning and swollen ankles by evening for 2 weeks, after a cold. His trousers feel tight around the waist. Urine is frothy. Urine dipstick shows 4+ protein with no blood, and serum albumin is 1.5 g/dL; cholesterol is 380 mg/dL. Blood pressure and jugular venous pressure are normal, and creatinine is normal.

What is the main mechanism of the oedema?

  1. A. Low plasma oncotic pressure
  2. B. Raised venous hydrostatic pressure
  3. C. Lymphatic blockage
  4. D. Leaky vessels from inflammation
  5. E. Excess capillary blood flow
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Low plasma oncotic pressure

Oedema results from four mechanisms acting on Starling forces: raised hydrostatic pressure (heart failure, venous obstruction such as DVT, fluid overload in renal failure); reduced plasma oncotic pressure (albumin loss in nephrotic syndrome or protein-losing enteropathy, poor synthesis in cirrhosis, malnutrition such as kwashiorkor); lymphatic obstruction (filariasis, tumour, surgery or radiation, giving protein-rich lymphoedema); and sodium and water retention (renal failure, activation of renin-angiotensin-aldosterone in heart failure). Non-inflammatory oedema is a transudate; inflammatory oedema is an exudate.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Raised venous hydrostatic pressure: Raised venous pressure, as in heart failure, would raise the JVP; his is normal.
  • C. Lymphatic blockage: Lymphatic blockage causes firm, protein-rich swelling of one limb, not puffy eyes with heavy proteinuria.
  • D. Leaky vessels from inflammation: Inflammatory leak brings redness, warmth and pain at one site; he has painless generalised swelling.
  • E. Excess capillary blood flow: Increased blood flow (hyperaemia) reddens tissue but does not cause generalised pitting oedema.

Key point: Oedema arises from high hydrostatic pressure, low oncotic pressure, lymphatic obstruction, or sodium retention; nephrotic syndrome acts by low albumin.

Question 9 · Immunopathology · medium

A newborn has a slow heart rate (45/min) from complete heart block, which was first heard as a slow fetal heart at 22 weeks. The heart is otherwise structurally normal on echo. The mother is well but has a mild photosensitive rash on her face and dry eyes, and has never been tested for autoimmune disease.

Which maternal antibody most likely caused this?

  1. A. Anti-dsDNA
  2. B. Anti-Ro (SS-A)
  3. C. Anti-Jo-1
  4. D. Anti-centromere
  5. E. Anti-TPO
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Anti-Ro (SS-A)

Neonatal lupus occurs when maternal IgG anti-Ro (SS-A) or anti-La (SS-B) crosses the placenta. The baby may have a transient photosensitive rash, cytopenias and liver changes, but congenital complete heart block is permanent, caused by inflammation and fibrosis of the conducting system, and may need a pacemaker. Mothers may have Sjögren syndrome, SLE or no symptoms. Pregnant women with anti-Ro are monitored with fetal echocardiography; hydroxychloroquine reduces risk.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Anti-dsDNA: Anti-dsDNA is linked to lupus nephritis and does not damage the fetal conducting system.
  • C. Anti-Jo-1: Anti-Jo-1 is linked to myositis with interstitial lung disease.
  • D. Anti-centromere: Anti-centromere is linked to limited systemic sclerosis.
  • E. Anti-TPO: Anti-TPO is linked to autoimmune thyroid disease, not fetal heart block.

Key point: Maternal anti-Ro can cross the placenta and cause permanent congenital heart block in neonatal lupus.

Question 10 · Inflammation and repair · easy

A 9-year-old boy playing in a garden in Islamabad is stung on the forearm by a bee. His mother scrapes out the sting. Within minutes his forearm is red, warm and swollen around the sting, and it hurts. There is no wheeze, no swelling of the lips, and his pulse and blood pressure are normal. The swelling is soft and pits slightly when pressed.

Which vascular change mainly causes the swelling?

  1. A. Arteriolar constriction
  2. B. Increased venular permeability
  3. C. Lymphatic obstruction
  4. D. Lower plasma protein levels
  5. E. Higher venous pressure from heart failure
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Increased venular permeability

The cardinal signs of acute inflammation are redness (rubor), heat (calor), swelling (tumor), pain (dolor) and loss of function. Vascular changes come first: a brief arteriolar constriction, then vasodilatation (histamine, nitric oxide, prostaglandins), giving redness and heat; then increased permeability, mainly by endothelial cell contraction in postcapillary venules (histamine, bradykinin, leukotrienes), which lets protein-rich fluid (exudate) escape, causing swelling. Slowing of flow (stasis) follows, allowing leukocytes to margin. Pain comes from bradykinin and prostaglandins sensitising nerve endings.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Arteriolar constriction: Arteriolar constriction is brief, lasts seconds and reduces flow; it does not cause swelling.
  • C. Lymphatic obstruction: Lymph flow increases in inflammation to drain the extra fluid; it is not blocked.
  • D. Lower plasma protein levels: Low plasma protein, as in nephrotic syndrome, causes generalised oedema, not a red hot sting site.
  • E. Higher venous pressure from heart failure: Heart failure raises venous pressure in both legs; it does not cause a red swelling at one sting.

Key point: Swelling in acute inflammation comes from increased venular permeability, mainly endothelial contraction, letting protein-rich exudate escape.
