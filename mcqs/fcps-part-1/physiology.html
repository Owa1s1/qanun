Question 1 · Endocrine and reproduction · medium

A 25-year-old woman from Skardu took prednisolone tablets for severe asthma for eight weeks and stopped them suddenly two days ago. She now feels weak, nauseated and dizzy when standing. Her blood pressure is 90/60 mmHg (below 120/80) and her blood glucose is 58 mg/dL (70-110).

How should her course of steroid have been stopped to avoid this?

  1. A. Stopped on the day she felt better, since short courses never cause trouble
  2. B. Changed to inhaled steroid at once with no oral taper
  3. C. A gradual taper, with a steroid card and sick-day advice
  4. D. Replaced immediately with fludrocortisone, which is the missing hormone
  5. E. Continued indefinitely, since the axis never recovers once suppressed
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. A gradual taper, with a steroid card and sick-day advice

Glucocorticoids given for more than a few weeks suppress CRH and ACTH, and the adrenal cortex atrophies. If the drug is stopped abruptly, cortisol cannot be produced and the patient develops the features of adrenal insufficiency: weakness, nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, postural hypotension and, under the stress of infection or surgery, a life-threatening crisis with hypoglycaemia and hypotension. Prevention lies in using the lowest effective dose, tapering gradually once the course has lasted more than about three weeks, issuing a steroid card, teaching sick-day rules and giving extra cover for surgery. Recovery of the axis is assessed clinically and, if needed, with a morning cortisol or a short Synacthen test. Mineralocorticoid secretion is largely preserved, because the renin-angiotensin system remains intact.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Stopped on the day she felt better, since short courses never cause trouble: A course lasting more than about three weeks, or repeated courses, can suppress the axis, so this one could not simply be stopped.
  • B. Changed to inhaled steroid at once with no oral taper: Inhaled steroid does not replace systemic cortisol; the adrenal glands remain suppressed.
  • D. Replaced immediately with fludrocortisone, which is the missing hormone: The deficiency is of cortisol, and exogenous glucocorticoid does not suppress the renin-aldosterone system to the same degree, so fludrocortisone is not the answer here.
  • E. Continued indefinitely, since the axis never recovers once suppressed: The axis usually recovers, although it may take months.

Key point: Prevent steroid withdrawal adrenal insufficiency by tapering, a steroid card and sick-day rules.

Question 2 · Cell, nerve and muscle · medium

The potassium concentration is about 140 mEq/L inside a nerve cell and 4 mEq/L outside. At body temperature, the Nernst equation is E = −61 × log₁₀ (Cᵢₙ ÷ Cₒᵤₜ) mV for a monovalent cation.

What is the approximate equilibrium potential for potassium?

  1. A. −94 mV
  2. B. −70 mV
  3. C. −35 mV
  4. D. 0 mV
  5. E. +61 mV
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. −94 mV

The Nernst potential is the membrane voltage at which the electrical force exactly balances the concentration gradient for one ion. For K⁺ it is about −94 mV; for Na⁺ (142 out, 14 in) about +61 mV. The resting potential (about −90 mV in large nerve and muscle fibres, about −70 mV in many neurons) lies close to the K⁺ value because the resting membrane is far more permeable to K⁺ than to Na⁺, through leak channels. The Goldman equation combines the ions according to their permeabilities.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. −70 mV: About -70 mV is a typical neuron resting potential, which sits a little above the K+ equilibrium because the membrane also lets in some Na+.
  • C. −35 mV: -35 mV comes from using the ratio 35 without taking its logarithm and multiplying by 61.
  • D. 0 mV: 0 mV would mean no concentration difference at all; here K+ is 35 times higher inside.
  • E. +61 mV: About +61 mV is the sodium equilibrium potential, which is positive because Na+ is higher outside.

Key point: E_K ≈ −94 mV and E_Na ≈ +61 mV; the resting potential lies near E_K because of high K⁺ permeability.

Question 3 · Gastrointestinal system · medium

A researcher in Gilgit collects blood samples from a volunteer after a meal. Blood from the hepatic portal vein contains a high concentration of bile acids, while blood from a peripheral vein contains very little. The volunteer has a normal liver and gallbladder.

What process returns most of these bile acids to the liver?

  1. A. Enterohepatic circulation
  2. B. De novo hepatic synthesis
  3. C. Gallbladder concentration
  4. D. Bacterial deconjugation in the colon
  5. E. Renal excretion and reabsorption
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Enterohepatic circulation

Most bile acids are not lost in the faeces. After helping to digest fat in the small intestine, they are absorbed mainly in the terminal ileum. From there they enter the hepatic portal vein and return to the liver, which extracts them and resecretes them into bile. This cycle is called enterohepatic circulation. The gallbladder stores and concentrates bile between meals, but the return of bile acids from the gut happens through this circulation. The liver synthesizes only enough new bile acids to replace the small amount lost in faeces.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. De novo hepatic synthesis: The liver makes new bile acids from cholesterol, but this replaces only a small fraction of the daily loss.
  • C. Gallbladder concentration: The gallbladder stores and concentrates bile between meals but does not return bile acids from the gut.
  • D. Bacterial deconjugation in the colon: Colon bacteria can deconjugate bile acids, but this does not return them to the liver.
  • E. Renal excretion and reabsorption: The kidneys excrete only tiny amounts of bile acids and are not the main route back to the liver.

Key point: Describe enterohepatic circulation of bile acids and the role of the terminal ileum.

Question 4 · Nervous system and special senses · medium

A 25-year-old man is stabbed in the back in a street fight. The knife cuts the left half of his spinal cord at T10, shown on MRI, with the right half intact. He is examined the next day for power, reflexes, vibration and joint position, and pinprick and temperature in both legs and on the trunk.

Which pattern of loss is expected below the injury?

  1. A. Left weakness; right pain loss
  2. B. Right weakness and left pain loss
  3. C. Loss of all sensation on both sides
  4. D. Left pain and temperature loss only
  5. E. Bilateral weakness with normal sensation
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Left weakness; right pain loss

In a hemisection of the cord (Brown-Séquard syndrome): the corticospinal tract has crossed in the medulla, so weakness (with upper motor neuron signs) is on the same side; the dorsal columns ascend on the same side, so vibration and proprioception are lost on the same side; the spinothalamic tract carries fibres that have crossed within one or two segments, so pain and temperature are lost on the opposite side, starting a segment or two below the lesion. There may be a band of total loss at the level of the injury.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Right weakness and left pain loss: Weakness appears on the side of the cord lesion (left), not the opposite side.
  • C. Loss of all sensation on both sides: Only half the cord is cut, so sensation is not lost on both sides.
  • D. Left pain and temperature loss only: Pain fibres from the left leg have already crossed to the right side of the cord, so left pain sensation is spared.
  • E. Bilateral weakness with normal sensation: Sensation is affected: vibration and position on the left, and pain and temperature on the right.

Key point: Cord hemisection: ipsilateral weakness and dorsal column loss, contralateral pain and temperature loss.

Question 5 · Renal physiology, body fluids and acid-base · medium

A 40-year-old man from Dera Ismail Khan eats a meal rich in potassium. Two hours later, tests show plasma potassium 5.4 mEq/L (3.5-5.0), sodium 140 mEq/L (135-145) and plasma renin activity normal. His blood pressure is 118/76 mmHg. He is healthy and takes no medication.

What is the next step in potassium handling?

  1. A. Aldosterone falls and potassium is secreted in the proximal tubule
  2. B. Potassium moves into cells under insulin alone
  3. C. Aldosterone rises and increases potassium secretion in the distal nephron
  4. D. Aldosterone acts on the thick ascending limb to block potassium reabsorption
  5. E. Aldosterone causes sodium loss and potassium retention
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Aldosterone rises and increases potassium secretion in the distal nephron

Key idea: A potassium load raises aldosterone, which makes the kidney secrete more potassium. Aldosterone is a hormone from the adrenal cortex. Step by step: High plasma potassium directly stimulates the adrenal glands to release aldosterone. Aldosterone acts on principal cells in the distal tubule and collecting duct. Principal cells are the main sodium- and potassium-handling cells of the collecting duct. These cells reabsorb sodium and secrete potassium and hydrogen into the urine. Plasma potassium therefore falls back toward normal. Why the tempting option is wrong: Insulin can shift potassium into cells after a glucose load, but the main renal response to a potassium meal is aldosterone-driven secretion. Why it matters: Potassium secretion by the kidney prevents dangerous heart rhythm disturbances after a high-potassium meal.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Aldosterone falls and potassium is secreted in the proximal tubule: Potassium secretion is mainly distal and aldosterone-dependent; the proximal tubule reabsorbs most potassium but does not regulate secretion this way.
  • B. Potassium moves into cells under insulin alone: Insulin helps after a glucose load, but the dominant renal response to a potassium load is aldosterone-driven secretion.
  • D. Aldosterone acts on the thick ascending limb to block potassium reabsorption: The loop transporter reabsorbs potassium; the main potassium effect of aldosterone is in the collecting duct principal cells.
  • E. Aldosterone causes sodium loss and potassium retention: Aldosterone does the opposite: it causes sodium retention with potassium and hydrogen secretion.

Key point: Explain how aldosterone maintains potassium balance after a potassium load.

Question 6 · Respiratory system · medium

During a forced expiration, expiratory flow rises quickly to a peak and then, at lower lung volumes, it cannot be increased further however hard the person tries.

What limits flow in this effort-independent part of forced expiration?

  1. A. Dynamic compression of the airways
  2. B. Fatigue of the expiratory muscles
  3. C. Closure of the glottis
  4. D. Lack of surfactant in the bronchi
  5. E. Contraction of bronchial smooth muscle
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Dynamic compression of the airways

In forced expiration, pleural pressure becomes strongly positive. Pressure inside the airways falls from the alveoli to the mouth; at some point (the equal pressure point) it equals pleural pressure. Downstream of that point, pleural pressure exceeds airway pressure and compresses the airways, so trying harder only compresses them more. In emphysema, loss of elastic recoil lowers alveolar driving pressure and the airways lose their tethering, so the equal pressure point moves towards the alveoli and airways collapse early, trapping air. Pursed-lip breathing helps by keeping airway pressure up.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Fatigue of the expiratory muscles: Flow is limited even in the first maximal effort, before the muscles could tire.
  • C. Closure of the glottis: The glottis is open during forced expiration; closing it would stop flow completely.
  • D. Lack of surfactant in the bronchi: Surfactant works in the alveoli and does not explain why flow reaches a limit in the airways.
  • E. Contraction of bronchial smooth muscle: Smooth muscle tone does not change within one breath; flow limitation happens even with normal tone.

Key point: Effort-independent expiratory flow is limited by dynamic compression of the airways.

Question 7 · Haematology and immunity · easy

A 25-year-old woman has had heavy periods with clots for a year, changing pads hourly on the worst days. She is tired, breathless on stairs and craves ice. Her nails are spoon-shaped. Her haemoglobin is 8.5 g/dL, with a low MCV of 68 fL and pencil cells on the film. Her doctor sends iron studies.

Which iron study results are expected?

  1. A. Low ferritin, high TIBC
  2. B. High ferritin, low TIBC, low saturation
  3. C. High ferritin, high saturation
  4. D. Normal ferritin and normal TIBC
  5. E. Low ferritin, low TIBC, high saturation
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Low ferritin, high TIBC

In iron deficiency, iron stores are used up first (low ferritin, the most useful single test), then serum iron falls, the liver makes more transferrin (high TIBC) and transferrin saturation falls. Red cells become small (microcytic) and pale (hypochromic), with a raised red cell distribution width. In anaemia of chronic disease, hepcidin traps iron in macrophages: ferritin is normal or high (also an acute-phase protein), TIBC is low and saturation is low. Iron deficiency in women of childbearing age in Pakistan is very common.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. High ferritin, low TIBC, low saturation: A high ferritin with low TIBC fits anaemia of chronic disease, where iron is trapped in stores; her heavy periods point to iron loss.
  • C. High ferritin, high saturation: High ferritin and high saturation mean too much iron, as in haemochromatosis or after many transfusions.
  • D. Normal ferritin and normal TIBC: Iron deficiency this severe always changes iron studies; normal ferritin and TIBC would point away from it.
  • E. Low ferritin, low TIBC, high saturation: TIBC rises, not falls, in iron deficiency, and saturation falls because there is little iron to fill the transferrin.

Key point: Iron deficiency: low ferritin, high TIBC, low saturation; chronic disease: high ferritin, low TIBC.

Question 8 · Cardiovascular system · medium

A 28-year-old woman from a rural area, with a history of rheumatic fever in childhood, becomes breathless in the third trimester of pregnancy and coughs at night. Her heart rate is 110. She has an opening snap and a low-pitched mid-diastolic rumbling murmur at the apex. Her lung bases crackle.

Why does her breathlessness worsen in pregnancy?

  1. A. Higher flow and shorter diastole raise left atrial pressure
  2. B. The narrow aortic valve obstructs left ventricular outflow
  3. C. Pregnancy lowers the heart rate, reducing cardiac output
  4. D. Blood volume falls in pregnancy
  5. E. Left ventricular pressure rises during diastole
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Higher flow and shorter diastole raise left atrial pressure

In mitral stenosis the narrow valve limits flow from the left atrium to the left ventricle in diastole. The pressure gradient across the valve increases with the flow and falls with the time available for filling. In pregnancy, cardiac output and blood volume rise and heart rate increases (shortening diastole), so left atrial pressure and pulmonary capillary pressure rise, causing pulmonary congestion and breathlessness. Rheumatic mitral stenosis remains common in Pakistan and often presents for the first time in pregnancy.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. The narrow aortic valve obstructs left ventricular outflow: The lesion is mitral stenosis (opening snap, apical rumble), not narrowing of the aortic valve.
  • C. Pregnancy lowers the heart rate, reducing cardiac output: Heart rate rises in pregnancy, here to 110, and this shortens the filling time across the valve.
  • D. Blood volume falls in pregnancy: Blood volume rises by about 40-50% in pregnancy, which increases flow across the narrow valve.
  • E. Left ventricular pressure rises during diastole: In mitral stenosis the left ventricle is protected; the high pressure is in the left atrium.

Key point: In mitral stenosis, increased flow and shorter diastole raise left atrial pressure, as in pregnancy or tachycardia.

Question 9 · Endocrine and reproduction · easy

A 32-year-old woman has had weight loss despite eating more, palpitations, heat intolerance and a tremor for 3 months. She has a diffuse smooth goitre with a bruit, protruding eyes with a stare and lid lag, and raised thickened skin on her shins. Her TSH is undetectable and her free T4 is high.

What causes the excess thyroid hormone in this condition?

  1. A. Antibodies that stimulate the TSH receptor
  2. B. Excess TSH from the pituitary
  3. C. Destruction of the gland releasing stored hormone
  4. D. A single autonomous nodule
  5. E. Excess dietary iodine
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Antibodies that stimulate the TSH receptor

Graves' disease is an autoimmune disorder in which IgG antibodies bind and stimulate the TSH receptor, causing growth (diffuse goitre, increased blood flow and bruit) and excess hormone secretion. The high T4 and T3 suppress pituitary TSH. Similar autoimmune processes in the orbit cause exophthalmos, and in the skin pretibial myxoedema. TSH receptor antibodies cross the placenta and can cause neonatal thyrotoxicosis.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Excess TSH from the pituitary: TSH is undetectable, so the pituitary is not driving the gland.
  • C. Destruction of the gland releasing stored hormone: Thyroiditis releases stored hormone from a damaged gland, without a bruit or eye disease.
  • D. A single autonomous nodule: A single overactive nodule gives one lump, not a diffuse goitre with eye signs.
  • E. Excess dietary iodine: Excess iodine can occasionally cause thyrotoxicosis, but not the eye and skin changes of Graves disease.

Key point: Graves' disease is caused by TSH-receptor-stimulating antibodies; TSH is suppressed.

Question 10 · Cell, nerve and muscle · easy

A 2-year-old boy has had recurrent chest infections with Pseudomonas, poor weight gain despite a good appetite, and bulky, greasy, foul stools. He had a bowel blockage at birth. His mother says his skin tastes salty. His sweat chloride is high, at 90 mEq/L, on two tests.

What is the function of the protein that is defective?

  1. A. A cAMP-regulated chloride channel
  2. B. A sodium-potassium pump
  3. C. A water channel (aquaporin)
  4. D. A glucose transporter
  5. E. A calcium channel
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. A cAMP-regulated chloride channel

Cystic fibrosis is an autosomal recessive disorder of the CFTR (cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator) gene, which codes for a chloride channel opened by cAMP and protein kinase A. The commonest mutation (ΔF508) causes misfolding, so the protein is destroyed before reaching the membrane. In the airways and pancreatic ducts, poor chloride and bicarbonate secretion makes secretions thick, causing infection, bronchiectasis and pancreatic insufficiency. In sweat ducts, chloride cannot be reabsorbed, so sweat is salty, the basis of the sweat test.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. A sodium-potassium pump: The Na+/K+ pump works normally in cystic fibrosis; the defect is in a chloride channel.
  • C. A water channel (aquaporin): Aquaporins carry water; they are normal in cystic fibrosis.
  • D. A glucose transporter: Glucose transporters are not affected; his malabsorption is due to a blocked pancreas.
  • E. A calcium channel: CFTR is a chloride channel, not a calcium channel.

Key point: CFTR is a cAMP-regulated chloride channel; its defect causes thick secretions and salty sweat.
