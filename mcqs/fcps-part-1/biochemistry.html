Question 1 · Chemistry of biomolecules · medium

A 4-year-old boy has coarse facial features, stiff joints, a large liver, recurrent ear infections, and pebbly skin on his back. He is hyperactive with aggressive behaviour. His corneas are clear. His maternal uncle had a similar illness and died at 15. Dermatan and heparan sulfate are high in his urine.

What is the most likely diagnosis?

  1. A. Hurler syndrome
  2. B. Hunter syndrome
  3. C. Tay-Sachs disease
  4. D. Pompe disease
  5. E. Down syndrome
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Hunter syndrome

Hunter syndrome (MPS II) is caused by iduronate-2-sulfatase deficiency. It is X-linked recessive, so it affects boys and passes through carrier mothers (an affected maternal uncle is a clue). It resembles Hurler syndrome, with coarse features, stiff joints, a large liver and spleen and heart disease, but the corneas stay clear and the course is often milder. Aggressive or hyperactive behaviour is common. A memory aid: "Hunters see clearly and aim for the X".

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Hurler syndrome: Hurler syndrome clouds the cornea and is autosomal recessive; his corneas are clear and the uncle on his mother's side suggests X-linked inheritance.
  • C. Tay-Sachs disease: Tay-Sachs disease causes loss of skills with a cherry-red spot, without coarse features or urinary GAGs.
  • D. Pompe disease: Pompe disease is a glycogen storage disease with a huge heart and floppiness, not coarse features and urinary GAGs.
  • E. Down syndrome: Down syndrome has its features from birth, without progressive coarsening or GAGs in the urine.

Key point: Hunter syndrome is X-linked iduronate-2-sulfatase deficiency without corneal clouding.

Question 2 · Hormone action, metabolism in health and disease, clinical biochemistry · medium

An arterial blood sample shows bicarbonate 12 mEq/L and PaCO2 24 mmHg. The pKa of the bicarbonate buffer is 6.1 and the solubility of CO2 is 0.03 mmol/L per mmHg.

What is the pH, approximately?

  1. A. 7.12
  2. B. 7.22
  3. C. 7.32
  4. D. 7.42
  5. E. 7.52
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. 7.32

Henderson-Hasselbalch: pH = pKa + log ([HCO3-] / (0.03 x PaCO2)). Here 0.03 x 24 = 0.72 mmol/L of dissolved CO2, and 12 / 0.72 = 16.7. The log of 16.7 is about 1.22, so pH = 6.1 + 1.22 = 7.32. Normal blood has bicarbonate 24 and PaCO2 40 mmHg, a ratio of 20:1 (log 1.3), giving pH 7.4. The pH depends on the ratio, not on the absolute values, which is why compensation works: here a metabolic acidosis (low bicarbonate) is partly compensated by hyperventilation (low PaCO2). Using kPa, the factor is 0.23 instead of 0.03.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. 7.12: This needs a ratio of about 10:1.
  • B. 7.22: This needs a ratio of about 13:1.
  • D. 7.42: This needs a ratio of about 20:1 or more.
  • E. 7.52: This needs a ratio of about 26:1.

Key point: pH = 6.1 + log (HCO3 / 0.03 x PaCO2); pH depends on the bicarbonate to CO2 ratio.

Question 3 · Enzymes, bioenergetics and free radicals · easy

A 50-year-old man takes a single 300 mg aspirin tablet for a headache the day before a dental extraction. His dentist notes prolonged oozing. His platelets show reduced aggregation for about a week, although aspirin leaves his blood within hours. His clotting tests are normal.

Why does the effect last so long?

  1. A. Aspirin is stored in platelet granules
  2. B. Aspirin blocks platelet production in the marrow
  3. C. Aspirin is a competitive inhibitor with a high affinity
  4. D. It permanently acetylates platelet COX
  5. E. Aspirin is slowly excreted by the kidney
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. It permanently acetylates platelet COX

Aspirin transfers its acetyl group to a serine at the active site of cyclooxygenase (COX-1 and COX-2), permanently blocking it (irreversible inhibition). Platelets have no nucleus, so they cannot make new COX, and they cannot make thromboxane A2 for the rest of their life (7-10 days). Recovery depends on new platelets from the marrow. This is why low-dose aspirin protects against clots and why it is stopped about a week before some surgery. Other irreversible (suicide) inhibitors include penicillins (transpeptidase), organophosphates (acetylcholinesterase) and omeprazole (proton pump).

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Aspirin is stored in platelet granules: Aspirin is not stored in platelets; it acts in minutes and is cleared within hours.
  • B. Aspirin blocks platelet production in the marrow: Aspirin does not affect platelet production; the count stays normal while function is lost.
  • C. Aspirin is a competitive inhibitor with a high affinity: Aspirin binds covalently and permanently; a competitive drug would wear off as levels fell.
  • E. Aspirin is slowly excreted by the kidney: Aspirin and salicylate are cleared within hours to a day at this dose, much shorter than the effect.

Key point: Aspirin irreversibly acetylates COX; platelets cannot replace it, so the effect lasts their lifetime.

Question 4 · Lipid metabolism and lipoproteins · medium

A 22-year-old man develops aching muscle pain, weakness and dark urine after a long day's hike in the cold without much food. His CK is 40,000 U/L. He had a similar episode after fasting during a flu illness. Short bursts of exercise, like sprinting, do not bother him, and he has no cramps at the start of exercise.

What is the most likely cause?

  1. A. McArdle disease (muscle phosphorylase)
  2. B. CPT II deficiency
  3. C. Duchenne muscular dystrophy
  4. D. Pompe disease
  5. E. Myasthenia gravis
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. CPT II deficiency

Fatty acids are the main fuel for muscle during prolonged exercise and fasting. In the adult form of CPT II deficiency, long-chain acyl-carnitines cannot be converted back to acyl-CoA inside mitochondria, so muscle runs short of energy during long exercise, fasting, cold or illness. This causes muscle pain, rhabdomyolysis and myoglobinuria, which can cause kidney failure. Between attacks CK is often normal. This contrasts with McArdle disease, in which problems come in the first minutes of intense exercise. Frequent carbohydrate intake and avoiding fasting help.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. McArdle disease (muscle phosphorylase): McArdle disease causes cramps and fatigue within minutes of intense exercise, with a second wind; his sprinting is fine.
  • C. Duchenne muscular dystrophy: Duchenne dystrophy causes progressive weakness from early childhood with a constantly high CK.
  • D. Pompe disease: Pompe disease causes progressive weakness and, in infants, heart enlargement, not episodes after fasting.
  • E. Myasthenia gravis: Myasthenia gravis causes weakness that worsens with use but no muscle breakdown or raised CK.

Key point: CPT II deficiency causes rhabdomyolysis during prolonged exercise or fasting, when muscle relies on fat.

Question 5 · Cell and molecular biology, genetics · easy

A cell biologist in Mirpur studies a newly made pancreatic enzyme. She finds that a short hydrophobic sequence at its start directs the ribosome to a membrane-bound organelle, where the protein enters the lumen for folding and modification before secretion.

What happens to the protein once this structure has delivered it to the membrane?

  1. A. It is released into the cytosol and folds there
  2. B. It is tagged with ubiquitin and destroyed at once
  3. C. It enters the rough ER, loses its signal and is processed
  4. D. It is imported into the mitochondrion through the TOM and TIM complexes
  5. E. It passes into the nucleus through a nuclear pore
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. It enters the rough ER, loses its signal and is processed

Protein targeting begins while translation is still in progress. A hydrophobic signal sequence of about 15 to 30 residues at the amino terminus is bound by the signal recognition particle, which arrests elongation and brings the ribosome to its receptor on the rough endoplasmic reticulum; the chain then passes through the Sec61 translocon into the lumen, the signal is cleaved, and glycosylation and disulphide formation occur with the help of chaperones. From there the protein travels to the Golgi, where oligosaccharides are trimmed and lysosomal enzymes receive mannose-6-phosphate tags, and is sorted to secretory vesicles, lysosomes or the plasma membrane. Failures at each stage cause disease: loss of the signal peptide prevents secretion, loss of the mannose-6-phosphate tag causes I-cell disease, and misfolding in the lumen underlies alpha-1 antitrypsin liver injury.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. It is released into the cytosol and folds there: Proteins destined for secretion do not fold free in the cytosol.
  • B. It is tagged with ubiquitin and destroyed at once: Degradation happens only if folding fails.
  • D. It is imported into the mitochondrion through the TOM and TIM complexes: Mitochondrial import uses different signals and translocases.
  • E. It passes into the nucleus through a nuclear pore: Nuclear import requires a nuclear localisation signal.

Key point: Trace a secretory protein from signal recognition particle through the translocon to the Golgi.

Question 6 · Nutrition, vitamins and minerals · medium

A 32-year-old woman who covers fully outdoors and has had three pregnancies close together, breastfeeding each baby, has aching bones and a waddling walk. She has trouble climbing stairs and rising from a chair. Her calcium is 8.6 mg/dL, phosphate 2.1 mg/dL and alkaline phosphatase 390 U/L (normal 40-130). X-ray of the pelvis shows narrow lucent lines across the pubic rami.

What is the underlying bone defect?

  1. A. Loss of bone mass with normal mineralisation
  2. B. Increased bone turnover with disorganised new bone
  3. C. Defective mineralisation of osteoid in adult bone
  4. D. Infection of the pubic bones
  5. E. Tumour deposits in the pelvis
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Defective mineralisation of osteoid in adult bone

Osteomalacia is the adult form of rickets: new bone matrix (osteoid) is laid down but not mineralised because of vitamin D deficiency (or phosphate loss). Patients have diffuse bone pain and tenderness and a proximal myopathy (waddling gait, trouble rising), with Looser zones (pseudofractures) on X-ray, typically in the pubic rami, femoral neck and scapula. Calcium and phosphate are low or normal, ALP and PTH high, and 25-OH vitamin D low. Risks include little sun exposure, dark skin, repeated pregnancies and breastfeeding, malabsorption and anticonvulsants. Osteoporosis, by contrast, has normal labs.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Loss of bone mass with normal mineralisation: Osteoporosis thins bone with normal mineralisation and normal calcium, phosphate and ALP.
  • B. Increased bone turnover with disorganised new bone: Paget disease shows disorganised new bone with a very high ALP, usually in older people.
  • D. Infection of the pubic bones: Infection would cause fever and focal pain, not symmetrical pseudofractures.
  • E. Tumour deposits in the pelvis: Tumour deposits cause destructive lesions, not thin lucent lines across both pubic rami.

Key point: Osteomalacia is defective mineralisation in adults, with bone pain, proximal myopathy and Looser zones.

Question 7 · Amino acids, haem and nucleotides · easy

A 20-year-old woman from Larkana has fatigue and dyspnoea. Haemoglobin is 8.0 g/dL (12-16). Mean corpuscular volume is 102 fL (80-100). Blood neutrophils show hypersegmentation. Her diet lacks green vegetables and liver.

Why does giving folate alone risk harm if her deficiency is actually of vitamin B12?

  1. A. It corrects the anaemia while the cord damage continues
  2. B. Because folate causes haemolysis in B12 deficiency
  3. C. Because folate blocks the absorption of vitamin B12 in the ileum
  4. D. Because folate raises homocysteine further and causes thrombosis
  5. E. Because folate is converted into B12 and masks the deficiency on testing
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. It corrects the anaemia while the cord damage continues

Folate and vitamin B12 meet at methionine synthase, which transfers the methyl group of methyl-tetrahydrofolate to homocysteine using methylcobalamin. In B12 deficiency this reaction stalls, folate accumulates in its methyl form and cannot be used for thymidylate synthesis, which is the methyl folate trap: hence the identical megaloblastic marrow in both deficiencies. The two can be separated biochemically, because B12 is also required by methylmalonyl-CoA mutase, so methylmalonic acid rises in B12 deficiency alone, while homocysteine rises in both. Clinically, B12 deficiency adds glossitis, peripheral neuropathy and subacute combined degeneration with loss of vibration and position sense and an extensor plantar response, and it can cause dementia. Treating with folate alone improves the blood count while the cord disease advances, so B12 must always be measured and replaced first.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Because folate causes haemolysis in B12 deficiency: Folate does not cause haemolysis.
  • C. Because folate blocks the absorption of vitamin B12 in the ileum: It does not interfere with ileal absorption.
  • D. Because folate raises homocysteine further and causes thrombosis: Folate lowers homocysteine.
  • E. Because folate is converted into B12 and masks the deficiency on testing: Folate cannot be converted to B12.

Key point: Explain the methyl folate trap and the danger of treating B12 deficiency with folate alone.

Question 8 · Carbohydrate metabolism · medium

A 22-year-old woman from Chitral eats a large breakfast of paratha and eggs. Two hours later her blood glucose is 98 mg/dL (70-110) and insulin is rising. Her liver cells are taking up glucose and glycolysis speeds up.

Which enzyme makes and destroys this signal, and how is it switched?

  1. A. Phosphofructokinase-1, which is phosphorylated by protein kinase A
  2. B. Glucokinase, acting in both directions according to the glucose level
  3. C. The bifunctional PFK-2/FBPase-2, switched by phosphorylation
  4. D. Pyruvate kinase, which is activated by fructose-1,6-bisphosphate
  5. E. Hexokinase in muscle, which is inhibited by its product
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. The bifunctional PFK-2/FBPase-2, switched by phosphorylation

Fructose-2,6-bisphosphate is the central signal that decides whether the liver makes or consumes glucose, and it is unusual in being made and broken down by two domains of the same protein. Insulin, through a phosphatase, leaves the protein dephosphorylated so that the kinase domain is active; glucagon, through cyclic AMP and protein kinase A, phosphorylates it so that the bisphosphatase domain is active. Because the product activates phosphofructokinase-1 and inhibits fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase at the same time, a single signal moves both the forward and the reverse pathway in the same direction and prevents a futile cycle. Muscle has a different isoform that is not controlled by glucagon, which is why muscle glycolysis is not switched off during fasting.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Phosphofructokinase-1, which is phosphorylated by protein kinase A: PFK-1 is the target of the signal, not its maker.
  • B. Glucokinase, acting in both directions according to the glucose level: Glucokinase phosphorylates glucose and has no part in making this regulator.
  • D. Pyruvate kinase, which is activated by fructose-1,6-bisphosphate: Pyruvate kinase is regulated by this system but does not produce the signal.
  • E. Hexokinase in muscle, which is inhibited by its product: Hexokinase is unrelated to this control.

Key point: Describe the bifunctional PFK-2/FBPase-2 enzyme and its hormonal control of hepatic glycolysis.

Question 9 · Chemistry of biomolecules · medium

A patient who had brain surgery is later found to have sporadic Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease. The hospital must decide how to deal with the surgical instruments, which were cleaned and autoclaved in the usual way.

Why are standard sterilisation methods inadequate?

  1. A. Prions have a tough protein coat around their DNA
  2. B. Prions are bacteria that form heat-resistant spores
  3. C. Prions are small viruses that resist ultraviolet light
  4. D. Beta-sheet prion protein resists heat and proteases
  5. E. Prions are fats that dissolve only in strong acid
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. Beta-sheet prion protein resists heat and proteases

Prions are misfolded forms (PrPSc) of a normal host protein (PrPC). The normal protein is mainly alpha-helical; the prion form is rich in beta-pleated sheet, aggregates, and converts normal protein into the misfolded form, spreading through the brain. Because it contains no nucleic acid and its aggregated beta structure is highly stable, it resists standard autoclaving, ionising radiation, formaldehyde and proteases. Instruments exposed to high-risk tissue (brain, spinal cord, posterior eye) need special decontamination (prolonged high-temperature autoclaving with sodium hydroxide) or are discarded, and are quarantined while diagnosis is pending. Single-use instruments are preferred.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Prions have a tough protein coat around their DNA: Prions contain no nucleic acid at all; they are misfolded proteins.
  • B. Prions are bacteria that form heat-resistant spores: Prions are not bacteria and form no spores; their resistance comes from the beta-sheet protein.
  • C. Prions are small viruses that resist ultraviolet light: Prions are not viruses and have no genome; standard UV and autoclaving do not inactivate them.
  • E. Prions are fats that dissolve only in strong acid: Prions are proteins, not fats.

Key point: Prion protein is a stable beta-sheet misfold that resists usual sterilisation; instruments need special treatment.

Question 10 · Hormone action, metabolism in health and disease, clinical biochemistry · medium

A 65-year-old smoker has confusion, thirst and constipation. Calcium is 14.4 mg/dL, phosphate 2.0 mg/dL, PTH 6 pg/mL (normal 15-65) and 25-hydroxyvitamin D normal. Chest X-ray shows a mass, and biopsy shows squamous cell carcinoma without bone metastases.

What is the most likely mechanism?

  1. A. A parathyroid adenoma secreting PTH
  2. B. Familial hypocalciuric hypercalcaemia
  3. C. Granuloma macrophages making calcitriol
  4. D. PTH-related peptide from the tumour
  5. E. Bone destruction by tumour deposits
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. PTH-related peptide from the tumour

Hypercalcaemia with suppressed PTH means the calcium is not coming from the parathyroids. Some cancers, especially squamous cell carcinomas (lung, head and neck), renal and breast cancers, secrete PTH-related peptide, which shares its N-terminal region with PTH and acts on the same receptor, increasing bone resorption and renal calcium reabsorption and causing phosphate loss (low phosphate). Normal PTH assays do not detect PTHrP, which can be measured separately. Other mechanisms of malignant hypercalcaemia include local bone destruction (myeloma, metastases) and calcitriol production by lymphomas. Treatment is intravenous fluids, bisphosphonates or denosumab, and treating the cancer.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. A parathyroid adenoma secreting PTH: A parathyroid adenoma raises PTH; his PTH is suppressed.
  • B. Familial hypocalciuric hypercalcaemia: FHH causes mild hypercalcaemia with normal PTH and low urine calcium, not a calcium of 14.4 mg/dL.
  • C. Granuloma macrophages making calcitriol: Granulomas make calcitriol, which raises phosphate absorption; his phosphate is low and he has a squamous cancer.
  • E. Bone destruction by tumour deposits: There are no bone metastases on staging.

Key point: Hypercalcaemia with suppressed PTH and low phosphate in squamous cancer suggests PTHrP secretion.
