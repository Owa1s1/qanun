Question 1 · Virology · medium

A 4-month-old boy born at 34 weeks is brought to a Karachi hospital in December with 3 days of runny nose, then cough, fast breathing and difficulty feeding. Respiratory rate is 62/min with chest recession and widespread fine crackles and wheeze. Oxygen saturation is 91%. Chest X-ray shows hyperinflation. Nasal swab PCR is positive for a virus whose infected cells fuse into multinucleate masses in culture.

What is the mainstay of his treatment?

  1. A. Nebulised salbutamol every four hours
  2. B. Supportive care with feeds and oxygen
  3. C. Oral prednisolone for three days
  4. D. Intravenous antibiotics for a week
  5. E. Nebulised ribavirin for five days
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Supportive care with feeds and oxygen

This is acute bronchiolitis caused by respiratory syncytial virus, the commonest cause of hospital admission in infants in the cool months. RSV is an enveloped, negative-sense RNA paramyxovirus whose F (fusion) protein makes infected cells fuse into syncytia, giving the virus its name. Infection causes inflammation and plugging of small airways, with coryza followed by cough, tachypnoea, recession, crackles and wheeze, hyperinflation on X-ray, and feeding difficulty; apnoea may occur in small or premature babies. Treatment is supportive: oxygen for hypoxia, suction of secretions, nasogastric or intravenous fluids, and respiratory support if needed. Bronchodilators, steroids, antibiotics and nebulised ribavirin have no routine role. Risk factors for severe disease are prematurity, age under 3 months, congenital heart and lung disease and immunodeficiency; palivizumab (and newer long-acting antibodies) and maternal vaccination can protect high-risk infants. Immunity is incomplete, so reinfection is common.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Nebulised salbutamol every four hours: Salbutamol gives little benefit in infants of his age, whose wheeze comes from plugged small airways rather than smooth muscle.
  • C. Oral prednisolone for three days: Steroids do not shorten bronchiolitis or reduce admission in infants of his age, and would not improve his saturation of 91%.
  • D. Intravenous antibiotics for a week: Antibiotics add nothing here: his PCR is positive for a virus and his X-ray shows hyperinflation, not consolidation.
  • E. Nebulised ribavirin for five days: Nebulised ribavirin is toxic to staff, of doubtful benefit, and is not used for an otherwise stable infant with bronchiolitis.

Key point: RSV bronchiolitis in infants is treated supportively with oxygen and feeding support; the F protein causes the syncytia that name the virus.

Question 2 · Immunology · easy

A medical student in Islamabad asks about how the thymus prevents autoimmunity. The professor explains that developing T-cells undergo rigorous testing. Cells that bind too weakly to self-MHC die by neglect, while those binding too strongly to self-antigens presented on MHC are eliminated. This latter process ensures tolerance to self.

Which process eliminates high-affinity self-reactive T cells, mainly in the thymic medulla?

  1. A. Positive selection
  2. B. Class switching
  3. C. Somatic hypermutation
  4. D. Negative selection
  5. E. Anergy
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. Negative selection

Developing T cells are tested twice in the thymus. In the cortex, positive selection keeps only cells whose receptors bind self-MHC well enough to be useful. In the medulla, negative selection removes cells that bind self-peptide on MHC too strongly: medullary epithelial cells, helped by the AIRE gene, display proteins from many tissues, and strongly reacting cells die by apoptosis. This central tolerance removes most autoreactive clones before they reach the blood; peripheral mechanisms such as anergy and regulatory T cells deal with the rest.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Positive selection: Ensures survival of cells that recognise self-MHC appropriately, not elimination of strong binders.
  • B. Class switching: Occurs in peripheral lymphoid organs during antibody production, not T-cell development in thymus.
  • C. Somatic hypermutation: Affects B-cell receptor affinity in germinal centers, unrelated to thymic T-cell selection.
  • E. Anergy: Refers to functional inactivation of mature peripheral T-cells, not their deletion in the thymus.

Key point: High affinity for self-MHC/Ag in thymus = Negative selection (apoptosis).

Question 3 · Bacteriology · medium

A 23-year-old woman in Gujranwala has had 2 days of fever with rigors, right loin pain and vomiting, after 4 days of burning on passing urine. Her temperature is 39.1°C, pulse 112/min, blood pressure 110/70 mmHg, and there is marked tenderness over the right renal angle. Urine microscopy shows many white cells and white cell casts. She cannot keep fluids down.

Which feature most clearly separates her illness from simple cystitis?

  1. A. Burning on passing urine
  2. B. Many white cells in urine
  3. C. More than 100,000 colonies/mL
  4. D. White cell casts in urine
  5. E. Positive nitrite on dipstick
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. White cell casts in urine

Cystitis is confined to the bladder: frequency, urgency, burning and suprapubic discomfort, without fever or systemic upset. Acute pyelonephritis means infection of the kidney, shown by fever with rigors, loin pain and tenderness, vomiting and systemic illness; white cell casts in the urine indicate that the inflammation is within the renal tubules and so point to upper tract disease, as does raised CRP. Pyuria and bacteriuria occur in both, so they do not separate them. Pyelonephritis in a woman who cannot keep fluids down, or who is pregnant, septic or has a single or obstructed kidney, needs admission and intravenous antibiotics (a third-generation cephalosporin or an aminoglycoside, adjusted to sensitivities), with imaging to exclude obstruction, stones or abscess if she does not improve in 48-72 hours; treatment lasts 7-14 days. Mild cases can be treated at home with oral ciprofloxacin or co-amoxiclav. Nitrofurantoin must not be used, as it does not reach therapeutic levels in kidney tissue.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Burning on passing urine: Burning on passing urine occurs in simple cystitis too, as it did in her first 4 days.
  • B. Many white cells in urine: Pyuria is present in cystitis as well, so white cells alone do not place the infection in her kidney.
  • C. More than 100,000 colonies/mL: Colony counts above 100,000/mL occur in simple cystitis too, so this does not place her infection in the kidney.
  • E. Positive nitrite on dipstick: A positive nitrite shows coliforms anywhere in the urinary tract, including the bladder alone.

Key point: Fever, loin pain and white cell casts show pyelonephritis rather than cystitis; nitrofurantoin is unsuitable because it does not reach kidney tissue.

Question 4 · Mycology · medium

A 34-year-old man in Multan has a scaly, itchy, ring-shaped patch on his trunk with a raised edge. The laboratory receives scrapings from the edge of the lesion. The technician wants a quick bench method that will dissolve the keratin so that any fungal filaments can be seen clearly under the microscope within half an hour.

Which preparation should be used?

  1. A. India ink preparation
  2. B. Ziehl-Neelsen staining
  3. C. Potassium hydroxide
  4. D. Gram staining
  5. E. Giemsa staining
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Potassium hydroxide

Fungi are eukaryotes with a chitinous cell wall containing ergosterol in the membrane, which is the target of most antifungal drugs. They grow as yeasts (round cells dividing by budding), as moulds (branching filamentous hyphae forming a mycelium) or as dimorphic fungi that are moulds in the environment at 25°C and yeasts in tissue at 37°C. Laboratory diagnosis starts with direct microscopy: skin, nail and hair samples are treated with 10-20% potassium hydroxide, which digests keratin and leaves fungal hyphae and spores visible, sometimes with added calcofluor white or Parker ink for contrast. Other direct methods are India ink or capsular antigen for Cryptococcus in CSF, silver and periodic acid-Schiff stains on tissue, and Giemsa for intracellular Histoplasma. Culture uses Sabouraud dextrose agar, often with antibiotics to suppress bacteria, incubated for up to 4 weeks. Antigen tests (galactomannan, beta-D-glucan, cryptococcal antigen) and PCR are increasingly used.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. India ink preparation: India ink outlines capsulated yeast in CSF; it would not clear the keratin in his skin scrapings.
  • B. Ziehl-Neelsen staining: Ziehl-Neelsen looks for acid-fast bacilli, which are not expected in a scaly ring-shaped patch on his trunk.
  • D. Gram staining: Gram stain suits bacteria in pus; it leaves his keratin intact so any hyphae stay hidden.
  • E. Giemsa staining: Giemsa is for blood films and marrow; it would not digest the keratin covering the edge of his lesion.

Key point: Potassium hydroxide mounts dissolve keratin to show fungal hyphae in skin, nail and hair; Sabouraud agar is the standard culture medium.

Question 5 · General microbiology · easy

A 26-year-old man has a painful boil on his thigh. Pus is sent to the laboratory, and a trainee makes a Gram stain. The next day the supervisor learns that the trainee applied crystal violet, then washed with alcohol-acetone and counterstained with safranin, but forgot to add Gram iodine. The pus later grows Staphylococcus aureus.

How would the organisms most likely have looked on this smear?

  1. A. Purple cocci in clusters
  2. B. Purple cocci in chains
  3. C. Pink rods in pairs
  4. D. Unstained clear cocci
  5. E. Pink cocci in clusters
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Pink cocci in clusters

The Gram stain has four steps: crystal violet (primary stain), Gram iodine (mordant), alcohol or acetone (decoloriser) and safranin (counterstain). Iodine combines with crystal violet to form a large dye-iodine complex inside the cell. In gram-positive bacteria the thick, many-layered peptidoglycan wall dehydrates and tightens when alcohol is applied, trapping this large complex, so the cells stay purple. Without iodine, the smaller crystal violet molecules wash out of gram-positive cells too, and the safranin then colours them pink, so gram-positive bacteria falsely appear gram-negative. Over-decolorising or using old cultures (damaged walls) gives the same error. Staphylococci are gram-positive cocci in grape-like clusters; streptococci form chains.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Purple cocci in clusters: Purple requires iodine to lock the dye inside the thick wall, and the trainee left that step out.
  • B. Purple cocci in chains: Chains would suggest streptococci; his pus grew Staphylococcus aureus, which forms grape-like clusters.
  • C. Pink rods in pairs: Rods would point to a different organism; the pus from his boil grew cocci.
  • D. Unstained clear cocci: The cells are not left colourless, because the safranin counterstain was applied and takes up where the crystal violet washed out.

Key point: Iodine fixes crystal violet as a large complex in the thick gram-positive wall; skipping it makes gram-positive bacteria look gram-negative.

Question 6 · Parasitology · easy

A 6-year-old girl in Lahore has been irritable and sleeping badly for 3 weeks, scratching around the anus at night. Her younger brother has started doing the same. She is otherwise well, with a normal abdomen and no diarrhoea. Routine stool microscopy is negative. Her mother is advised to use adhesive tape on the perianal skin in the early morning before washing.

Why is this method used instead of stool examination?

  1. A. Eggs dissolve in stool
  2. B. The worms live in the liver
  3. C. Eggs are too small to see
  4. D. Eggs appear in urine
  5. E. Eggs are laid on the skin
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Eggs are laid on the skin

Enterobius vermicularis (pinworm, threadworm) lives in the caecum and colon. At night the gravid female migrates out through the anus and deposits thousands of sticky eggs on the perianal skin, causing intense itching; the eggs become infective within hours, and scratching transfers them to fingers and nails, then to the mouth, bedding, clothes and dust, so reinfection and spread through a household, nursery or hostel are the rule. Because eggs are rarely passed in faeces, stool microscopy is usually negative; the diagnosis is made by the perianal adhesive tape (cellophane swab) test taken in the early morning before washing, showing the characteristic asymmetrical, flattened, oval eggs, or by seeing the small white worms. Occasionally the worm causes vulvovaginitis in girls or enters the appendix. Treatment is albendazole, mebendazole or pyrantel, repeated after 2 weeks to catch newly matured worms, and the whole household is treated at once, with hot washing of bedding and nightclothes, nail cutting and hand hygiene.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Eggs dissolve in stool: The eggs are durable and would survive in stool; the reason they are absent is that the female lays them outside the bowel.
  • B. The worms live in the liver: These worms live in her caecum and colon, not the liver, and the female crawls out at night to lay.
  • C. Eggs are too small to see: The eggs are easily seen under the microscope once collected; the difficulty is that they are not passed in her stool.
  • D. Eggs appear in urine: Eggs in urine indicate Schistosoma haematobium with haematuria; her symptoms are night-time perianal itching.

Key point: Enterobius lays eggs on perianal skin, so diagnosis uses the morning adhesive tape test; treat the whole household and repeat in 2 weeks.

Question 7 · Virology · easy

Eleven children from one school in a town near Sahiwal develop fever, loss of appetite, nausea and then jaundice over 3 weeks, after a sewage pipe leaked into the water supply. All recover fully within a month. One child's tests show anti-HAV IgM positive, HBsAg negative and anti-HCV negative. None of the children becomes a long-term carrier.

Which feature of this virus explains the absence of chronic infection?

  1. A. It is fully cleared by immunity
  2. B. It integrates into liver DNA
  3. C. It is cleared by antibiotics
  4. D. It infects children
  5. E. It has a lipid envelope
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. It is fully cleared by immunity

Hepatitis A is a naked, positive-sense RNA picornavirus spread by the faecal-oral route through contaminated water and food, so outbreaks follow sewage contamination; being non-enveloped, it survives in water, acid and drying, and alcohol hand rubs are not reliable. The illness is an acute hepatitis that is often silent or mild in young children and more severe with age, and it never becomes chronic: there is no carrier state and no long-term liver damage, although rare fulminant hepatitis occurs, mainly in older adults and those with existing liver disease. Diagnosis is by anti-HAV IgM. Management is supportive; prevention is clean water, sanitation, hand hygiene and vaccination, with vaccine or immunoglobulin for post-exposure prophylaxis in contacts. Hepatitis E is spread the same way and is also usually self-limiting, but it is dangerous in pregnancy and can persist in the immunosuppressed.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. It integrates into liver DNA: This virus never integrates into liver DNA, which is one reason none of the eleven children became a carrier.
  • C. It is cleared by antibiotics: Antibiotics have no action on viruses, and the children recovered without any treatment.
  • D. It infects children: Adults are infected as well and more severely, so an age limit cannot explain the absence of carriage.
  • E. It has a lipid envelope: This virus has no envelope, which is why it survived in the contaminated water supply.

Key point: Hepatitis A is a faecal-orally spread naked RNA virus causing acute hepatitis with no chronic carrier state; diagnose with anti-HAV IgM.

Question 8 · Immunology · easy

A 24-year-old man from Abbottabad had an emergency splenectomy for splenic rupture after a motorcycle accident. He did not complete his post-splenectomy vaccinations. Three years later he presents to a hospital in Abbottabad with a day of high fever, severe headache, neck stiffness and confusion. His temperature is 39.8 °C and Kernig sign is positive. Blood cultures grow Gram-positive diplococci.

What is the most likely pathogen?

  1. A. Streptococcus pneumoniae
  2. B. Neisseria meningitidis
  3. C. Haemophilus influenzae type b
  4. D. Listeria monocytogenes
  5. E. Staphylococcus aureus
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Streptococcus pneumoniae

The spleen filters blood-borne pathogens and facilitates opsonisation of encapsulated bacteria via marginal zone macrophages and IgM production. Asplenic patients are highly susceptible to overwhelming infection by Streptococcus pneumoniae (most common), Neisseria meningitidis, and Haemophilus influenzae type b. S. pneumoniae appears as Gram-positive lancet-shaped diplococci.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Neisseria meningitidis: Meningococcus is also dangerous after splenectomy, but it is a Gram-negative diplococcus, while this culture grew Gram-positive diplococci.
  • C. Haemophilus influenzae type b: Haemophilus influenzae type b is encapsulated and a risk after splenectomy, but it is a Gram-negative coccobacillus, not the Gram-positive diplococci seen here.
  • D. Listeria monocytogenes: Listeria is a Gram-positive rod causing meningitis in neonates, the elderly and the immunosuppressed; it would not appear as diplococci on this culture.
  • E. Staphylococcus aureus: Gram-positive cocci in clusters; less common cause of spontaneous meningitis in asplenic adults.

Key point: Asplenia + Gram-positive diplococci meningitis = Streptococcus pneumoniae.

Question 9 · Bacteriology · easy

Within 2 to 4 hours of a wedding lunch in Gujranwala, 23 guests develop sudden nausea, repeated vomiting and abdominal cramps, some with mild diarrhoea. None has fever, and most recover within a day. All ate a chicken dish that the caterer cooked the evening before, left at room temperature overnight and reheated thoroughly before serving. A cook has a pustule on his thumb. Gram-positive cocci in clusters that are coagulase positive are grown from the leftover chicken.

Why did thorough reheating fail to prevent the illness?

  1. A. Spores survived the heating
  2. B. The bacteria invade the gut wall
  3. C. The toxin is heat stable
  4. D. The toxin needs gut activation
  5. E. The organism grows in the fridge
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. The toxin is heat stable

This is staphylococcal food poisoning. Staphylococcus aureus from a food handler (often from a skin lesion or the nose) multiplies in protein-rich food left at room temperature and makes enterotoxins (A to E and others) in the food. The enterotoxins are heat stable and resist gut enzymes, so reheating kills the bacteria but not the toxin already formed. Because the toxin is preformed, symptoms start quickly (1-6 hours), mainly vomiting from toxin action on the vagus nerve and vomiting centre, with little fever, and recovery comes within 24-48 hours. Bacillus cereus emetic toxin (fried rice) gives a similar picture. Longer incubation points to organisms that must grow or make toxin in the gut: Clostridium perfringens (8-16 hours, reheated meat stews), enterotoxigenic E. coli, Salmonella. Staphylococcal enterotoxins are also superantigens.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Spores survived the heating: Spores would survive, but spore-formers take 8-16 hours to cause illness, and these guests vomited within 2 to 4 hours.
  • B. The bacteria invade the gut wall: Invasion would bring fever and faecal white cells after a day or more; none of the guests had fever and all recovered within a day.
  • D. The toxin needs gut activation: The toxin was already active in the chicken, so no activation in the gut is needed before vomiting begins.
  • E. The organism grows in the fridge: Growth in the cold is a Listeria trait and would not matter here, as the chicken stood at room temperature all night.

Key point: Staphylococcal food poisoning is caused by preformed heat-stable enterotoxin, giving vomiting within 1-6 hours even after reheating.

Question 10 · Mycology · medium

An 8-year-old boy in a village near Bahawalpur has three scaly bald patches on his scalp with broken-off hairs and some pustules, present for 6 weeks and slowly enlarging. Two brothers have similar patches and the family keeps goats. Potassium hydroxide examination of plucked hairs shows spores around the hair shafts.

What is the most appropriate treatment?

  1. A. Topical antifungal cream
  2. B. Topical steroid cream
  3. C. Oral antibiotics for 7 days
  4. D. Shaving the head
  5. E. Oral griseofulvin
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Oral griseofulvin

This is tinea capitis caused by dermatophytes, keratin-digesting moulds of the genera Trichophyton, Microsporum and Epidermophyton, which infect skin, hair and nails but not living tissue. Species may be spread from people, animals (Microsporum canis from cats and dogs, Trichophyton verrucosum from cattle and goats) or soil, and animal contact is common in rural Pakistan. Scalp and nail infections cannot be cured with topical agents, because the fungus lies within the hair follicle or nail plate, so oral treatment is needed: griseofulvin or terbinafine for 6-8 weeks in tinea capitis (griseofulvin is preferred for Microsporum), and terbinafine or itraconazole for several months in onychomycosis. Body, groin and foot infections respond to topical terbinafine or an azole. Topical steroids, often sold over the counter, suppress inflammation while the fungus spreads, producing the atypical "tinea incognito". Family members and pets should be examined, and combs and caps not shared.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Topical antifungal cream: Cream treats the skin surface but cannot reach the fungus inside his hair shafts, where the spores are seen.
  • B. Topical steroid cream: A steroid would calm the inflammation while the fungus spreads, enlarging his bald patches.
  • C. Oral antibiotics for 7 days: Antibiotics would treat the pustules only; the spores around his hair shafts are fungal.
  • D. Shaving the head: Shaving removes infected hair but leaves fungus in the follicles, so his patches would continue to spread.

Key point: Tinea capitis and onychomycosis need oral antifungals such as griseofulvin or terbinafine; topical treatment alone fails and steroids worsen it.
