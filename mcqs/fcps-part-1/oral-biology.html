Question 1 · Embryology of the face, mouth and teeth · easy

A 10-year-old boy presents to an orthodontic clinic in Gilgit with a congenital alveolar cleft located strictly between the upper right lateral incisor (12) and upper right canine (13). Periapical radiography confirms a bony defect extending posteromedially to the incisive fossa, while the bone supporting all four maxillary incisors is a single continuous segment.

The intact anterior bony segment bearing the four maxillary incisors develops from which embryonic structure?

  1. A. Lateral palatine shelves
  2. B. Intermaxillary segment
  3. C. Lateral nasal prominences
  4. D. Mandibular prominences
  5. E. Frontonasal prominence ectoderm alone
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Intermaxillary segment

As the paired medial nasal prominences are pushed toward the midline by the expanding maxillary prominences during the sixth week of development, they merge at the surface and at a deeper level to form the intermaxillary segment. This wedge-shaped structure comprises three components: a labial component that forms the philtrum of the upper lip; an upper jaw component that carries the four maxillary incisor teeth; and a palatal component that forms the triangular primary palate anterior to the incisive foramen. Because the intermaxillary segment carries all four maxillary incisors, an alveolar cleft resulting from failed fusion between the intermaxillary segment and the maxillary prominence classically passes between the maxillary lateral incisor and the canine (or occasionally through a duplicated lateral incisor germ).

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Lateral palatine shelves: The lateral palatine shelves grow medially from the maxillary prominences to form the secondary palate posterior to the incisive foramen, which does not carry the incisor teeth.
  • C. Lateral nasal prominences: The lateral nasal prominences form only the alae (wings) of the nose and do not contribute to the upper lip margin or the tooth-bearing maxillary alveolus.
  • D. Mandibular prominences: The mandibular prominences merge in the midline to form the lower lip, chin, and lower jaw rather than the premaxillary segment of the upper jaw.
  • E. Frontonasal prominence ectoderm alone: Although the medial nasal prominences arise from the frontonasal region, the bony premaxillary segment requires neural crest ectomesenchyme within the merged intermaxillary segment, not ectoderm alone.

Key point: Identify the three components of the intermaxillary segment and explain why anterior alveolar clefts pass between the lateral incisor and canine.

Question 2 · Oral histology: enamel, dentine, pulp and periodontium · easy

A forensic odontologist in Gilgit examines a longitudinal ground section of a primary mandibular first molar (74) under transmitted light to determine whether an infant survived for at least 2 weeks after a difficult premature birth. The section shows fine rhythmic transverse markings along each enamel rod spaced 4 µm apart, broader brownish concentric lines spaced ~25-30 µm apart, and one exceptionally prominent, dark incremental line separating prenatal from postnatal enamel.

Which line separates prenatal from postnatal enamel?

  1. A. Hunter-Schreger band
  2. B. Neonatal line
  3. C. Perikymata
  4. D. Enamel lamella
  5. E. Cross-striation
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Neonatal line

Enamel formation proceeds rhythmically, leaving two sets of incremental markings visible in longitudinal and transverse ground sections: (1) Cross-striations are fine periodic transverse bands across individual enamel rods spaced approximately 4 µm apart, representing the circadian (24-hour/daily) secretory rate of ameloblasts; and (2) Incremental striae of Retzius are broader brownish bands (in transmitted light) spaced 20 to 40 µm apart—each spanning roughly 7 to 10 daily cross-striations (a circaseptan rhythm)—that reflect systemic physiological pauses and slight bending of the rods with narrowing of Tomes processes. Over the cusp tip or incisal edge, striae of Retzius form closed dome-like arcs that do not reach the surface; along the lateral (cervical two-thirds) surfaces of the crown, they run obliquely upward and outward from the amelodentinal junction to terminate at the outer enamel surface as a series of shallow wave-like transverse grooves called perikymata (separated by imbrication ridges of Pickerill), which gradually wear away with age and toothbrushing. In all 20 deciduous teeth and the cusps of the permanent first molars (the only teeth mineralising at the time of birth), the physiological stress and nutritional transition of birth produces a step-like, accentuated stria of Retzius called the neonatal line, separating smoother, more homogeneous prenatal enamel from more defective postnatal enamel.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Hunter-Schreger band: Hunter-Schreger bands are an optical effect of rods crossing each other, not a birth marker.
  • C. Perikymata: Perikymata are the grooves where striae of Retzius reach the enamel surface.
  • D. Enamel lamella: Lamellae are hypomineralised cracks running in from the surface.
  • E. Cross-striation: Cross-striations are the fine daily lines; the prominent birth line is a different marker.

Key point: Distinguish daily cross-striations (~4 µm), circaseptan striae of Retzius (~7-10 days), surface perikymata, and the neonatal line (and identify which teeth exhibit a neonatal line).

Question 3 · Orofacial anatomy for dentistry · easy

During an oral anatomy tutorial at a dental college in Peshawar, a lecturer compares a histological section of the adult mandibular condyle and articular eminence with a section of the distal femur from the knee joint. Unlike the knee joint, the load-bearing surface of the jaw joint lacks hyaline cartilage at its outermost layer.

What tissue directly covers the load-bearing articular surfaces of the adult mandibular condyle and articular eminence?

  1. A. Elastic cartilage rich in elastin fibres
  2. B. Hyaline cartilage with a vascular perichondrium
  3. C. Dense avascular fibrous tissue (fibrocartilage)
  4. D. Keratinised stratified squamous epithelium
  5. E. Vascular synovial membrane over the central load zone
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Dense avascular fibrous tissue (fibrocartilage)

The temporomandibular joint (TMJ) is a synovial joint that differs histologically from almost all other weight-bearing synovial joints in the body. Because both the squamous part of the temporal bone and the mandible ossify intramembranously rather than from a preformed hyaline cartilage model, their articular surfaces are covered by a layer of dense, avascular, non-innervated fibrous connective tissue (predominantly type I collagen fibres running parallel to the surface) containing variable numbers of cartilage cells, often termed fibrocartilage. Beneath this superficial fibrous articular zone on the condyle lies a proliferative cell zone that serves as a source of reserve cells, followed by a fibrocartilaginous zone and calcified cartilage. Compared with hyaline cartilage, this fibrous covering resists shearing forces better, undergoes less age-related degeneration, and has superior capacity for biological repair and remodelling.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Elastic cartilage rich in elastin fibres: Elastic cartilage forms flexible structures such as the external ear pinna and epiglottis, whereas the jaw joint surface is dense collagenous tissue.
  • B. Hyaline cartilage with a vascular perichondrium: Hyaline cartilage lines typical diarthrodial joints developed from endochondral bones without perichondrium, whereas the TMJ develops intramembranously with a fibrous covering.
  • D. Keratinised stratified squamous epithelium: Stratified squamous epithelium lines the oral mucosa and skin, whereas synovial joint surfaces are connective tissue structures lacking an epithelial lining.
  • E. Vascular synovial membrane over the central load zone: Synovial membrane lines only the non-load-bearing inner aspect of the joint capsule and retrodiscal pad, stopping at the margins of the articular surfaces.

Key point: Contrast the dense avascular fibrous (fibrocartilaginous) articular covering of the temporomandibular joint with the hyaline cartilage of typical synovial joints.

Question 4 · Tooth morphology and occlusion · easy

During a tooth-identification spot test at a dental college in Quetta, a student is handed an extracted permanent anterior tooth with a crown length of 10.5 mm and a mesiodistal crown width of 8.5 mm (the widest of any anterior tooth). Viewed from the labial aspect, one incisal angle is a sharp nearly 90-degree right angle while the other incisal angle is noticeably more rounded; in cervical cross-section, the single conical root has a rounded-triangular outline.

Which tooth is this?

  1. A. Permanent mandibular central incisor
  2. B. Permanent maxillary central incisor
  3. C. Permanent maxillary lateral incisor
  4. D. Permanent maxillary canine
  5. E. Primary maxillary central incisor
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Permanent maxillary central incisor

The permanent maxillary central incisor (11 and 21) is the most prominent tooth in the human mouth and possesses several unmistakable morphological superlatives in Wheeler dental anatomy: (1) Crown Dimensions: It has the widest mesiodistal crown dimension (~8.5 to 9.0 mm) of any anterior tooth, with a cervico-incisal crown length of ~10.5 to 11.0 mm (so crown length is ~2 mm greater than width—unlike the primary maxillary central incisor, which is slightly wider mesiodistally than it is long!). Newly erupted incisors display three rounded protuberances on the incisal ridge called mamelons (mesial, middle, and distal) separated by two developmental grooves on the labial face. (2) Mesial vs Distal Asymmetry (Side Identification): The mesial crown outline is relatively straight, meeting the incisal edge at a sharp, nearly 90-degree right angle (mesioincisal angle), with the mesial contact area located in the incisal third right near the mesioincisal corner. Conversely, the distal crown outline is more convex, meeting the incisal edge at an obtuse, rounded distoincisal angle, with the distal contact area positioned more cervically at the junction of the incisal and middle thirds. (3) Lingual Aspect and Cervical Line: The palatal surface features a well-developed convex cervical cingulum (slightly off-centre toward the distal), mesial and distal marginal ridges, and a broad shallow lingual fossa; the mesial cervical line (CEJ) exhibits the greatest coronal curvature (~3.5 mm toward the incisal edge) of any tooth in the human dentition. (4) Root Morphology: It has a single, straight, stout conical root (~13 mm long) whose cervical cross-section is a rounded triangle (base facing labially, apex facing lingually, mesial side slightly longer than distal) without proximal root concavities—explaining why the maxillary central incisor is the classic tooth extracted using primary rotational (twisting) forceps movements!

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Permanent mandibular central incisor: The lower central incisor is small and symmetrical, with equally sharp angles.
  • C. Permanent maxillary lateral incisor: In all maxillary incisors, the mesioincisal angle is sharper than the distoincisal angle, and the maxillary lateral incisor has a narrower crown (~6.5 mm) with an oval (mesiodistally flattened) root cross-section.
  • D. Permanent maxillary canine: The maxillary canine has a pointed cusp with two long incisal slopes rather than a straight incisal edge, and its mesial contact is at the junction of the incisal and middle thirds.
  • E. Primary maxillary central incisor: While a primary maxillary central incisor is indeed the only incisor wider mesiodistally (~6.5 mm) than its crown length (~6.0 mm), a 10.5 mm long by 8.5 mm wide crown is a permanent maxillary central incisor (whose crown length exceeds its width).

Key point: Identify the permanent maxillary central incisor by its 8.5 mm mesiodistal width, sharp mesioincisal vs rounded distoincisal angle, incisal-third mesial vs junctional distal contact, 3.5 mm mesial CEJ curvature, and rounded-triangular root cross-section.

Question 5 · Oral mucosa, saliva, oral physiology and eruption · easy

During an oral histology practical at a dental college in Peshawar, a first-year BDS student examines three stained sections of human salivary glands: Gland A consists entirely of dark-staining pyramidal acinar cells with spherical basal nuclei; Gland B consists predominantly of dark pyramidal acini mixed with pale tubular acini capped by crescent-shaped demilunes; and Gland C consists almost exclusively of pale, foamy mucin-filled tubules with flattened basal nuclei.

Which glands are pure serous, like Gland A?

  1. A. Submandibular and sublingual
  2. B. Sublingual and palatine glands
  3. C. Parotid and von Ebner glands
  4. D. All three major glands
  5. E. Labial and buccal minor glands
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Parotid and von Ebner glands

Human salivary glands are compound tubuloacinar exocrine glands classified histologically by the secretory product of their terminal acinar endpieces into serous, mucous, or mixed seromucous glands: (1) Pure serous glands consist entirely of pyramidal serous cells containing spherical basal nuclei, abundant basal rough endoplasmic reticulum, and eosinophilic apical zymogen granules rich in alpha-amylase (ptyalin), proline-rich proteins, and peroxidase, surrounding a very narrow star-shaped lumen with intercellular canaliculi. Only two human salivary glands are purely serous: the major parotid gland (which also characteristically accumulates abundant unilocular adipocytes in its connective tissue stroma with age) and the minor lingual glands of von Ebner (which open into the trenches of the circumvallate and foliate papillae). (2) Mixed seromucous glands contain both serous and mucous cells: the major submandibular gland is mixed but predominantly serous (~80% serous acini and ~20% mucous tubules capped by serous demilunes of Giannuzzi/von Ebner, which in live freeze-substituted tissue actually sit aligned in the acinar wall and discharge into the lumen via intercellular canaliculi). (3) Predominantly or purely mucous glands consist of pale-staining tubular mucous cells packed with water-soluble mucinogen droplets (staining positively with periodic acid-Schiff [PAS], Alcian blue, and mucicarmine) that compress the nucleus flat against the basal cell membrane around a wide lumen: the major sublingual gland and minor labial, buccal, and anterior lingual (glands of Blandin and Nuhn) are mixed but overwhelmingly mucous, whereas the minor palatine, glossopalatine, and posterior lingual (Weber) glands are purely mucous.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Submandibular and sublingual: This reverses the gland types: the parotid and von Ebner lingual glands are pure serous, whereas the submandibular gland is mixed (predominantly serous).
  • B. Sublingual and palatine glands: Both the sublingual gland (predominantly mucous) and the minor palatine glands (pure mucous) secrete viscous mucin rather than pure watery serous fluid.
  • D. All three major glands: Almost all minor salivary glands (approximately 600 to 1,000 across the oral mucosa) are predominantly or purely mucous—with the sole exception of the pure serous glands of von Ebner.
  • E. Labial and buccal minor glands: Labial and buccal minor glands are mixed, mainly mucous.

Key point: Classify all major and minor human salivary glands into pure serous (parotid, von Ebner), mixed predominantly serous (submandibular), mixed predominantly mucous (sublingual, labial, buccal, Blandin-Nuhn), and pure mucous (palatine, glossopalatine, Weber).

Question 6 · Embryology of the face, mouth and teeth · easy

A 9-month-old girl from Kohat is examined in a cleft clinic for a midline palatal defect that extends anteriorly through the soft palate and hard palate but stops abruptly at a small midline depression just behind the maxillary central incisors, leaving the anterior alveolar ridge intact.

Which adult anatomical landmark marks the embryonic junction where this defect terminates anteriorly?

  1. A. Greater palatine foramen
  2. B. Incisive foramen
  3. C. Lesser palatine foramen
  4. D. Palatomaxillary suture
  5. E. Posterior nasal spine
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Incisive foramen

The definitive palate develops from two separate embryonic primordia: the primary palate and the secondary palate. The primary palate arises in the sixth week from the deep palatal component of the intermaxillary segment (formed by merged medial nasal prominences) and ossifies to form the premaxillary portion of the maxilla lodging the four incisor teeth. The secondary palate develops in the seventh and eighth weeks from two shelf-like outgrowths of the maxillary prominences called the lateral palatine processes (shelves), which elevate above the tongue and fuse in the midline. In the adult hard palate, the suture junction between the primary palate anteriorly and the secondary palate posteriorly is marked in the midline by the incisive foramen (fossa), making it the clinical dividing line between anterior and posterior palatal clefts.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Greater palatine foramen: The greater palatine foramen lies posterolaterally near the last molar and transmits vessels to the hard palate rather than marking the junction with the primary palate.
  • C. Lesser palatine foramen: The lesser palatine foramen is located in the pyramidal process of the palatine bone and transmits nerves to the soft palate, far posterior to the primary palate.
  • D. Palatomaxillary suture: The transverse palatomaxillary suture lies between the horizontal plate of the palatine bone and the palatine process of the maxilla, well within the secondary palate itself.
  • E. Posterior nasal spine: The posterior nasal spine is at the posterior border of the hard palate where the uvular muscle attaches, not at the anterior boundary of the secondary palate.

Key point: Identify the incisive foramen as the developmental boundary between the primary and secondary palate.

Question 7 · Oral histology: enamel, dentine, pulp and periodontium · easy

During an endodontic seminar at a dental college in Peshawar, a lecturer compares the action of 17% EDTA (which chelates calcium ions) and 5.25% sodium hypochlorite (which dissolves organic proteins) on root canal walls. Both irrigants are needed because mature dentine contains substantial proportions of both inorganic mineral and fibrous organic matrix.

What is the approximate composition of mature dentine by weight?

  1. A. 96% mineral, 1% organic, 3% water
  2. B. 50% mineral, 40% collagen, 10% water
  3. C. 70% mineral, 20% organic, 10% water
  4. D. 85% mineral, 5% elastin, 10% water
  5. E. 60% carbonate, 30% keratin, 10% water
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. 70% mineral, 20% organic, 10% water

Mature human dentine is a vital, hydrated, mineralized connective tissue that forms the bulk of the tooth crown and root. By weight, mature dentine is composed of approximately 70% inorganic material (small plate-like carbonated hydroxyapatite crystals that are much thinner and shorter than those of enamel), 20% organic material, and 10% water. Because of the density difference between hydroxyapatite and hydrated proteins, expressing dentine composition by volume gives approximately 45% to 50% inorganic mineral, 30% organic matrix, and 20% to 25% water—a critical parameter in adhesive dentistry where over-drying etched dentine collapses the 30% volume collagen network. Roughly 90% of the organic matrix of dentine consists of type I collagen fibrils (with traces of types III and V collagen), while the remaining 10% comprises non-collagenous matrix proteins—chiefly dentine phosphoprotein (DPP/phosphophoryn) and dentine sialoprotein (DSP), both cleaved from the single precursor protein dentine sialophosphoprotein (DSPP)—along with dentine matrix protein-1 (DMP1), osteocalcin, osteonectin, and proteoglycans.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. 96% mineral, 1% organic, 3% water: These figures represent mature dental enamel rather than dentine, which has a much higher organic collagen and water content.
  • B. 50% mineral, 40% collagen, 10% water: Mature dentine is 70% mineral by weight (not 50%), and its collagen framework is approximately 90% type I collagen rather than type III collagen.
  • D. 85% mineral, 5% elastin, 10% water: Dentine contains no elastin fibres and is 70% (not 85%) mineral by weight, making it softer and more elastic than enamel.
  • E. 60% carbonate, 30% keratin, 10% water: The mineral phase of dentine is carbonated hydroxyapatite rather than calcium carbonate, and its organic matrix is type I collagen rather than epithelial keratin.

Key point: State the composition of mature dentine by weight (70% inorganic, 20% organic, 10% water) and by volume (45-50% inorganic, 30% organic, 20-25% water) and identify type I collagen and DSPP as its major organic constituents.

Question 8 · Orofacial anatomy for dentistry · easy

A 40-year-old man attends a neurology and dental clinic in Mardan with difficulty opening his mouth against resistance and inability to protrude his chin forward. When asked to open his jaw wide, his chin deviates markedly toward the right side.

Paralysis of which muscle of mastication and on which side accounts for this clinical presentation?

  1. A. Left lateral pterygoid muscle
  2. B. Left masseter muscle
  3. C. Right lateral pterygoid muscle
  4. D. Right medial pterygoid muscle
  5. E. Right posterior temporalis fibres
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Right lateral pterygoid muscle

Of the four principal muscles of mastication (masseter, temporalis, medial pterygoid, and lateral pterygoid), three—the masseter, temporalis, and medial pterygoid—are elevators that close the jaws, whereas only the lateral pterygoid assists in depressing (opening) the mandible. The lateral pterygoid arises by two heads: an upper head from the infratemporal surface and crest of the greater wing of the sphenoid (inserting mainly into the joint capsule and anterior band of the articular disc) and a lower head from the lateral surface of the lateral pterygoid plate (inserting into the pterygoid fovea on the anterior neck of the mandibular condyle). Bilateral contraction pulls both condyles and discs forward and downward onto the articular eminences to protrude and depress the mandible. In unilateral contraction, the ipsilateral condyle is pulled forward and medially, swinging the chin to the opposite side; therefore, in unilateral V3 or lateral pterygoid palsy, the unopposed healthy muscle pushes the chin toward the paralysed side.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Left lateral pterygoid muscle: Paralysis of the left lateral pterygoid muscle causes the chin to deviate toward the paralysed left side on opening due to the unopposed forward pull of the intact right muscle.
  • B. Left masseter muscle: The masseter is a powerful elevator of the mandible during biting and clenching rather than the primary muscle that opens the jaw or pulls the condyle forward.
  • D. Right medial pterygoid muscle: Although a paralysed right medial pterygoid could reduce forceful elevation and contralateral grinding, the medial pterygoid elevates rather than depresses the mandible during mouth opening.
  • E. Right posterior temporalis fibres: The horizontal posterior fibres of the temporalis muscle retract the mandible backward into the glenoid fossa rather than protruding or depressing it.

Key point: Identify the lateral pterygoid as the sole primary muscle of mastication that depresses and protrudes the mandible and explain why unilateral paralysis deviates the chin toward the lesion.

Question 9 · Tooth morphology and occlusion · easy

During a dental anatomy practical exam in Gilgit, a student is given two extracted permanent mandibular incisors: Tooth X has a mesiodistal crown width of 5.0 mm, is bilaterally symmetrical with sharp 90-degree mesioincisal and distoincisal angles, and has both contact areas at the exact same level in the incisal third; Tooth Y has a crown width of 5.5 mm and, when viewed from the incisal aspect, its incisal edge is twisted distolingually relative to the labiolingual root bisector.

Which tooth is Tooth X?

  1. A. Permanent mandibular lateral incisor
  2. B. Primary maxillary lateral incisor
  3. C. Permanent mandibular canine
  4. D. Permanent mandibular central incisor
  5. E. Permanent maxillary lateral incisor
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. Permanent mandibular central incisor

The permanent mandibular central (31, 41) and lateral (32, 42) incisors share smooth, featureless lingual surfaces (lacking grooves or pits and bathed continuously by Wharton and Rivinus salivary ducts, which makes them the teeth most resistant to dental caries in the mouth but most prone to supragingival calculus deposition) and broad, ribbon-like roots that are heavily flattened mesiodistally with deep longitudinal developmental depressions on both mesial and distal root surfaces (with the distal depression deeper)—an anatomy that produces two separate root canals (a labial canal and a lingual canal that usually rejoin near the apex, Vertucci Type III/II) in 20% to 40% of mandibular incisors! However, comparing the mandibular central and lateral incisors reveals three classic exam distinctions: (1) Size Reversal Relative to the Maxilla: Whereas the maxillary central incisor is much larger than the maxillary lateral incisor, in the mandible the relationship is REVERSED—the mandibular central incisor is the smallest and narrowest tooth in the entire permanent dentition (~5.0 mm mesiodistal width by 9.0 mm crown length), while the mandibular lateral incisor is larger (~5.5 mm wide by 9.5 mm crown length, with a longer root of 14.0 mm vs 12.5 mm). (2) Bilateral Symmetry of the Mandibular Central Incisor: The mandibular central incisor is the most bilaterally symmetrical tooth in the human mouth: its mesial and distal outlines taper evenly, both its mesioincisal and distoincisal angles are equally sharp (~90° right angles), both its mesial and distal contact areas sit at the exact same horizontal level in the incisal third, and from the incisal view its straight incisal edge stands strictly perpendicular (90°) to the labiolingual bisecting line of the crown and root (making left 31 and right 41 virtually impossible to tell apart when extracted!). (3) Distolingual Twist of the Mandibular Lateral Incisor: On the mandibular lateral incisor, the mesial side of the crown is slightly longer than the distal side (so the incisal edge slopes downward distally to a more rounded distoincisal angle and a slightly lower distal contact area, though still in the incisal third), the cingulum is shifted slightly distally, and from the incisal view the crown and incisal edge are twisted distolingually on the root base to conform to the sharper curvature of the mandibular arch.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Permanent mandibular lateral incisor: The lower lateral incisor is the wider tooth (Tooth Y), with its edge twisted distolingually.
  • B. Primary maxillary lateral incisor: These are permanent mandibular incisors.
  • C. Permanent mandibular canine: A canine has a cusp, not a straight incisal edge.
  • E. Permanent maxillary lateral incisor: The upper lateral incisor is wider and has rounded, unequal angles.

Key point: Distinguish the permanent mandibular central incisor (smallest/narrowest tooth at 5.0 mm, bilaterally symmetrical, 90° incisal angles, incisal edge perpendicular to labiolingual axis) from the permanent mandibular lateral incisor (larger at 5.5 mm, distolingually twisted incisal edge and distally offset cingulum), and note the 20-40% incidence of a second (lingual) root canal.

Question 10 · Oral mucosa, saliva, oral physiology and eruption · easy

A clinical physiology demonstration in Kohat collects parotid saliva using a Lashley cup from a healthy 22-year-old volunteer first at rest (flow rate 0.05 mL/min) and then while applying 2% citric acid to the tongue (stimulated flow rate 1.8 mL/min). As the flow rate increases 36-fold, the pH of the collected saliva rises from 6.1 to 7.6 and its sodium and bicarbonate concentrations increase markedly.

Why do salivary sodium, bicarbonate and pH rise at high flow rates?

  1. A. Acinar cells secrete acid instead of water
  2. B. Acini make hypertonic fluid; ducts add water
  3. C. Saliva is a passive filtrate of blood
  4. D. Less duct time to reabsorb sodium
  5. E. Oral bacteria consume the bicarbonate
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. Less duct time to reabsorb sodium

The formation of saliva follows the classic two-stage model established by Thaysen et al.: In Stage 1 (within the secretory acini and intercalated ducts), autonomic stimulation triggers intracellular Ca2+ release that opens apical TMEM16A (ANO1) chloride channels and basolateral K+ channels; Cl- enters the acinar lumen, drawing Na+ paracellularly across leaky tight junctions and water transcellularly (via aquaporin-5 water channels) and paracellularly to produce an isotonic primary secretion whose electrolyte concentrations ([Na+] ~140 mEq/L, [Cl-] ~105 mEq/L, [K+] ~5 mEq/L, [HCO3-] ~25 mEq/L, ~300 mOsm/L) closely mirror blood plasma. In Stage 2 (within the striated and excretory ducts), the ductal epithelium is devoid of aquaporins and is impermeable to water, while basolateral Na+/K+-ATPase and apical ENaC/CFTR channels actively reabsorb Na+ and Cl- and secrete K+ and HCO3-. At low, unstimulated resting flow rates, primary fluid moves slowly through the striated ducts, allowing maximal modification: resting saliva becomes strongly hypotonic (~50-70 mOsm/L), very low in Na+ (~5-15 mEq/L) and Cl- (~15 mEq/L), high in K+ (~20-30 mEq/L, 4 to 6 times plasma!), and low in HCO3- (~3-5 mEq/L, pH ~6.2-6.8, where dihydrophosphate H2PO4-/HPO42- is the main buffer). During vigorous gustatory or masticatory stimulation, primary saliva surges through the striated ducts so rapidly that there is insufficient contact time for Na+/Cl- reabsorption or K+ enrichment, AND parasympathetic stimulation directly activates ductal carbonic anhydrase to pump HCO3- into the lumen. Consequently, in stimulated saliva, [Na+], [Cl-], and especially [HCO3-] (rising to 40-60 mEq/L) increase sharply, [K+] drops toward ~15 mEq/L, and pH climbs to 7.4 to 7.8—making the carbonic acid/bicarbonate (H2CO3/HCO3-) system responsible for >85% of the buffering capacity of stimulated saliva.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Acinar cells secrete acid instead of water: Acinar cells dramatically increase isotonic fluid secretion during stimulation and never secrete hydrochloric acid; moreover, salivary pH rises (becomes alkaline, ~7.4-7.8) rather than falling at high flow rates.
  • B. Acini make hypertonic fluid; ducts add water: Stage 1 primary acinar fluid is isotonic (plasma-like, high NaCl), and Stage 2 striated ducts are completely impermeable to water.
  • C. Saliva is a passive filtrate of blood: Saliva is NOT a passive ultrafiltrate: active Cl- and Na+ transport in acini can generate secretory pressures exceeding systemic arterial blood pressure, and striated ducts actively modify electrolytes using ATP.
  • E. Oral bacteria consume the bicarbonate: Inside the sterile parotid duct, bicarbonate concentration rises up to 40-60 mEq/L (exceeding plasma) during stimulation, providing the principal buffer against Stephan curve plaque acid drops.

Key point: Explain the two-stage mechanism of salivary secretion (isotonic acinar Stage 1 via AQP5/TMEM16A vs water-impermeable striated duct Stage 2) and detail how high stimulated flow rates alter [Na+], [Cl-], [K+], [HCO3-], pH, and buffering systems (phosphate at rest vs bicarbonate when stimulated).
