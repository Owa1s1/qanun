Question 1 · Biostatistics, epidemiology and research · easy

In a district during one year, there were 6,000 live births and 90 stillbirths. During the same year, 360 children died before their first birthday, of whom 240 died in the first 28 days of life. The district health officer wants to report the infant mortality rate.

What is the infant mortality rate?

  1. A. 6 per 1,000 population
  2. B. 40 per 1,000 live births
  3. C. 54 per 1,000 total births
  4. D. 60 per 1,000 live births
  5. E. 75 per 1,000 live births
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. 60 per 1,000 live births

The infant mortality rate (IMR) is the number of deaths of infants under 1 year of age in a year divided by the number of live births in the same year, multiplied by 1,000: 360/6,000 × 1,000 = 60 per 1,000 live births. The neonatal mortality rate counts deaths in the first 28 days per 1,000 live births (here 240/6,000 × 1,000 = 40). Stillbirths are not included in IMR but are included in the perinatal mortality rate (stillbirths plus early neonatal deaths in the first week, per 1,000 total births). IMR is a sensitive indicator of a population's health, nutrition and health services; Pakistan's rate remains among the highest in the region.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. 6 per 1,000 population: Infant mortality uses live births.
  • B. 40 per 1,000 live births: This is the neonatal mortality rate.
  • C. 54 per 1,000 total births: Uses the wrong numerator and denominator.
  • E. 75 per 1,000 live births: Not the correct calculation.

Key point: Infant mortality rate = deaths under 1 year ÷ live births in the year × 1,000.

Question 2 · Biostatistics, epidemiology and research · easy

A cohort study of men in Karachi finds that the risk of lung cancer rises steadily with the number of cigarettes smoked per day: compared with non-smokers, the relative risk is 5 for 1-10 cigarettes a day, 10 for 11-20 and 20 for more than 20. Researchers use this finding to strengthen the argument that smoking causes lung cancer.

Which criterion of causation does this finding support?

  1. A. Temporality
  2. B. Biological gradient
  3. C. Specificity
  4. D. Analogy
  5. E. Experimental evidence
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Biological gradient

The Bradford Hill criteria are considerations used to judge whether an observed association is likely to be causal: strength of association, consistency across studies, specificity, temporality (cause before effect), biological gradient (dose-response), plausibility, coherence with existing knowledge, experimental evidence (for example, reduced risk after stopping exposure) and analogy. A dose-response relationship, in which risk rises with increasing exposure, makes a causal explanation more likely, because confounding or bias would rarely produce such a regular gradient. Smoking and lung cancer satisfy almost all the criteria. Only temporality is essential; the others add weight but are not required.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Temporality: Concerns cause before effect.
  • C. Specificity: One cause leading to one effect.
  • D. Analogy: Similar effects of similar exposures.
  • E. Experimental evidence: Evidence from intervention or removal.

Key point: A dose-response relationship (biological gradient) is one of the Bradford Hill criteria supporting causation.

Question 3 · Biostatistics, epidemiology and research · easy

In a study of pain after caesarean section in a hospital in Lahore, nurses record each woman's pain at 24 hours as none, mild, moderate or severe. The categories have a natural order, but the difference between "mild" and "moderate" is not necessarily the same as between "moderate" and "severe". The researchers must choose suitable statistical methods.

What type of variable is this pain rating?

  1. A. Nominal
  2. B. Ordinal
  3. C. Continuous
  4. D. Discrete count
  5. E. Ratio
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Ordinal

Data can be categorical (qualitative) or numerical (quantitative). Categorical data are nominal (categories with no order, such as blood group or sex) or ordinal (categories with a natural order but unequal or unknown intervals, such as pain ratings, cancer stages, NYHA class or Likert scales). Numerical data are discrete (counts, such as number of children) or continuous (measurements that can take any value, such as weight, blood pressure or haemoglobin). The type of data decides the summary measures and tests: ordinal data are usually summarised with medians or proportions and compared with non-parametric tests or chi-square, not with means and t-tests.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Nominal: Categories without order.
  • C. Continuous: Measured on a scale with any value.
  • D. Discrete count: A discrete count records whole numbers of events, not ordered categories.
  • E. Ratio: A continuous scale with a true zero.

Key point: Ordinal data are ordered categories with unequal intervals, such as pain ratings or disease stages.

Question 4 · Biostatistics, epidemiology and research · easy

A dataset of 500 women attending an antenatal clinic in Multan records each woman's blood group, number of previous pregnancies, haemoglobin concentration in g/dL, heart failure class (I-IV) for those with heart disease, and whether she lives in an urban or rural area. The statistician needs to identify which variable is continuous.

Which variable is continuous?

  1. A. Blood group
  2. B. Number of previous pregnancies
  3. C. Heart failure class
  4. D. Haemoglobin concentration
  5. E. Urban or rural residence
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. Haemoglobin concentration

A continuous variable is measured on a scale and can, in principle, take any value within a range, limited only by the precision of the measuring instrument, such as haemoglobin, height, weight, blood pressure or glucose. Discrete numerical variables can take only whole-number values, usually counts (number of pregnancies, number of admissions). Ordinal variables (heart failure class, cancer stage) and nominal variables (blood group, residence, sex) are categorical. Continuous data that are roughly normally distributed are summarised with the mean and standard deviation; skewed data with the median and interquartile range.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Blood group: Blood group is nominal.
  • B. Number of previous pregnancies: Number of previous pregnancies is a discrete count.
  • C. Heart failure class: Heart failure class is ordinal.
  • E. Urban or rural residence: Urban or rural residence is nominal (binary).

Key point: Continuous variables, such as haemoglobin, can take any value within a range; counts are discrete.

Question 5 · Biostatistics, epidemiology and research · easy

In a large survey of healthy adult women in Islamabad, haemoglobin values follow a normal distribution with a mean of 13.0 g/dL and a standard deviation of 1.0 g/dL. The laboratory wants to define a reference range that includes the central 95% of healthy women.

What is this reference range?

  1. A. 10 to 16 g/dL
  2. B. 11 to 15 g/dL
  3. C. 12 to 14 g/dL
  4. D. 12.5 to 13.5 g/dL
  5. E. 13 to 15 g/dL
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. 11 to 15 g/dL

In a normal (Gaussian) distribution, about 68% of values lie within 1 standard deviation of the mean, about 95% within 2 SD (more exactly 1.96 SD), and about 99.7% within 3 SD. A 95% reference range is therefore the mean ± 1.96 SD: 13.0 ± 2.0 = 11 to 15 g/dL. By definition, about 5% of healthy people (2.5% at each end) fall outside this range, so a value just outside it does not necessarily mean disease, and the more tests that are done on a healthy person, the more likely one will be "abnormal". Reference ranges also depend on the population studied, such as sex, age, pregnancy and altitude.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. 10 to 16 g/dL: Covers about 99.7% (3 SD each side).
  • C. 12 to 14 g/dL: Covers about 68% (1 SD each side).
  • D. 12.5 to 13.5 g/dL: 12.5 to 13.5 g/dL is only plus or minus 0.5 SD and covers about 38% of women.
  • E. 13 to 15 g/dL: 13 to 15 g/dL is not centred on the mean.

Key point: In a normal distribution, about 95% of values lie within mean ± 2 SD, the basis of reference ranges.

Question 6 · Biostatistics, epidemiology and research · easy

Researchers compare systolic blood pressure in 120 men who use a lot of salt and 130 men who use little salt. Blood pressure is roughly normally distributed in both groups, with similar spread. Each man belongs to only one group, and they want to test whether the mean systolic pressure differs between the groups.

Which statistical test is most appropriate?

  1. A. Paired t-test
  2. B. Unpaired t-test
  3. C. Chi-square test
  4. D. ANOVA
  5. E. Wilcoxon signed-rank test
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Unpaired t-test

The choice of test depends on the type of outcome, the number of groups, whether observations are paired, and the distribution. To compare means of a continuous, roughly normally distributed outcome between two independent groups, the unpaired (independent samples, two-sample) t-test is used. When measurements are paired (the same people before and after, or matched pairs), the paired t-test is used. Three or more groups are compared with one-way ANOVA. Categorical outcomes (proportions) are compared with the chi-square test. If data are not normally distributed, non-parametric equivalents are used: Mann-Whitney U (for unpaired) and Wilcoxon signed-rank (for paired).

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Paired t-test: For two measurements on the same people.
  • C. Chi-square test: The chi-square test compares categorical data, not means of a continuous measure.
  • D. ANOVA: For three or more groups.
  • E. Wilcoxon signed-rank test: For paired non-normal data.

Key point: The unpaired t-test compares the means of a normally distributed outcome between two independent groups.

Question 7 · Biostatistics, epidemiology and research · easy

Thirty adults with mild hypertension have their systolic blood pressure measured before and after an 8-week yoga course. The differences between each person's before and after readings are roughly normally distributed. The researchers want to test whether the course changed systolic blood pressure.

Which statistical test is most appropriate?

  1. A. Unpaired t-test
  2. B. Chi-square test
  3. C. One-way ANOVA
  4. D. Paired t-test
  5. E. Mann-Whitney U test
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. Paired t-test

When the same individuals are measured twice (before and after an intervention) or when subjects are matched in pairs, the observations are not independent. The paired t-test analyses the differences within each pair, removing variation between people and giving more power than an unpaired test. It assumes the differences are roughly normally distributed; if not, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test is used. Note that a before-and-after study without a control group cannot show that the yoga caused any change, because blood pressure may fall over time anyway (for example through regression to the mean or the Hawthorne effect).

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Unpaired t-test: Ignores the pairing of measurements.
  • B. Chi-square test: The chi-square test compares categorical data, not paired continuous readings.
  • C. One-way ANOVA: For three or more independent groups.
  • E. Mann-Whitney U test: For two independent non-normal groups.

Key point: The paired t-test compares two related measurements, such as before and after in the same people.

Question 8 · Biostatistics, epidemiology and research · easy

A study compares mean haemoglobin in pregnant women from three groups: those given daily iron, those given weekly iron, and those given no supplements. Haemoglobin is roughly normally distributed in each group, and the groups are independent. The researchers want a single test of whether the mean haemoglobin differs among the three groups.

Which statistical test is most appropriate?

  1. A. Repeated unpaired t-tests
  2. B. Chi-square test
  3. C. One-way ANOVA
  4. D. Paired t-test
  5. E. Pearson correlation
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. One-way ANOVA

Analysis of variance (ANOVA) compares the means of three or more independent groups in a single test, by comparing variation between group means with variation within groups (F test). Doing multiple t-tests between each pair increases the chance of a type I error. If ANOVA shows a significant difference, post-hoc tests (such as Tukey or Bonferroni-adjusted comparisons) identify which groups differ. ANOVA assumes approximately normal data and similar variances; if these are not met, the Kruskal-Wallis test is the non-parametric alternative. Repeated measures ANOVA is used when the same people are measured several times.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Repeated unpaired t-tests: Inflates the chance of a false positive.
  • B. Chi-square test: The chi-square test is for categorical data, not mean haemoglobin.
  • D. Paired t-test: A paired t-test compares two related measurements, not three independent groups.
  • E. Pearson correlation: For two continuous variables.

Key point: One-way ANOVA compares means of three or more independent groups; Kruskal-Wallis is its non-parametric equivalent.

Question 9 · Biostatistics, epidemiology and research · easy

A survey finds that 420 of 600 children in District A and 330 of 600 children in District B are fully vaccinated. The researchers want to test whether the proportion of fully vaccinated children differs between the two districts. All expected cell counts in the 2 × 2 table are well above 5.

Which statistical test is most appropriate?

  1. A. Chi-square test
  2. B. Unpaired t-test
  3. C. Paired t-test
  4. D. Pearson correlation
  5. E. ANOVA
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Chi-square test

The chi-square (χ²) test compares observed frequencies in a contingency table with the frequencies expected if there were no association, and is used to compare proportions between two or more independent groups for categorical outcomes. Here, 70% versus 55% vaccinated would be compared in a 2 × 2 table. When expected counts are small (usually any cell below 5), Fisher's exact test is used instead. For paired categorical data (the same people tested twice), McNemar's test is used. The chi-square test shows whether there is an association; measures such as the risk difference or odds ratio with confidence intervals show its size.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Unpaired t-test: An unpaired t-test compares means, not proportions.
  • C. Paired t-test: A paired t-test compares paired means, not two independent proportions.
  • D. Pearson correlation: For two continuous variables.
  • E. ANOVA: For comparing means of several groups.

Key point: The chi-square test compares proportions between independent groups; Fisher's exact test is used with small expected counts.

Question 10 · Biostatistics, epidemiology and research · easy

A guideline committee in Pakistan is deciding whether to recommend a drug to prevent postpartum haemorrhage. They have a case series from one hospital, a large cohort study, several expert opinions, a single randomised controlled trial and a systematic review with meta-analysis of 12 well-conducted randomised controlled trials, all addressing the same question.

Which provides the strongest evidence of the drug's effect?

  1. A. The case series
  2. B. The cohort study
  3. C. Expert opinion
  4. D. The systematic review
  5. E. The single randomised trial
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. The systematic review

Hierarchies of evidence rank study designs by how well they protect against bias when answering questions about treatment effects. From highest to lowest: systematic reviews and meta-analyses of randomised controlled trials, individual well-conducted randomised trials, cohort studies, case-control studies, case series and case reports, and expert opinion or mechanistic reasoning. A systematic review combines all relevant trials using explicit, reproducible methods, increasing precision and showing consistency. However, the quality of the individual studies matters: a meta-analysis of poor trials is not reliable, and systems such as GRADE rate the overall certainty of evidence. For questions about harm, prognosis or diagnosis, other designs may be most appropriate.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. The case series: A case series is low in the evidence hierarchy.
  • B. The cohort study: Observational; prone to confounding.
  • C. Expert opinion: Expert opinion is the weakest level of evidence.
  • E. The single randomised trial: Strong, but one trial can be misleading.

Key point: For treatment effects, systematic reviews of well-conducted RCTs give the strongest evidence; expert opinion is weakest.
