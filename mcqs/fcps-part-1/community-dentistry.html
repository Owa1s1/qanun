Question 1 · Dental public health, epidemiology and indices · easy

During an oral health survey of 12-year-old schoolchildren in Abbottabad, three newly graduated dental examiners record vastly different caries prevalence rates on the same cohort of 30 pupils during pilot testing. The principal investigator arranges joint calibration sessions using extracted teeth and clinical models until inter-examiner kappa exceeds 0.85.

Which epidemiological error was this calibration procedure designed to minimise?

  1. A. Confounding bias
  2. B. Recall bias
  3. C. Selection bias
  4. D. Information bias
  5. E. Attrition bias
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. Information bias

Information (or measurement) bias occurs when data are collected, measured, or classified inaccurately. In dental surveys, variation between different examiners (inter-examiner variability) or within the same examiner across time (intra-examiner variability) creates diagnostic misclassification. Conducting rigorous training and calibration sessions to achieve a high kappa statistic (>0.80) standardises diagnostic thresholds, directly reducing information bias.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Confounding bias: Confounding occurs when an extraneous variable distorts the true association between exposure and outcome, controlled by matching or multivariable regression.
  • B. Recall bias: Recall bias occurs when participants inconsistently remember past exposures or events based on whether they developed disease.
  • C. Selection bias: Selection bias arises from non-random sampling or systematic exclusion of eligible subjects from the study sample.
  • E. Attrition bias: Attrition bias results from differential loss to follow-up over time in longitudinal studies, not baseline examiner variability.

Key point: Recognise calibration as a primary method to prevent information bias in dental epidemiological surveys.

Question 2 · Preventive dentistry and fluorides · easy

A 14-year-old girl in Mardan attends an orthodontic clinic with early white spot lesions along the gingival margins of her upper anterior teeth. The dentist prescribes a neutral sodium fluoride mouthrinse to arrest demineralisation. The mechanism involves the conversion of carbonated hydroxyapatite into fluorapatite on the enamel surface.

What is the critical pH below which pure fluorapatite begins to dissolve?

  1. A. 3.5
  2. B. 4.5
  3. C. 5.5
  4. D. 6.2
  5. E. 6.8
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. 4.5

Demineralisation occurs when plaque pH drops below the critical pH of the mineral phase. Pure enamel hydroxyapatite dissolves when the pH falls below 5.5. When fluoride ions substitute for hydroxyl groups in the crystal lattice, fluorapatite or fluorhydroxyapatite is formed. Fluorapatite crystals are larger, chemically more stable, and have lower solubility in acid, lowering the critical pH to approximately 4.5. This allows enamel incorporated with fluoride to resist acid dissolution during typical dietary acid challenges until plaque pH drops below this lower threshold.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. 3.5: This pH is far below the dissolution threshold of fluorapatite and represents severe dietary acid attack.
  • C. 5.5: This is the classical critical pH for pure hydroxyapatite, whereas fluorapatite withstands significantly higher acidity.
  • D. 6.2: This is close to the critical pH for dental root dentine and cementum, not fluoridated enamel.
  • E. 6.8: This is close to normal resting salivary pH where supersaturation with calcium and phosphate maintains enamel integrity.

Key point: Identify the critical pH for fluorapatite dissolution compared to hydroxyapatite.

Question 3 · Dental public health, epidemiology and indices · easy

A 13-year-old boy attends a dental teaching hospital in Mardan for a routine preventive check-up. His permanent first and second molars have erupted, and the dentist is deciding where preventive care will matter most.

Which surface of a newly erupted permanent tooth is most prone to caries?

  1. A. Smooth cervical surface
  2. B. Occlusal pit and fissure
  3. C. Proximal surface of anterior teeth
  4. D. Lingual surface of lower incisors
  5. E. Buccal smooth surface of upper premolars
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Occlusal pit and fissure

Epidemiological studies consistently demonstrate that occlusal pits and fissures are the most susceptible anatomical sites for dental caries in permanent teeth. Their deep, narrow morphology prevents mechanical debridement by toothbrushes and prevents salivary buffering while offering an ideal microenvironment for aciduric biofilm accumulation. Proximal surfaces become vulnerable later, whereas lingual surfaces of mandibular anterior teeth are protected by salivary secretions from submandibular and sublingual glands.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Smooth cervical surface: Cervical smooth surfaces are prone to caries primarily in older adults with root exposure or individuals with persistent cervical plaque.
  • C. Proximal surface of anterior teeth: Proximal surfaces of incisors are relatively resistant compared to posterior fissures and develop caries later in high-caries-risk diets.
  • D. Lingual surface of lower incisors: Lingual surfaces of mandibular incisors are the most caries-resistant surfaces due to salivary flow and buffer capacity from submandibular ducts.
  • E. Buccal smooth surface of upper premolars: Smooth facial surfaces of premolars are cleaned by perioral muscles and mastication, exhibiting substantially lower susceptibility than occlusal pits.

Key point: Identify the hierarchy of dental caries susceptibility across different tooth surfaces.

Question 4 · Preventive dentistry and fluorides · easy

A community health team in Mirpur is reviewing the historical foundations of community water fluoridation. They examine the landmark 1945 interventional trial where sodium fluoride was first artificially added to the municipal public water supply to monitor caries reduction in school children over a 15-year period.

In which city was this first artificial water fluoridation study conducted?

  1. A. Brantford
  2. B. Colorado Springs
  3. C. Evanston
  4. D. Grand Rapids
  5. E. Newburgh
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. Grand Rapids

Community water fluoridation began as an interventional public health experiment on January 25, 1945, when Grand Rapids, Michigan, became the first city in the world to artificially adjust the fluoride content of its public water supply to 1.0 ppm (mg/L). Muskegon served as the unfluoridated control city. Conducted under H. Trendley Dean and the US Public Health Service, this 15-year trial demonstrated a 50-65% reduction in dental caries among school children without producing undesirable skeletal or systemic side effects.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Brantford: Brantford in Ontario, Canada, began community water fluoridation in June 1945, several months after the first US trial.
  • B. Colorado Springs: Colorado Springs was where Frederick McKay investigated naturally occurring mottled enamel (Colorado brown stain), not artificial fluoridation.
  • C. Evanston: Evanston, Illinois, started its community water fluoridation trial in 1946 with Oak Park as its control.
  • E. Newburgh: Newburgh, New York, started its fluoridation trial in May 1945, paired with Kingston as the control city.

Key point: Recall the historical site of the first artificial water fluoridation study in 1945.

Question 5 · Dental public health, epidemiology and indices · easy

A 24-year-old male bus conductor presents to a dental teaching hospital in Karachi with a 1-year history of progressive inability to open his mouth and a burning sensation when consuming spicy food. He reports chewing 8 to 10 packets of gutka daily since age 16. Clinical examination shows an inter-incisal opening of 18 mm, blanched, marble-like bilateral buccal mucosa, and dense, palpable vertical fibrous bands in both cheeks and the soft palate.

Which constituent is the primary etiological agent responsible for this condition?

  1. A. Areca nut
  2. B. Nicotine
  3. C. Slaked lime
  4. D. Betel leaf
  5. E. Menthol additives
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Areca nut

Oral submucous fibrosis (OSF) is a chronic, progressive, high-risk potentially malignant disorder strongly associated with the consumption of areca nut, commonly used in Pakistan in preparations such as gutka, paan, and mainpuri. Arecoline, the principal alkaloid in areca nut, stimulates mucosal fibroblasts to overproduce types I and III collagen while upregulating tissue inhibitor of metalloproteinases (TIMP-1) and downregulating matrix metalloproteinases, shifting the metabolic balance heavily toward collagen deposition. Flavan-3,4-diols in areca nut cross-link collagen fibrils, rendering them resistant to collagenase degradation. While slaked lime accelerates alkaloid release by raising pH, areca nut remains the indispensable biological driver of the subepithelial fibrosis and marked trismus characteristic of OSF.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Nicotine: Nicotine is the addictive alkaloid in tobacco responsible for systemic cardiovascular effects and addiction, but it does not independently cause the dense subepithelial fibrosis seen here.
  • C. Slaked lime: Slaked lime (calcium hydroxide) facilitates alkaloid absorption by raising local pH and causes superficial mucosal chemical irritation, but it cannot induce chronic fibrosis without areca nut.
  • D. Betel leaf: Betel leaf (Piper betle) contains essential oils and antioxidants with mild anti-inflammatory properties and does not possess the fibrogenic alkaloids found in the areca nut.
  • E. Menthol additives: Menthol and artificial flavouring agents provide cooling sensations in commercial gutka sachets, but they do not stimulate fibroblast proliferation or inhibit matrix metalloproteinases.

Key point: Identify areca nut as the primary etiological agent of oral submucous fibrosis.

Question 6 · Preventive dentistry and fluorides · easy

A mother brings her 18-month-old son to a dental teaching hospital in Peshawar for a first dental check-up. The child is healthy, asymptomatic, and drinks municipal piped water. Intraoral examination reveals 12 erupted primary teeth with plaque-free smooth surfaces and no clinical evidence of demineralisation or cavitation. The mother asks which type and quantity of dentifrice should be used for his daily oral hygiene.

Which toothpaste advice suits this child?

  1. A. Smear layer of toothpaste with at least 1,000 ppm fluoride
  2. B. Smear layer of non-fluoridated toothpaste
  3. C. Pea-sized amount of 500 ppm fluoride toothpaste
  4. D. Pea-sized amount of 1,000 ppm fluoride toothpaste
  5. E. Ribbon layer of 1,450 ppm fluoride toothpaste
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Smear layer of toothpaste with at least 1,000 ppm fluoride

Delivering Better Oral Health and WHO oral health guidelines advise that children from the eruption of the first tooth up to 3 years of age should have their teeth brushed twice daily using a smear (or grain-of-rice sized amount) of toothpaste containing at least 1,000 parts per million (ppm) fluoride. Non-fluoridated pastes fail to offer remineralisation, while sub-therapeutic concentrations like 500 ppm show significantly less caries-preventive efficacy in clinical trials. A smear minimises inadvertent systemic ingestion while maximising local salivary and biofilm fluoride concentration, safeguarding against the development of dental fluorosis during the critical calcification window of the permanent anterior incisors.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Smear layer of non-fluoridated toothpaste: Non-fluoridated dentifrice provides no anti-caries protection, which is necessary twice daily even in children without active cavitated lesions.
  • C. Pea-sized amount of 500 ppm fluoride toothpaste: A 500 ppm fluoride dentifrice provides inferior enamel remineralisation, and a pea-sized volume is excessive for children younger than 3 years.
  • D. Pea-sized amount of 1,000 ppm fluoride toothpaste: A pea-sized amount increases the risk of excessive systemic ingestion and mild fluorosis in an 18-month-old with an immature swallowing reflex.
  • E. Ribbon layer of 1,450 ppm fluoride toothpaste: A ribbon layer dispenses far too much paste, vastly exceeding the recommended smear and posing an unnecessary risk of systemic fluoride ingestion.

Key point: Identify the recommended fluoride toothpaste concentration and quantity for children under 3 years of age.

Question 7 · Dental public health, epidemiology and indices · easy

A 9-year-old boy is brought to a hospital dental clinic in Abbottabad after tripping while running in school. Clinical examination reveals an uncomplicated enamel-dentine fracture of the maxillary right central incisor, tooth 11. Orthodontic assessment reveals a Class II division 1 malocclusion with an overjet of 9 mm and marked lip incompetence.

Which occlusal feature most predisposes to this injury?

  1. A. Angle Class III skeletal relationship
  2. B. Bilateral posterior crossbite
  3. C. Anterior open bite under 2 mm
  4. D. Increased overjet exceeding 6 mm
  5. E. Deep bite with complete incisal overlap
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. Increased overjet exceeding 6 mm

Epidemiological studies consistently demonstrate that traumatic dental injuries (TDIs) to permanent teeth peak between 8 and 10 years of age, most commonly affecting the maxillary central incisors. The single strongest predisposing anatomical risk factor for anterior crown fractures is an excessive overjet (>3 mm, and especially >6 mm) combined with incompetent lips. Incompetent upper lips fail to provide the normal protective soft-tissue cushioning against frontal impacts during falls, sports, or road accidents. Children with an overjet exceeding 6 mm have more than double to triple the risk of experiencing traumatised incisors compared to those with normal incisal relationships, highlighting the preventive utility of early interceptive orthodontic correction.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Angle Class III skeletal relationship: Angle Class III malocclusions feature a reverse overjet or anterior crossbite, which tends to shield the maxillary incisors rather than expose them to impact.
  • B. Bilateral posterior crossbite: Posterior crossbites affect transverse dental arch coordination and masticatory function, without altering the physical vulnerability of anterior teeth to blunt frontal trauma.
  • C. Anterior open bite under 2 mm: Mild anterior open bites do not inherently project teeth forward into impact zones unless accompanied by significant maxillary incisor proclination and overjet.
  • E. Deep bite with complete incisal overlap: An increased overbite without excessive horizontal overjet leaves the upper incisors relatively protected behind the lower lip and does not multiply impact risk.

Key point: Identify increased overjet and lip incompetence as primary anatomical predisposing factors for traumatic dental injuries.

Question 8 · Preventive dentistry and fluorides · easy

A father brings his 4-year-old son to an outpatient dental department in Quetta for a check-up. The boy has a complete primary dentition with mild marginal gingivitis but no enamel breakdown or cavitated lesions. His drinking water is sourced from an untreated mountain spring containing 0.2 mg/L fluoride. The father asks what amount and strength of toothpaste should be placed on the toothbrush.

What is the recommended toothpaste guidance for this child?

  1. A. Smear layer of 500 ppm paediatric fluoride toothpaste
  2. B. Smear layer of 1,450 ppm standard fluoride toothpaste
  3. C. Pea-sized amount of non-fluoridated herbal toothpaste
  4. D. Full ribbon layer of 500 ppm fluoride toothpaste
  5. E. Pea-sized amount of 1,000 ppm fluoride toothpaste
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Pea-sized amount of 1,000 ppm fluoride toothpaste

Delivering Better Oral Health and WHO guidelines recommend that children aged 3 through 6 years brush twice daily with a pea-sized amount of toothpaste containing at least 1,000 ppm fluoride (up to 1,450 ppm). By age 3, a child has developed sufficient swallowing control to clear excess paste safely, whereas a smear remains restricted to children under 3 years. Formulations containing only 500 ppm fluoride fail to demonstrate equivalent caries prevention in clinical trials. Parents should actively supervise brushing to ensure the child spits out excess toothpaste and does not rinse with water, maintaining salivary fluoride bioavailability without risking systemic overexposure during permanent tooth development.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Smear layer of 500 ppm paediatric fluoride toothpaste: A smear layer is recommended only for children younger than 3 years, and 500 ppm offers inferior protection compared with standard 1,000 ppm formulations.
  • B. Smear layer of 1,450 ppm standard fluoride toothpaste: A smear layer delivers an insufficient therapeutic volume of paste for a 4-year-old child who has developed adequate swallowing control.
  • C. Pea-sized amount of non-fluoridated herbal toothpaste: Non-fluoridated dentifrices do not promote enamel remineralisation or prevent demineralisation, providing no chemical protection against caries.
  • D. Full ribbon layer of 500 ppm fluoride toothpaste: A full ribbon layer dispenses an excessive quantity of dentifrice, while 500 ppm fluoride provides inadequate remineralisation for caries prevention.

Key point: Identify the recommended fluoride toothpaste concentration and quantity for children aged 3 to 6 years.

Question 9 · Dental public health, epidemiology and indices · easy

A public health dental team plans a community screening programme for oral potentially malignant disorders in rural Khyber Pakhtunkhwa. Before securing governmental funding, the planning committee reviews established principles of disease screening formulated by Wilson and Jungner to verify whether oral premalignancy meets population-based screening criteria.

Which principle is an essential requirement for justifying disease screening?

  1. A. An early detectable stage must exist
  2. B. The natural history of the condition may remain unknown
  3. C. The screening test should be invasive to guarantee accuracy
  4. D. The cost of case-finding should disregard subsequent treatment costs
  5. E. The condition should have a low prevalence and negligible morbidity
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. An early detectable stage must exist

The Wilson and Jungner criteria, adopted by the World Health Organization, define the core principles governing public health screening programmes. A valid screening candidate must constitute an important public health problem with an adequately understood natural history. Crucially, there must be an identifiable latent or early asymptomatic/symptomatic phase during which the condition can be detected before advanced clinical manifestations arise. Furthermore, a safe, valid, reliable, and socially acceptable screening test must be available; there must be an agreed, effective treatment for identified patients; and facilities for definitive diagnostic confirmation and subsequent management must be pre-established and financially sustainable within the overall healthcare budget.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. The natural history of the condition may remain unknown: Wilson and Jungner dictate that the natural history of the condition, including development from latent to declared disease, should be adequately understood.
  • C. The screening test should be invasive to guarantee accuracy: Screening tests are applied to asymptomatic populations and must be safe, non-invasive, quick, simple, and acceptable to the public, unlike invasive biopsies.
  • D. The cost of case-finding should disregard subsequent treatment costs: Screening economics require that the cost of case-finding (including diagnosis and treatment) must be economically balanced in relation to overall medical care expenditure.
  • E. The condition should have a low prevalence and negligible morbidity: Screening is appropriate for conditions that represent important public health problems with high morbidity or mortality, not conditions of negligible health impact.

Key point: State the fundamental principles of disease screening as defined by Wilson and Jungner.

Question 10 · Preventive dentistry and fluorides · easy

A 7-year-old boy is brought to a dental college clinic in Peshawar by his mother for a check-up. Intraoral examination reveals newly erupted maxillary first permanent molars (16 and 26) with deep, retentive occlusal fissures without detectable cavitation. The child has treated proximal carious lesions on primary molars and has an overall high caries risk status.

What is the most appropriate preventive measure for 16 and 26?

  1. A. Preventive resin restorations
  2. B. Review in six months without treatment
  3. C. Resin-based fissure sealants
  4. D. Occlusal amalgam restorations
  5. E. Chlorhexidine mouthwash daily
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Resin-based fissure sealants

Pit and fissure sealants are indicated primarily based on individual caries risk and the morphological vulnerability of the tooth surface. Deep, narrow, or retentive pits and fissures create a protected niche that toothbrush bristles cannot reach, allowing biofilm stagnation and rapid demineralisation. In children with high caries risk—evidenced here by previous caries experience in primary teeth—newly erupted permanent molars with deep anatomical fissures benefit most from sealing within the first few years after eruption. Conversely, shallow, self-cleansing fissures and fully cavitated dentinal lesions do not warrant preventive sealant therapy.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Preventive resin restorations: A preventive resin restoration is for a fissure with a small confirmed carious lesion; these fissures are sound.
  • B. Review in six months without treatment: Deep fissures in a high-risk child are likely to decay soon after eruption; waiting loses the chance to protect them.
  • D. Occlusal amalgam restorations: Sound teeth are not cut for restorations.
  • E. Chlorhexidine mouthwash daily: Chlorhexidine does not protect fissures and is not suitable for long-term use in a 7-year-old.

Key point: Identify the clinical indications for placing resin-based pit and fissure sealants.
