Question 1 · Gastrointestinal drugs · easy

A 4-year-old girl from Swabi is brought by her mother with two years of hard stools passed every four to five days, with pain on defecation and daily leakage of loose stool into her underwear. Her mother has been giving liquid paraffin without improvement. On examination she is thriving, her abdomen is soft, and a faecal mass can be felt in the lower abdomen. There are no red flag features such as vomiting, weight loss or delayed passage of meconium.

Which is the most appropriate first-line treatment?

  1. A. Lactulose syrup at a standard dose for several weeks
  2. B. Senna as a long-term daily laxative without disimpaction
  3. C. Higher doses of daily liquid paraffin
  4. D. Macrogol for disimpaction, then maintenance dosing
  5. E. A phosphate enema every night for two weeks
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. Macrogol for disimpaction, then maintenance dosing

Chronic constipation with overflow soiling in children is caused by faecal impaction: a large hard mass in the rectum, with liquid stool from above leaking around it. First-line treatment is an osmotic laxative, macrogol (polyethylene glycol 3350 or 4000), in an escalating dose regimen to clear the impaction, followed by a maintenance dose for months. Macrogol is not absorbed or metabolised; it holds water in the lumen by hydrogen bonding, softening stool and increasing volume, and it is more effective and better tolerated than lactulose, which is fermented by colonic bacteria to gas and acids that cause bloating. A stimulant laxative (senna, sodium picosulfate or bisacodyl) is added if stools are still not passed after disimpaction, but not before, since it causes colic with a loaded rectum. Liquid paraffin is no longer recommended in children. Phosphate enemas are reserved for failure of oral treatment because of the risk of electrolyte disturbance. Dietary advice and toilet training accompany treatment.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Lactulose syrup at a standard dose for several weeks: Lactulose is fermented by colonic bacteria, causing bloating, and is less effective than macrogol for impaction.
  • B. Senna as a long-term daily laxative without disimpaction: A stimulant causes colicky pain against a loaded rectum and should be added only after the impaction has been cleared.
  • C. Higher doses of daily liquid paraffin: Liquid paraffin is no longer recommended in children because of the risk of lipoid pneumonia and loss of fat-soluble vitamins.
  • E. A phosphate enema every night for two weeks: Enemas are reserved for failure of oral treatment, and repeated phosphate enemas can cause electrolyte disturbance.

Key point: Choose macrogol as first-line therapy for childhood constipation with faecal impaction.

Question 2 · Autacoids, NSAIDs, gout and immunosuppressants · medium

A 38-year-old teacher has generalised anxiety disorder with constant worry, poor sleep and muscle tension for a year. Her father became dependent on diazepam, and she does not want any drug that is habit-forming or makes her drowsy while teaching. She has no depression. Her doctor prescribes a drug that she is told will take about two weeks to help.

What is the mechanism of this drug?

  1. A. GABA-A positive modulation
  2. B. 5-HT3 antagonism
  3. C. H1 antagonism
  4. D. Beta-adrenergic blockade
  5. E. 5-HT1A partial agonism
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. 5-HT1A partial agonism

Buspirone is a partial agonist at 5-HT1A receptors (both presynaptic autoreceptors in the raphe nuclei and postsynaptic receptors in the limbic system). It relieves generalised anxiety over 1-3 weeks. Unlike benzodiazepines, it causes little sedation, no significant impairment of driving or work, no dependence or withdrawal, and does not add to the effects of alcohol. It is not useful for acute panic attacks, because it acts slowly, and it does not prevent benzodiazepine withdrawal, as there is no cross-tolerance. Side effects include dizziness, nausea and headache. SSRIs are also first-line for generalised anxiety disorder.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. GABA-A positive modulation: That is a benzodiazepine.
  • B. 5-HT3 antagonism: 5-HT3 antagonism is the action of ondansetron.
  • C. H1 antagonism: Sedating antihistamines like hydroxyzine.
  • D. Beta-adrenergic blockade: Only helps physical symptoms.

Key point: Buspirone, a 5-HT1A partial agonist, treats generalised anxiety slowly, without sedation or dependence.

Question 3 · Anticancer and immunomodulating drugs · medium

A 14-year-old boy with sickle cell anaemia has had five painful crises and one acute chest syndrome in the past year. He starts hydroxyurea (hydroxycarbamide) once daily, with blood counts every few weeks. A year later his crises are much less frequent, his MCV has risen and his haemoglobin electrophoresis pattern has changed.

Which effect of hydroxyurea explains the improvement?

  1. A. It corrects the beta-globin mutation
  2. B. It raises fetal haemoglobin
  3. C. It increases iron absorption
  4. D. It binds HbS and stops it polymerising
  5. E. It reduces blood viscosity by dilution
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. It raises fetal haemoglobin

Hydroxyurea inhibits ribonucleotide reductase, which converts ribonucleotides to deoxyribonucleotides, so it is an S-phase-specific drug that suppresses the marrow. In sickle cell disease, mild marrow stress shifts red cell production towards cells containing fetal haemoglobin (HbF); HbF does not join HbS polymers, so red cells sickle less. It also lowers neutrophil counts and red cell adhesion, and releases nitric oxide. It reduces painful crises, acute chest syndrome, transfusions and mortality. The rising MCV reflects its effect on DNA synthesis and is a useful sign of adherence. It is also used in polycythaemia vera, essential thrombocythaemia and CML before imatinib. Blood counts are monitored for cytopenias, and it is avoided in pregnancy.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. It corrects the beta-globin mutation: It does not change the gene.
  • C. It increases iron absorption: Hydroxyurea does not increase iron absorption.
  • D. It binds HbS and stops it polymerising: Hydroxyurea does not bind HbS (that is how voxelotor works); its main benefit comes from raising fetal haemoglobin.
  • E. It reduces blood viscosity by dilution: Hydroxyurea does not work by diluting blood; it raises fetal haemoglobin.

Key point: Hydroxyurea inhibits ribonucleotide reductase and raises fetal haemoglobin, reducing sickle cell crises.

Question 4 · Diuretics and renal drugs · medium

A 60-year-old woman started omeprazole for reflux six weeks ago. She now has fever, a fine rash and joint aches. Her creatinine has risen from 0.9 to 2.6 mg/dL. Urine shows white cells and white cell casts but no bacteria, and blood eosinophils are raised. Her blood pressure is normal.

What is the most likely cause, and the first step?

  1. A. Acute tubular necrosis; give fluids
  2. B. Pyelonephritis; give antibiotics
  3. C. Prerenal injury; give saline
  4. D. Glomerulonephritis; start cyclophosphamide
  5. E. Interstitial nephritis; stop the drug
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Interstitial nephritis; stop the drug

Acute interstitial nephritis is an immune reaction in the kidney interstitium to a drug (or infection), causing acute kidney injury with fever, rash, joint pains and eosinophilia in some patients, and white cells and white cell casts in the urine. Common causes: proton pump inhibitors, penicillins and cephalosporins, NSAIDs (which can also cause nephrotic syndrome), sulfonamides, rifampicin, diuretics and allopurinol. The key step is stopping the suspected drug; steroids may speed recovery if kidney function does not improve within days. Kidney biopsy confirms it. Other drug kidney injuries: tubular necrosis (aminoglycosides, amphotericin, contrast, cisplatin), crystals (aciclovir, methotrexate) and haemodynamic (NSAIDs, ACE inhibitors).

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Acute tubular necrosis; give fluids: Not with rash and eosinophils.
  • B. Pyelonephritis; give antibiotics: There are no bacteria, so pyelonephritis is unlikely.
  • C. Prerenal injury; give saline: White cell casts, rash and fever do not fit a prerenal injury.
  • D. Glomerulonephritis; start cyclophosphamide: No blood or protein pattern of nephritis.

Key point: Drug-induced acute interstitial nephritis (PPIs, beta-lactams, NSAIDs) causes AKI with fever, rash, eosinophilia and white cell casts; stop the drug.

Question 5 · Respiratory drugs · medium

A 27-year-old woman from Dir has asthma and uses beclometasone 100 micrograms twice daily and salbutamol, needing salbutamol about five times weekly. She has woken twice a week with wheeze for two months, and her peak flow is 70% of her best. Inhaler technique is good, she has no hay fever and does not smoke. Her doctor changes to a budesonide-formoterol inhaler to be used as one or two puffs twice daily and as needed for symptoms. She asks how formoterol can be used for relief when she was told salmeterol cannot.

Which property allows formoterol to be used as a reliever?

  1. A. Rapid onset as a full beta-2 agonist
  2. B. Antimuscarinic activity in addition to beta-2 agonism
  3. C. A duration of action of 24 hours
  4. D. Direct anti-inflammatory action on the airway mucosa
  5. E. Selective action on beta-1 receptors in the airway
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Rapid onset as a full beta-2 agonist

Beta-2 agonists activate Gs-coupled receptors, raising cyclic AMP and activating protein kinase A, which relaxes airway smooth muscle. Short-acting agents (salbutamol, terbutaline) act within minutes for 4-6 hours. Long-acting agents last about 12 hours: formoterol is a full agonist with rapid onset (1-3 minutes) and salmeterol a partial agonist with slow onset (15-30 minutes) because of its long lipophilic tail binding an exosite. The rapid onset of formoterol allows maintenance-and-reliever therapy (MART) with budesonide-formoterol, which reduces severe exacerbations compared with a fixed dose plus a separate salbutamol reliever, because each symptom relief dose also delivers inhaled steroid. The maximum daily number of inhalations is limited (usually 8-12 depending on strength). A long-acting beta-2 agonist must never be used alone in asthma because of the risk of severe attacks, and adverse effects include tremor, palpitations and hypokalaemia.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Antimuscarinic activity in addition to beta-2 agonism: Formoterol has no antimuscarinic action.
  • C. A duration of action of 24 hours: Formoterol lasts about 12 hours, like salmeterol, so duration does not make it a reliever.
  • D. Direct anti-inflammatory action on the airway mucosa: It has no anti-inflammatory effect; the budesonide provides that.
  • E. Selective action on beta-1 receptors in the airway: Airway receptors are beta-2, and beta-1 action would cause tachycardia without bronchodilation.

Key point: Explain why formoterol but not salmeterol can be used for maintenance and reliever therapy.

Question 6 · Antibacterial drugs · easy

A 19-year-old student has a sore throat, fever and tiredness for a week. Her tonsils are large with white exudate, and she has tender lymph nodes at the back of the neck and a palpable spleen. Her GP prescribes amoxicillin. Three days later she has a widespread itchy red maculopapular rash. A blood film shows atypical lymphocytes, and a monospot test is positive.

What best explains the rash?

  1. A. A true penicillin allergy
  2. B. Scarlet fever from streptococci
  3. C. Stevens-Johnson syndrome
  4. D. Measles
  5. E. Amoxicillin in EBV infection
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Amoxicillin in EBV infection

In infectious mononucleosis (glandular fever, from Epstein-Barr virus), most patients given amoxicillin or ampicillin develop a widespread maculopapular rash a few days later. It is thought to be an immune reaction linked to the virus-driven activation of B and T cells, rather than a lasting IgE-mediated allergy; most patients tolerate penicillins once the infection has passed, so they should not automatically be labelled "penicillin allergic", although specialist testing may be advised. The lesson is to avoid antibiotics in sore throat that is likely viral. Other advice in glandular fever: avoid contact sports for at least 3-4 weeks because of the risk of splenic rupture.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. A true penicillin allergy: Usually not; most can take penicillins later.
  • B. Scarlet fever from streptococci: The tests show glandular fever.
  • C. Stevens-Johnson syndrome: Stevens-Johnson syndrome causes mucosal blistering and skin sloughing, which she does not have.
  • D. Measles: No cough or Koplik spots.

Key point: Amoxicillin in EBV infection commonly causes a rash that does not usually mean lasting penicillin allergy.

Question 7 · Antifungal drugs · medium

A 52-year-old man from Peshawar with poorly controlled type 2 diabetes (HbA1c 12.4%) presents with facial swelling, orbital pain and a black necrotic eschar on the palate. Glucose is 520 mg/dL (70-140), urinary ketones are positive and arterial pH is 7.18 (7.35-7.45). MRI shows sinus and orbital involvement, and biopsy shows broad, ribbon-like aseptate hyphae with right-angle branching. Fluconazole was started elsewhere and he is worsening.

Which antifungal is most appropriate?

  1. A. High-dose liposomal amphotericin B
  2. B. A higher dose of fluconazole
  3. C. Caspofungin
  4. D. Voriconazole
  5. E. Oral terbinafine
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. High-dose liposomal amphotericin B

Mucormycosis is an angioinvasive fungal infection that complicates diabetic ketoacidosis, neutropenia and iron overload, and rose sharply in South Asia with uncontrolled diabetes and steroid use. Rhino-orbital-cerebral disease causes necrotic eschars, tissue infarction and orbital invasion. Mucorales resist fluconazole, voriconazole and echinocandins. Amphotericin B binds ergosterol in fungal membranes, forming pores that leak potassium and other ions, and is fungicidal; lipid formulations allow 5-10 mg/kg daily with less nephrotoxicity. Treatment also needs urgent surgical debridement, correction of acidosis and hyperglycaemia, and reduction of immunosuppression. Posaconazole or isavuconazole are used for step-down or salvage. Monitor creatinine, potassium and magnesium, and give saline before conventional amphotericin.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. A higher dose of fluconazole: Mucorales are intrinsically resistant to fluconazole, so a higher dose would not work.
  • C. Caspofungin: Echinocandins have no useful activity against Mucorales.
  • D. Voriconazole: Is inactive against Mucorales, and exposure may even favour their growth.
  • E. Oral terbinafine: Is used for dermatophyte infection and is not a treatment for invasive mucormycosis.

Key point: Choose liposomal amphotericin B for mucormycosis and recognise azole and echinocandin resistance.

Question 8 · Autonomic drugs · medium

A 28-year-old man in Peshawar who has smoked and injected heroin for five years is admitted voluntarily for detoxification. Thirty hours after his last use he has sweating, a runny nose, yawning, dilated pupils, piloerection and abdominal cramps. His pulse is 104/min and his blood pressure is 150/96 mmHg. He asks for something to settle his restlessness that is not an opioid. The doctor prescribes lofexidine, a drug related to clonidine.

Which mechanism best explains how this drug eases his symptoms?

  1. A. Alpha-2 stimulation, reducing noradrenaline release
  2. B. Blockade of mu opioid receptors in the locus coeruleus
  3. C. Activation of nicotinic receptors in autonomic ganglia
  4. D. Blockade of beta-1 receptors in the heart and brainstem
  5. E. Inhibition of monoamine oxidase to raise noradrenaline levels
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Alpha-2 stimulation, reducing noradrenaline release

Chronic opioid use inhibits noradrenergic neurones of the locus coeruleus through mu receptors, with a compensatory rise in cyclic AMP. When the opioid stops, this inhibition is lost and the neurones fire excessively, causing sweating, tachycardia, hypertension, anxiety, rhinorrhoea, diarrhoea and piloerection. Clonidine and lofexidine are alpha-2 agonists that act on presynaptic and central alpha-2 receptors (Gi coupled) to reduce locus coeruleus firing and sympathetic outflow, controlling the autonomic features but not craving or muscle aches. Adjuncts include a non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug, an antiemetic and loperamide. Adverse effects are hypotension, bradycardia, sedation and dry mouth, and lofexidine causes less hypotension than clonidine but can prolong the QT interval. Doses should be tapered rather than stopped abruptly because of rebound hypertension. Substitution with methadone or buprenorphine is an alternative.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Blockade of mu opioid receptors in the locus coeruleus: Blocking mu receptors would worsen withdrawal; the drug is not an opioid antagonist.
  • C. Activation of nicotinic receptors in autonomic ganglia: This would increase sympathetic activity rather than reduce it.
  • D. Blockade of beta-1 receptors in the heart and brainstem: Beta-blockers do not treat withdrawal and would not be the mechanism of a clonidine-like drug.
  • E. Inhibition of monoamine oxidase to raise noradrenaline levels: This would increase noradrenaline and worsen his restlessness and sweating.

Key point: Explain how alpha-2 agonists relieve the autonomic features of opioid withdrawal.

Question 9 · Antiprotozoal and anthelmintic drugs · medium

A 23-year-old woman has had fever for two months, weight loss and darkening of her skin. She has a massive spleen, pancytopenia and high globulins. The rK39 rapid test is positive, and a bone marrow aspirate shows Leishman-Donovan bodies. She is not pregnant. The physician chooses a single high-dose intravenous infusion.

Which drug is the preferred first-line treatment in South Asia?

  1. A. Liposomal amphotericin B
  2. B. Oral fluconazole for 4 weeks
  3. C. Chloroquine
  4. D. Metronidazole
  5. E. Albendazole
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Liposomal amphotericin B

Visceral leishmaniasis (kala-azar), caused by Leishmania donovani in South Asia, presents with prolonged fever, weight loss, massive splenomegaly, pancytopenia and hypergammaglobulinaemia; skin darkening gives the name "black fever". Amphotericin B binds the ergosterol-like sterols of the parasite membrane and forms pores through which the cell leaks and dies; the liposomal form delivers it to the macrophages where Leishmania lives, with far less kidney toxicity. In the Indian subcontinent, WHO recommends a single dose of liposomal amphotericin B (10 mg/kg), which cures over 95% with little toxicity. Alternatives include combinations of miltefosine (oral, teratogenic, needing contraception during and after treatment) with paromomycin. Pentavalent antimonials (sodium stibogluconate, meglumine antimoniate) are no longer used for visceral disease in South Asia because of resistance and cardiotoxicity, though they remain important for cutaneous leishmaniasis.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Oral fluconazole for 4 weeks: Not effective for leishmaniasis.
  • C. Chloroquine: Chloroquine has no activity against Leishmania.
  • D. Metronidazole: Metronidazole treats amoebae and anaerobes, not Leishmania.
  • E. Albendazole: Albendazole is an anthelmintic and has no effect on Leishmania.

Key point: In South Asia, visceral leishmaniasis is treated with a single dose of liposomal amphotericin B.

Question 10 · Antiviral drugs · easy

During a winter influenza outbreak in a boarding school in Dir, a doctor remembers that amantadine was used years ago and proposes giving it to students as prophylaxis. The regional laboratory reports that circulating influenza A strains (H1N1 and H3N2) carry the S31N substitution in the M2 protein. Several students have had fever for 24 hours, and the school nurse asks whether amantadine can be used. The students are 14 to 17 years old and healthy, and kidney function has not been tested.

Why is amantadine no longer recommended for this outbreak?

  1. A. Circulating influenza A strains carry resistant M2 mutations
  2. B. It is active against influenza B, not A
  3. C. It blocks neuraminidase and so prevents virion release
  4. D. It is destroyed by hepatic metabolism and does not reach the lungs
  5. E. It is contraindicated in adolescents
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Circulating influenza A strains carry resistant M2 mutations

Amantadine and rimantadine block the M2 proton channel of influenza A, preventing acidification of the virion interior and uncoating. They have no activity against influenza B, which lacks M2. A single amino acid change, such as S31N, confers high-level resistance, and since the mid-2000s nearly all circulating influenza A viruses have carried it, so adamantanes are no longer recommended. Neuraminidase inhibitors (oseltamivir, zanamivir) and the endonuclease inhibitor baloxavir are the drugs of choice, started early in high-risk or severe disease. Amantadine is still used for Parkinson disease and drug-induced dyskinesias; it is renally cleared, and adverse effects include insomnia, confusion, hallucinations and anticholinergic effects. Vaccination remains the main protection for schools.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. It is active against influenza B, not A: Amantadine has no activity against influenza B; its problem here is resistance in influenza A, which is the circulating virus.
  • C. It blocks neuraminidase and so prevents virion release: Neuraminidase inhibition is the action of oseltamivir and zanamivir, not amantadine.
  • D. It is destroyed by hepatic metabolism and does not reach the lungs: Amantadine is excreted unchanged by the kidney and reaches the respiratory tract well.
  • E. It is contraindicated in adolescents: There is no blanket contraindication in adolescents; resistance is the reason for not using it.

Key point: Explain why adamantanes are no longer used for influenza A.
