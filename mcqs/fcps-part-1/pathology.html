Question 1 · Systemic pathology: hepatobiliary system and pancreas · medium

A 35-year-old man in Hyderabad, Sindh, had bloody diarrhoea with mucus a month ago that settled without treatment. He now has fever, sweats and right upper quadrant pain worse on breathing, and his liver is tender. White cells are 17 × 10⁹/L. Ultrasound shows a single large cavity in the right lobe, and aspiration yields "anchovy paste" material without bacteria.

What is the cause?

  1. A. Echinococcus granulosus
  2. B. Klebsiella pneumoniae
  3. C. Hepatitis E
  4. D. Entamoeba histolytica
  5. E. Fasciola hepatica
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. Entamoeba histolytica

Liver abscesses: amoebic (Entamoeba histolytica reaches the liver through the portal vein from colitis; single, right lobe, "anchovy paste" pus, young men; serology; metronidazole), pyogenic (biliary infection, portal spread from appendicitis or diverticulitis; often multiple; E. coli, Klebsiella in diabetics, anaerobes; drainage and antibiotics), and hydatid cyst (Echinococcus granulosus from dog faeces in sheep-rearing areas; unilocular cyst with daughter cysts and "hydatid sand"; rupture causes anaphylaxis; treated with albendazole and careful surgery).

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Echinococcus granulosus: Hydatid cysts contain clear fluid and daughter cysts, not anchovy paste.
  • B. Klebsiella pneumoniae: A pyogenic abscess contains pus with bacteria, often in diabetics.
  • C. Hepatitis E: Hepatitis E causes acute hepatitis, not an abscess.
  • E. Fasciola hepatica: Fasciola affects bile ducts, with eosinophilia, not a single cavity.

Key point: Amoebic liver abscess follows colitis: single right-lobe cavity with anchovy-paste pus.

Question 2 · Systemic pathology: musculoskeletal system and skin · medium

A 45-year-old woman has had painful, swollen knuckles and wrists on both sides for six months, with morning stiffness lasting 2 hours, fatigue and painless nodules over her elbows. Her end finger joints are spared. Anti-CCP antibodies are strongly positive, and ESR is 68 mm/h. X-ray shows periarticular erosions and thinning of bone near the joints.

What drives joint destruction?

  1. A. Wear and tear of cartilage
  2. B. Crystal deposition
  3. C. Bacterial infection
  4. D. Synovial pannus
  5. E. Avascular necrosis
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. Synovial pannus

Rheumatoid arthritis: autoimmune (HLA-DR4 shared epitope, smoking, citrullinated peptide antibodies) symmetrical polyarthritis of small joints of hands (MCP, PIP; spares DIP) and feet, wrists. Synovium proliferates into pannus, with lymphocytes, plasma cells and TNF, IL-6 and RANKL-driven osteoclasts eroding cartilage and bone; ulnar deviation, swan-neck and boutonnière deformities, atlantoaxial subluxation. Extra-articular: rheumatoid nodules (central fibrinoid necrosis with palisading histiocytes), interstitial lung disease, Caplan syndrome, pericarditis, scleritis, vasculitis, Felty syndrome (splenomegaly, neutropenia), amyloidosis, anaemia of chronic disease, Sjögren. Treatment: methotrexate, biologics (anti-TNF: screen for TB).

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Wear and tear of cartilage: Wear and tear of cartilage is osteoarthritis, without 2-hour stiffness or anti-CCP.
  • B. Crystal deposition: Crystal deposition causes gout or pseudogout attacks.
  • C. Bacterial infection: Bacterial infection causes septic arthritis of one joint.
  • E. Avascular necrosis: Avascular necrosis affects the femoral head, not the knuckles.

Key point: Rheumatoid arthritis: symmetrical small-joint synovitis with pannus, anti-CCP antibodies and erosions.

Question 3 · Neoplasia · medium

A public health lecture in Lahore discusses cancer patterns in Pakistan, noting that the commonest cancer overall is diagnosed largely in women, often at a late stage.

Which cancer is it?

  1. A. Lung cancer
  2. B. Prostate cancer
  3. C. Colorectal cancer
  4. D. Breast cancer
  5. E. Cervical cancer
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. Breast cancer

In Pakistan, breast cancer is the commonest cancer overall and in women, often presenting late and at younger ages. Oral cavity and lip cancer is the leading cancer in men in many reports (tobacco, betel quid, naswar), followed by lung cancer. Liver cancer (hepatitis B and C) and oesophageal cancer are also prominent. Globally, lung cancer causes the most cancer deaths, with breast, colorectal and prostate cancers among the most common. Incidence refers to new cases, mortality to deaths; both are shaped by risk factors, screening, and access to care.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Lung cancer: Lung cancer is commoner in men and less common overall in Pakistan.
  • B. Prostate cancer: Prostate cancer is less common in Pakistan than in Western countries.
  • C. Colorectal cancer: Colorectal cancer is less common than breast cancer in Pakistan.
  • E. Cervical cancer: Cervical cancer is common, but breast cancer is more common.

Key point: In Pakistan, breast cancer is the commonest overall; oral cancer leads in men, linked to tobacco and betel quid.

Question 4 · Systemic pathology: renal and urinary tract · medium

A 55-year-old man has had swollen legs and frothy urine for three months. He has nephrotic syndrome, with albumin 2 g/dL and 9 g/day of protein. Screening finds no cancer, infection, lupus or new medicines; his chest X-ray, colonoscopy, hepatitis serology and ANA are normal. A blood test is positive for an autoantibody against a receptor on podocytes. A biopsy is planned but may not be needed.

Which antibody is this?

  1. A. Anti-GBM antibody (IgG)
  2. B. Anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibody
  3. C. Anti-PLA2R antibody
  4. D. Anti-streptolysin O
  5. E. Anti-double-stranded DNA
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Anti-PLA2R antibody

Primary membranous nephropathy, a common cause of nephrotic syndrome in adults, is an autoimmune disease: IgG4 antibodies against PLA2R (or less often THSD7A) on podocytes form immune complexes in place on the outer (subepithelial) side of the basement membrane, activating complement and damaging podocytes. Anti-PLA2R levels support the diagnosis and track response to treatment (rituximab). A third improve spontaneously.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Anti-GBM antibody (IgG): Anti-GBM antibodies cause crescentic GN with linear deposits and lung bleeding.
  • B. Anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibody: ANCA causes pauci-immune vasculitis with nephritis, not isolated nephrotic syndrome.
  • D. Anti-streptolysin O: Anti-streptolysin O marks recent streptococcal infection.
  • E. Anti-double-stranded DNA: Anti-dsDNA marks lupus, which screening excluded.

Key point: Primary membranous nephropathy: anti-PLA2R antibodies against podocytes.

Question 5 · Systemic pathology: breast and reproductive system · medium

A 65-year-old woman has had severe vulval itching for years, worse at night, and painful intercourse. The vulval skin is thin, white and wrinkled like parchment, with loss of the labia minora and a narrowed opening. There is a small area of thickening. Biopsy shows thinned epidermis, loss of rete ridges and a band of hyalinised dermis.

What is the condition, and what is its cancer risk?

  1. A. Vulval candidiasis; no risk
  2. B. Condyloma acuminatum; HPV-related
  3. C. Bartholin cyst; no risk
  4. D. Lichen sclerosus; small risk
  5. E. Extramammary Paget disease; invasive
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. Lichen sclerosus; small risk

Vulval lesions: lichen sclerosus (postmenopausal women, white parchment-like skin, itching, scarring; small risk of HPV-independent squamous cell carcinoma), lichen simplex chronicus (thick skin from scratching), condyloma acuminatum (HPV 6 and 11, koilocytes), vulval intraepithelial neoplasia and squamous carcinoma (HPV-related in younger women), Bartholin gland cyst and abscess, extramammary Paget disease (eczema-like red patch, intraepidermal adenocarcinoma cells). Vaginal: clear cell adenocarcinoma after diethylstilbestrol exposure in utero; sarcoma botryoides (embryonal rhabdomyosarcoma, grape-like mass in young girls).

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Vulval candidiasis; no risk: Candida causes itching with a curdy discharge, not white, thinned parchment-like skin with loss of labia.
  • B. Condyloma acuminatum; HPV-related: Condylomata are raised HPV warts, not flat white atrophic skin.
  • C. Bartholin cyst; no risk: A Bartholin cyst is a swelling at the vaginal opening, not diffuse white atrophy.
  • E. Extramammary Paget disease; invasive: Extramammary Paget disease is a red eczema-like patch, not white thin skin, and is often in situ.

Key point: Lichen sclerosus causes white, thin vulval skin with itching and a small risk of HPV-independent squamous carcinoma.

Question 6 · Systemic pathology: respiratory system · medium

A 60-year-old retired coal miner who never smoked has had increasing breathlessness for years. He also has rheumatoid arthritis with swollen knuckles and a positive rheumatoid factor. X-ray shows small rounded nodules in the upper zones and a large conglomerate mass, with multiple larger lung nodules of 1 to 3 cm scattered in the periphery.

What combination is this?

  1. A. Goodpasture syndrome
  2. B. Caplan syndrome
  3. C. Kartagener syndrome
  4. D. Lofgren syndrome
  5. E. Loeffler syndrome
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Caplan syndrome

Coal workers' pneumoconiosis ranges from harmless anthracosis to simple pneumoconiosis (coal macules and nodules, upper lobes) and complicated pneumoconiosis (progressive massive fibrosis, large black scars). Caplan syndrome is rheumatoid arthritis with pneumoconiosis (coal, silica or asbestos), producing large rheumatoid nodules in the lungs. Coal dust does not clearly raise lung cancer or TB risk, unlike silica.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Goodpasture syndrome: Goodpasture syndrome is lung haemorrhage with glomerulonephritis from anti-GBM antibodies.
  • C. Kartagener syndrome: Kartagener syndrome is ciliary dyskinesia with bronchiectasis and situs inversus.
  • D. Lofgren syndrome: Lofgren syndrome is acute sarcoidosis with erythema nodosum, hilar nodes and ankle arthritis.
  • E. Loeffler syndrome: Loeffler syndrome is a transient eosinophilic pneumonia, often from migrating worm larvae.

Key point: Caplan syndrome is rheumatoid arthritis with pneumoconiosis, giving rheumatoid lung nodules.

Question 7 · Cell injury, adaptation and death · medium

A 35-year-old man has yellow plaques on his eyelids and firm nodules in his Achilles tendons. His father died of a heart attack at 42, and his sister has the same tendon lumps. He does not smoke. His LDL is 329 mg/dL, with normal triglycerides. A grey-white ring is visible around his corneas. Biopsy of a tendon nodule shows clusters of macrophages with foamy cytoplasm.

What are the foamy macrophages full of?

  1. A. Cholesterol and its esters
  2. B. Triglyceride from dietary fat
  3. C. Glycogen
  4. D. Haemosiderin
  5. E. Amyloid
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Cholesterol and its esters

Cholesterol and its esters accumulate in macrophages as foam cells in several settings: atherosclerosis (in the intima, forming fatty streaks and plaques), xanthomas (clusters of foamy macrophages in skin and tendons in hyperlipidaemia, such as familial hypercholesterolaemia), cholesterolosis of the gallbladder mucosa ("strawberry gallbladder") and Niemann-Pick disease type C. Cholesterol clefts (needle-shaped spaces where crystals dissolved during processing) are seen in plaques and old haemorrhages. Xanthelasma can also occur with normal lipids, but tendon xanthomas strongly suggest familial hypercholesterolaemia.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Triglyceride from dietary fat: Triglyceride gives clear fat vacuoles; xanthomas in familial hypercholesterolaemia are full of cholesterol.
  • C. Glycogen: Glycogen gives clear, PAS-positive cells, and is not linked to tendon nodules or high LDL.
  • D. Haemosiderin: Haemosiderin is a golden-brown iron pigment from bleeding or iron overload.
  • E. Amyloid: Amyloid is extracellular protein, not material inside macrophages.

Key point: Foam cells are macrophages full of cholesterol; xanthomas in tendons suggest familial hypercholesterolaemia.

Question 8 · Systemic pathology: nervous system and special senses · medium

A 40-year-old man slips on a wet floor, falls backwards and strikes the back of his head on concrete. He becomes confused and dies two days later. At autopsy there is only a small bruise and fracture at the back of the skull, but there are haemorrhagic contusions on the undersurfaces of the frontal lobes and the tips of the temporal lobes, with only minor injury at the back of the brain.

What explains this pattern?

  1. A. A coup injury at the site of the impact
  2. B. Diffuse axonal injury
  3. C. Extradural haematoma
  4. D. Hypertensive haemorrhage
  5. E. Contrecoup injury against the skull base
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Contrecoup injury against the skull base

Contusions are bruises of the brain surface, worst at the crests of gyri. A coup injury is at the point of impact; a contrecoup injury is on the opposite side, as the moving brain strikes the inside of the skull. The orbital frontal lobes and temporal poles are most often contused because they lie against the rough, ridged anterior and middle cranial fossae. Old contusions leave golden-brown, depressed plaques jaunes.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. A coup injury at the site of the impact: The coup injury at the back of the head is minor; his main damage is opposite the impact.
  • B. Diffuse axonal injury: Diffuse axonal injury affects deep white matter, not the undersurfaces of the frontal and temporal lobes.
  • C. Extradural haematoma: An extradural haematoma is a lens-shaped clot outside the dura, which was not found.
  • D. Hypertensive haemorrhage: Hypertensive bleeds are deep in the basal ganglia, not on the frontal and temporal surfaces after a fall.

Key point: Contrecoup contusions affect frontal undersurfaces and temporal poles after falls on the back of the head.

Question 9 · Systemic pathology: cardiovascular system · medium

A 22-year-old student had a cold with sore throat and diarrhoea two weeks ago. He now has fever, sharp chest pain, breathlessness on climbing stairs and palpitations. Heart rate is 118/min with occasional ectopic beats. Troponin I is 3.2 ng/mL, and echo shows mildly reduced left ventricular function. His coronary arteries are normal on angiography, and biopsy shows lymphocytes infiltrating the myocardium with myocyte necrosis.

Which cause is most likely?

  1. A. Rheumatic fever
  2. B. Trypanosoma cruzi
  3. C. Diphtheria toxin
  4. D. Coxsackie B virus
  5. E. Giant cell myocarditis
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. Coxsackie B virus

Myocarditis is inflammation of the heart muscle with myocyte injury. Causes: viruses (Coxsackie A and B and other enteroviruses commonest; also adenovirus, parvovirus B19, HHV-6, SARS-CoV-2), bacteria (diphtheria toxin, Lyme disease with heart block), parasites (Chagas disease with dilated cardiomyopathy and megacolon; toxoplasmosis), immune causes (SLE, rheumatic fever, sarcoid, giant cell myocarditis, checkpoint inhibitors, hypersensitivity to drugs with eosinophils), and radiation. Lymphocytic infiltrate with necrosis on biopsy (Dallas criteria). Most recover; some develop dilated cardiomyopathy or arrhythmia and sudden death.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Rheumatic fever: Rheumatic carditis follows a streptococcal throat infection with arthritis and Aschoff bodies, not lymphocytic necrosis after diarrhoea.
  • B. Trypanosoma cruzi: Chagas disease occurs in South America after a bug bite and causes chronic heart failure years later.
  • C. Diphtheria toxin: Diphtheria myocarditis follows a grey membrane in the throat in unvaccinated people.
  • E. Giant cell myocarditis: Giant cell myocarditis is rare, rapidly fatal and shows multinucleated giant cells, which are absent.

Key point: Viral (often Coxsackie B) myocarditis gives lymphocytic infiltrates with myocyte necrosis and may cause dilated cardiomyopathy.

Question 10 · Systemic pathology: endocrine system · medium

A 32-year-old woman has a painless thyroid nodule found on ultrasound. Fine-needle aspiration of the nodule shows cells in papillary groups with enlarged, overlapping, pale "empty" nuclei, nuclear grooves and nuclear pseudoinclusions, and laminated calcified bodies. Her thyroid tests are normal.

Which features make the diagnosis?

  1. A. The papillary architecture
  2. B. Invasion of the tumour capsule
  3. C. Amyloid deposits in the stroma
  4. D. The nuclear features
  5. E. Giant and spindle cells
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. The nuclear features

Papillary thyroid carcinoma, the commonest thyroid cancer, is diagnosed by nuclear features: optically clear "Orphan Annie eye" nuclei, nuclear grooves, intranuclear pseudoinclusions and overlapping; psammoma bodies are frequent. The follicular variant has these nuclei without papillae. Mutations: BRAF V600E, RET/PTC rearrangements (especially after childhood radiation). It spreads to cervical lymph nodes but has an excellent prognosis.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. The papillary architecture: Papillae can occur in hyperplastic Graves glands, so architecture alone is not enough.
  • B. Invasion of the tumour capsule: Capsular invasion defines follicular carcinoma, not papillary.
  • C. Amyloid deposits in the stroma: Amyloid in the stroma suggests medullary carcinoma.
  • E. Giant and spindle cells: Giant and spindle cells suggest anaplastic carcinoma.

Key point: Papillary carcinoma is diagnosed by its nuclei: clear, grooved, with pseudoinclusions; psammoma bodies.
