Question 1 · Caries, pulp and periapical disease, and oral infections · easy

A 30-year-old woman in Faisalabad has had pain in her lower left first molar (36) for three days. Cold water causes severe pain that lasts more than a minute after the cold is removed, and the pain wakes her at night. The tooth has a deep carious lesion. It is not tender to percussion, and a radiograph shows no periapical change.

What is the diagnosis?

  1. A. Reversible pulpitis
  2. B. Irreversible pulpitis
  3. C. Pulp necrosis
  4. D. Acute apical abscess
  5. E. Dentine hypersensitivity of 36
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Irreversible pulpitis

Pulpitis is classified clinically according to whether the pulp can recover. In reversible pulpitis, a stimulus such as cold causes short, sharp pain that stops within seconds of removing it; removing the caries and placing a restoration allows recovery. In symptomatic irreversible pulpitis, pain lingers (often over 30 seconds) after the stimulus, may be spontaneous, and can be worse lying down at night, as increased pressure in a pulp enclosed by hard walls compromises blood flow. The inflamed pulp cannot heal, so treatment is pulpotomy, pulpectomy (root canal treatment) or extraction; in mature teeth, full pulpotomy is increasingly accepted. Untreated, the pulp becomes necrotic and infection spreads to the periapical tissues.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Reversible pulpitis: Reversible pulpitis causes short, sharp pain to cold that stops as soon as the stimulus is removed.
  • C. Pulp necrosis: A necrotic pulp does not respond to cold.
  • D. Acute apical abscess: An abscess causes tenderness to biting and percussion, and often swelling.
  • E. Dentine hypersensitivity of 36: Hypersensitivity causes brief, sharp pain from exposed dentine, not lingering night pain with deep caries.

Key point: Distinguish symptomatic irreversible pulpitis from reversible pulpitis by the duration and nature of pain.

Question 2 · Immunological, skin, blood and metabolic disease; injuries and forensic odontology · easy

A 30-year-old woman in Sialkot has a sore, burning tongue for two months. The tongue is smooth, red and shiny, with loss of the filiform papillae, and she has cracked mouth corners. She is pale, tired and breathless on stairs, and has heavy periods. Haemoglobin is 8.6 g/dL (12-15) with a low mean cell volume.

Which deficiency is the most likely cause?

  1. A. Vitamin C
  2. B. Iron
  3. C. Vitamin D
  4. D. Vitamin K
  5. E. Zinc
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Iron

Haematinic deficiencies (iron, vitamin B12, folate) cause oral signs because oral epithelium has a high turnover. Features include atrophic glossitis (a smooth, red, sore tongue from loss of papillae), burning mouth, angular cheilitis, recurrent aphthous-like ulcers and candidosis. Iron deficiency gives a microcytic anaemia, commonly from menstrual blood loss, poor diet or gastrointestinal bleeding; B12 and folate deficiency give macrocytic anaemia, and B12 deficiency can cause neurological symptoms. Oral signs may precede anaemia, so a full blood count, ferritin, B12 and folate should be checked in patients with these signs. Treatment is replacement and investigation of the cause.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Vitamin C: Vitamin C deficiency causes bleeding, swollen gums and poor healing, not microcytic anaemia with glossitis.
  • C. Vitamin D: Vitamin D deficiency affects bone, not the tongue papillae.
  • D. Vitamin K: Vitamin K deficiency causes bleeding, not glossitis and microcytic anaemia.
  • E. Zinc: Zinc deficiency causes taste changes and skin lesions, but not microcytic anaemia.

Key point: Recognise the oral signs of iron deficiency anaemia.

Question 3 · Developmental disturbances of teeth, jaws and oral tissues · medium

A 5-year-old girl is examined at a private dental clinic in Mardan because her mother noticed a very wide lower front tooth. Intraoral examination shows a large, notched crown in the lower right lateral incisor (82) and canine (83) region with a vertical labiolingual groove. Counting the anomalous double tooth as a single unit, there are only 9 teeth present in the mandibular arch. A periapical radiograph shows two separate pulp chambers and two root canals joining near the apex within the wide tooth.

How is this developmental dental anomaly classified?

  1. A. Concrescence
  2. B. Dilaceration
  3. C. Fusion
  4. D. Gemination
  5. E. Macrodontia
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Fusion

Double teeth arise from either fusion or gemination and are told apart clinically by counting the teeth in the arch. Fusion is the union of two normally separated adjacent tooth germs, most often in the primary anterior mandibular region. By definition, fusion involves confluence of dentine; depending on the stage of odontogenesis, the teeth may share one pulp chamber or have two separate pulp chambers and canals. When the anomalous tooth is counted as one unit, fusion results in one fewer tooth than normal in the arch (9 instead of 10 primary mandibular teeth), unless fusion occurred with a supernumerary tooth. Gemination is the attempted division of a single tooth germ, producing a bifid crown on a single root canal system while keeping the arch tooth count normal.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Concrescence: Concrescence is the union of two completely formed roots by cellular cementum alone without confluence of the crowns or dentine.
  • B. Dilaceration: Dilaceration is an acute angulation between the crown and root caused by trauma during development, not a double-width joined crown.
  • D. Gemination: Gemination is the incomplete division of a single tooth bud, which leaves the total tooth count in the dental arch normal when counted as one.
  • E. Macrodontia: True macrodontia produces an enlarged single tooth of normal anatomical contour rather than a grooved bifid crown with two pulp chambers.

Key point: Distinguish fusion from gemination using the arch tooth count rule and the requirement for dentinal union.

Question 4 · Bone, soft-tissue and salivary gland pathology · easy

A 22-year-old woman in Islamabad has a recurrent, painless, bluish swelling of her lower lip that appeared after she bit it. Excision shows a pool of mucus in the connective tissue, surrounded by granulation tissue and foamy macrophages, with no epithelial lining, and an adjacent minor salivary gland with a damaged duct.

How does this lesion form?

  1. A. A cut duct leaks mucus into the tissue
  2. B. A duct is blocked by a stone and dilates to form a lined cyst
  3. C. Embryonic epithelium becomes trapped in the lip
  4. D. A virus infects the salivary gland and causes swelling
  5. E. A benign salivary tumour grows in the lip
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. A cut duct leaks mucus into the tissue

Mucoceles are common lesions, most often on the lower lip, followed by the floor of mouth, ventral tongue and buccal mucosa. Most are mucus extravasation phenomena: trauma such as lip biting severs the excretory duct of a minor salivary gland, and mucus spills into the surrounding connective tissue, where it is walled off by granulation tissue. Because the wall is granulation tissue, not epithelium, they are pseudocysts. Histology shows pooled mucus with foamy macrophages (muciphages) and inflammation. Less commonly, mucus retention cysts form from partial duct obstruction and have an epithelial lining. Treatment is excision of the mucocele with the associated minor glands to prevent recurrence.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. A duct is blocked by a stone and dilates to form a lined cyst: Retention cysts from obstruction have an epithelial lining and are less common, mostly in older adults.
  • C. Embryonic epithelium becomes trapped in the lip: That describes developmental cysts, which have an epithelial lining.
  • D. A virus infects the salivary gland and causes swelling: Viral sialadenitis such as mumps affects the major glands, not a localised lip swelling.
  • E. A benign salivary tumour grows in the lip: Salivary tumours of the lip are usually in the upper lip and are solid, not mucus pools.

Key point: Explain the extravasation mechanism of mucoceles and why they lack an epithelial lining.

Question 5 · Cysts of the jaws and odontogenic tumours · easy

A 16-year-old girl attends a dental hospital in Karachi because her upper right permanent canine (13) has not erupted, and the primary canine (53) is still present. A radiograph shows the crown of 13 high in the maxilla, enclosed by a well-defined, unilocular radiolucency, 2 cm across, attached to the tooth at the cementoenamel junction. The root of the primary canine shows little resorption.

What is the most likely diagnosis?

  1. A. Adenomatoid odontogenic tumour
  2. B. Odontogenic keratocyst
  3. C. Dentigerous cyst
  4. D. Radicular cyst
  5. E. Eruption cyst
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Dentigerous cyst

The dentigerous cyst is the commonest developmental odontogenic cyst. It forms by accumulation of fluid between the reduced enamel epithelium and the crown of an unerupted tooth, and is attached at the cementoenamel junction. It most often involves the mandibular third molars, followed by maxillary canines and mandibular premolars. Radiographically it is a well-defined, unilocular radiolucency surrounding the crown. A follicular space wider than about 3-5 mm suggests a cyst. Treatment is enucleation with removal of the tooth, or marsupialisation to allow eruption of the tooth in young patients, with histological examination to exclude a keratocyst or ameloblastoma.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Adenomatoid odontogenic tumour: AOT can surround an unerupted canine, but it often extends past the cementoenamel junction and contains calcifications; the simplest diagnosis here is a dentigerous cyst.
  • B. Odontogenic keratocyst: A keratocyst can envelop a crown, but it usually occurs in the posterior mandible and grows along the bone.
  • D. Radicular cyst: A radicular cyst forms at the apex of a non-vital erupted tooth, not around an unerupted crown.
  • E. Eruption cyst: An eruption cyst is a soft tissue swelling over an erupting tooth in the gum, not a radiolucency deep in the maxilla.

Key point: Diagnose a dentigerous cyst from a pericoronal radiolucency attached at the cementoenamel junction of an unerupted tooth.

Question 6 · Epithelial pathology, potentially malignant disorders and oral cancer · easy

A 50-year-old man who smokes 20 cigarettes a day attends a dental hospital in Quetta with a white patch on his left buccal mucosa. It cannot be wiped off. He has no sharp teeth or denture, no amalgam fillings nearby, no skin lesions, and a smear for candida is negative. The patch persisted three weeks after he was advised on smoking and was reviewed. The registrar tells the student that the clinical label depends on what has been excluded.

Which statement best defines leukoplakia?

  1. A. Any white patch in the mouth of a tobacco user
  2. B. A white plaque showing epithelial dysplasia on biopsy specimens
  3. C. A white plaque of questionable risk, other causes excluded
  4. D. A white patch that resolves when the cause is removed
  5. E. A white patch caused by Candida albicans
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. A white plaque of questionable risk, other causes excluded

The WHO defines leukoplakia as a predominantly white plaque of questionable risk, having excluded other known diseases or disorders that carry no increased risk for cancer. It is therefore a clinical diagnosis of exclusion: frictional keratosis, candidosis, lichen planus, white sponge naevus, leukoedema and others must be ruled out first. It does not imply any particular histology; biopsy may show hyperkeratosis alone, dysplasia of varying degree, or even carcinoma. Tobacco is the main risk factor in many populations, but leukoplakia in non-smokers carries a higher risk of malignant change. All persistent leukoplakias should be biopsied, with the most suspicious area sampled.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Any white patch in the mouth of a tobacco user: Tobacco use is a risk factor, but leukoplakia is defined by excluding other known lesions, not by tobacco use.
  • B. A white plaque showing epithelial dysplasia on biopsy specimens: Many leukoplakias show no dysplasia; dysplasia is a histological finding, not the clinical definition.
  • D. A white patch that resolves when the cause is removed: A patch that resolves after removing an irritant is frictional keratosis, not leukoplakia.
  • E. A white patch caused by Candida albicans: A white patch due to candida is candidosis, which is excluded before calling a lesion leukoplakia.

Key point: Apply the WHO definition of leukoplakia as a clinical diagnosis of exclusion.

Question 7 · Caries, pulp and periapical disease, and oral infections · easy

A 40-year-old woman in Mardan has a small, painless 'pimple' on the gum above her upper left lateral incisor (22), which occasionally discharges a salty fluid and then settles. The tooth is discoloured, does not respond to cold and is not tender. A gutta-percha point inserted into the opening on the gum tracks to the apex of 22, where there is a periapical radiolucency.

What is the diagnosis?

  1. A. Periodontal abscess
  2. B. Chronic apical abscess
  3. C. Gingival cyst of the adult
  4. D. Pyogenic granuloma
  5. E. Mucocele
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Chronic apical abscess

A chronic apical abscess is a long-standing inflammatory lesion at the apex of a non-vital tooth that drains through a sinus tract, usually opening on the buccal gingiva as a small papule (parulis or 'gum boil'). Because pus drains, the tooth is usually painless and not very tender. Tracing the sinus with a gutta-percha point on a radiograph identifies the source tooth, which is important as sinuses can open some distance from the affected tooth. Treatment is root canal treatment or extraction, after which the sinus heals; antibiotics are not indicated. Sinuses rarely open on the skin of the face or neck, where they can be mistaken for skin lesions.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Periodontal abscess: A periodontal abscess comes from a pocket beside a vital tooth, not from the apex of a non-vital tooth.
  • C. Gingival cyst of the adult: A gingival cyst does not discharge pus and is not linked to a non-vital tooth.
  • D. Pyogenic granuloma: A pyogenic granuloma is a red lump that bleeds, not a sinus tracing to an apex.
  • E. Mucocele: A mucocele is a mucus swelling of a minor gland, not a sinus from a tooth apex.

Key point: Diagnose a chronic apical abscess with sinus tract and trace its source with a gutta-percha point.

Question 8 · Immunological, skin, blood and metabolic disease; injuries and forensic odontology · easy

A 45-year-old man in Lahore has a painful ulcer, 8 mm across, on the right lateral border of his tongue for two weeks. It lies opposite a sharp, fractured lower right first molar (46). The ulcer has a yellow base and a raised white keratotic margin, but it is not indurated. He does not smoke or chew tobacco. The dentist smooths the sharp edge.

What should happen if the ulcer has not healed two weeks after the sharp edge is removed?

  1. A. Apply a stronger topical steroid and wait another two months
  2. B. Prescribe antibiotics
  3. C. Reassure him, as traumatic ulcers can last for years
  4. D. Biopsy the ulcer
  5. E. Extract all lower teeth
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. Biopsy the ulcer

Traumatic ulcers are common and result from sharp teeth, restorations, dentures, biting or brushing. They are usually painful, with a yellow-grey fibrinous base and a red or keratotic margin, and they lie next to the source of trauma. The key management principle is to remove the cause and review within about two weeks; most heal in that time. Any oral ulcer that persists beyond 2-3 weeks after removing the cause, or that has features such as induration, rolled margins or fixation, must be biopsied, because squamous cell carcinoma can look like a traumatic ulcer, especially on the lateral tongue. Topical anaesthetics or chlorhexidine can relieve symptoms while healing.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Apply a stronger topical steroid and wait another two months: Delaying beyond two weeks after removing the cause risks missing a carcinoma.
  • B. Prescribe antibiotics: Antibiotics do not treat a traumatic ulcer or a carcinoma.
  • C. Reassure him, as traumatic ulcers can last for years: Traumatic ulcers usually heal within about two weeks once the cause is removed.
  • E. Extract all lower teeth: Extraction is not indicated.

Key point: Apply the two-week rule to traumatic ulcers and biopsy any that persist after removing the cause.

Question 9 · Developmental disturbances of teeth, jaws and oral tissues · medium

A 19-year-old man is referred to a dental teaching hospital in Quetta for root canal treatment of his lower left first molar (36). A dental panoramic tomogram shows that all permanent first and second molars have rectangular, vertically elongated pulp chambers, absent cervical constriction at the cementoenamel junction, and furcations displaced close to the root apices with very short root trunks.

With which condition is this dental anomaly most classically associated?

  1. A. Cleidocranial dysplasia
  2. B. Congenital syphilis
  3. C. Gardner syndrome
  4. D. Klinefelter syndrome
  5. E. Pierre Robin sequence
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. Klinefelter syndrome

Taurodontism ("bull-like teeth") is a morphologic variation of multirooted teeth characterised by an enlarged, vertically elongated pulp chamber, apical displacement of the pulpal floor and furcation, and lack of the normal constriction at the cementoenamel junction. It arises from failure of Hertwig epithelial root sheath to invaginate at the proper horizontal level during root development. It is subclassified into hypotaurodontism, mesotaurodontism, and hypertaurodontism according to the degree of apical displacement. While often an isolated trait, taurodontism occurs with high frequency in syndromes involving ectodermal defects or extra X chromosomes, including Klinefelter syndrome (47,XXY), tricho-dento-osseous syndrome, amelogenesis imperfecta (hypomaturation-hypoplastic type with taurodontism), and Down syndrome.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Cleidocranial dysplasia: Cleidocranial dysplasia features multiple supernumerary teeth, absent clavicles, and failed eruption rather than rectangular molar pulp chambers.
  • B. Congenital syphilis: Congenital syphilis produces mulberry first molars with constricted occlusal tables and notched incisors, not apically displaced molar furcations.
  • C. Gardner syndrome: Gardner syndrome presents with jaw osteomas, odontomas, and impacted teeth, without enlargement of the molar pulp chambers.
  • E. Pierre Robin sequence: Pierre Robin sequence involves micrognathia, glossoptosis, and a U-shaped cleft palate, not abnormal molar pulp chamber anatomy.

Key point: Identify the radiographic features of taurodontism and its association with Klinefelter syndrome and failure of Hertwig epithelial root sheath invagination.

Question 10 · Bone, soft-tissue and salivary gland pathology · easy

A 7-year-old unvaccinated boy in Quetta has fever, headache and painful swelling of both parotid glands for three days, making his earlobes stick out. The opening of Stensen's duct is red, but no pus comes from it. Two classmates have similar swelling. He has mild abdominal pain.

What is the most likely cause?

  1. A. Staphylococcus aureus
  2. B. Salivary stones
  3. C. Sjögren syndrome
  4. D. Paramyxovirus (mumps)
  5. E. HIV infection
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. Paramyxovirus (mumps)

Mumps is caused by a paramyxovirus spread by respiratory droplets. After an incubation of 2-3 weeks, it causes fever, malaise and painful swelling of the parotid glands, usually bilateral, lifting the earlobes; the submandibular glands may also be involved. The duct opening may be red, but there is no pus. Complications include orchitis (in post-pubertal males), oophoritis, pancreatitis (abdominal pain), meningitis and sensorineural deafness. Serum amylase is raised. Treatment is supportive. The MMR vaccine prevents it, and unvaccinated communities see outbreaks. Acute bacterial parotitis is usually unilateral with pus from the duct.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Staphylococcus aureus: Bacterial parotitis is usually one-sided, with pus from the duct, often in dehydrated or elderly patients.
  • B. Salivary stones: Stones cause mealtime swelling of one gland, not fever and bilateral swelling in classmates.
  • C. Sjögren syndrome: Sjögren syndrome causes chronic swelling in adults, mostly women, with dry eyes and mouth.
  • E. HIV infection: HIV can cause cystic parotid enlargement, but not acute febrile swelling spread among classmates.

Key point: Recognise mumps parotitis and its complications.
