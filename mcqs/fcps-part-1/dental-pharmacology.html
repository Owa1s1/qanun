Question 1 · Local anaesthetics, pain control, sedation and emergency drugs · easy

A 28-year-old woman attends an oral surgery clinic in Karachi for extraction of a retained root tip. Her medical records document a severe generalised urticarial reaction following application of a topical benzocaine gel at a private practice two years ago. The oral surgeon reviews the pharmacology of amino-ester and amino-amide anaesthetics before selecting a safe local anaesthetic agent.

Which ester metabolite causes true allergy?

  1. A. Ortho-toluidine
  2. B. Monoethylglycinexylidide
  3. C. Diethylaminoethanol
  4. D. Para-aminobenzoic acid
  5. E. Pipecoloxylidide
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. Para-aminobenzoic acid

Ester local anaesthetics (such as procaine, benzocaine, and tetracaine) are rapidly broken down in plasma and tissues by butyrylcholinesterase (pseudocholinesterase). A major metabolic product of this ester hydrolysis is para-aminobenzoic acid (PABA). PABA acts as a hapten, binding endogenous proteins to stimulate IgE-mediated (type I) and cell-mediated (type IV) allergic hypersensitivity reactions. In contrast, amino-amide local anaesthetics (such as lidocaine, articaine, and mepivacaine) are degraded primarily by hepatic cytochrome P450 enzymes without forming PABA, making true IgE-mediated allergic reactions to amides exceedingly rare.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Ortho-toluidine: This aromatic metabolite is produced during hepatic clearance of prilocaine and causes methaemoglobinaemia rather than immune hypersensitivity.
  • B. Monoethylglycinexylidide: This dealkylated derivative is an active hepatic metabolite of lidocaine associated with central nervous system toxicity.
  • C. Diethylaminoethanol: This alcohol fragment results from procaine hydrolysis but possesses very low immunogenic potential in sensitized individuals.
  • E. Pipecoloxylidide: This metabolic intermediate is formed during hepatic degradation of pipecolic acid derivatives such as bupivacaine and mepivacaine.

Key point: Identify para-aminobenzoic acid as the major antigenic metabolite responsible for allergic reactions to ester local anaesthetics.

Question 2 · Antimicrobials, antiseptics and drugs used in the mouth · easy

The dental infection control committee at a hospital in Muzaffarabad reviews chemical agents used for high-level disinfection of semi-critical heat-sensitive items, such as acrylic intraoral photography mirrors and rubber dam frames, using a 2% alkaline glutaraldehyde solution.

What is the primary mechanism of antimicrobial action of this chemical disinfectant?

  1. A. Generation of hydroxyl free radicals that oxidize membrane lipids
  2. B. Alkylation of amino, carboxyl, and hydroxyl groups of proteins
  3. C. Competitive inhibition of bacterial dihydrofolate reductase
  4. D. Disruption of bacterial cytoplasmic membranes by cationic biguanide binding
  5. E. Osmotic lysis via selective extraction of membrane cholesterol
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Alkylation of amino, carboxyl, and hydroxyl groups of proteins

Glutaraldehyde is a saturated dialdehyde used as a high-level disinfectant and chemical sterilant for heat-sensitive semi-critical medical and dental instruments. It exerts rapid bactericidal, virucidal, fungicidal, and (with prolonged exposure of 3 to 10 hours) sporicidal activity. Its mechanism of action involves the alkylation of sulfhydryl, hydroxyl, carboxyl, and amino groups of microbial proteins, enzymes, and nucleic acids. This causes extensive intermolecular and intramolecular cross-linking of peptide chains, irreversibly disrupting protein tertiary structure, inactivating essential metabolic enzymes, and freezing cellular transport mechanisms. Because glutaraldehyde is irritating to skin and mucous membranes and releases toxic vapours, treated instruments must be thoroughly rinsed with sterile water before clinical use.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Generation of hydroxyl free radicals that oxidize membrane lipids: Hydroxyl free radical generation is the oxidative mechanism of hydrogen peroxide and peracetic acid, not glutaraldehyde.
  • C. Competitive inhibition of bacterial dihydrofolate reductase: Inhibition of dihydrofolate reductase is the mechanism of trimethoprim, not a broad-spectrum liquid chemical disinfectant.
  • D. Disruption of bacterial cytoplasmic membranes by cationic biguanide binding: Cationic disruption of cytoplasmic membranes is the mechanism of chlorhexidine and quaternary ammonium compounds.
  • E. Osmotic lysis via selective extraction of membrane cholesterol: Glutaraldehyde does not selectively extract lipids; it chemically cross-links proteins and amine-containing structures.

Key point: Describe the mechanism of glutaraldehyde as chemical alkylation and cross-linking of microbial proteins and nucleic acids.

Question 3 · Local anaesthetics, pain control, sedation and emergency drugs · medium

A 62-year-old man presents to a dental teaching hospital in Peshawar for surgical removal of a retained root of the lower right first molar (tooth 46). He has a history of stage 2 primary hypertension and ischemic heart disease, and takes amlodipine and bisoprolol. Preoperative blood pressure is 158/94 mmHg and pulse is 68/min. The operator plans local anaesthesia using 3% prilocaine with a non-adrenergic vasoconstrictor to minimise cardiac stimulation.

Which vasoconstrictor is present in combination with 3% prilocaine for this purpose?

  1. A. Adrenaline
  2. B. Phenylephrine
  3. C. Noradrenaline
  4. D. Felypressin
  5. E. Levonordefrin
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. Felypressin

Felypressin (octapressin) is a synthetic analogue of vasopressin used at a concentration of 0.03 IU/mL in combination with 3% prilocaine. It acts directly on V1 receptors on the vascular smooth muscle of the venous and arteriolar microcirculation, causing vasoconstriction without direct stimulation of cardiac beta-1 adrenergic receptors. This makes it a valuable alternative to adrenaline-containing formulations in patients with significant cardiovascular disease, uncontrolled hypertension, or cardiac dysrhythmias, although it should be avoided in pregnant women due to potential oxytocic uterine contraction effects.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Adrenaline: Adrenaline is a sympathomimetic amine with potent beta-1 cardiac stimulating effects that this clinician specifically aims to avoid in this hypertensive patient.
  • B. Phenylephrine: Phenylephrine is an alpha-1 agonist rarely used in modern dental local anaesthetic cartridges and would cause significant peripheral vasoconstriction and blood pressure elevation.
  • C. Noradrenaline: Noradrenaline produces marked alpha-1 vasoconstriction and severe reflex bradycardia and is not recommended as an LA additive in modern dental practice.
  • E. Levonordefrin: Levonordefrin acts primarily on alpha receptors and is not formulated with 3% prilocaine in standard dental cartridges.

Key point: Identify felypressin as the non-adrenergic synthetic vasopressin analogue paired with prilocaine in dental practice.

Question 4 · Antimicrobials, antiseptics and drugs used in the mouth · medium

A 45-year-old farmer from Charsadda attends the dental OPD with chronic apical periodontitis of tooth 12 (upper right lateral incisor). Following chemo-mechanical preparation, the clinician places non-setting calcium hydroxide paste into the canal as an inter-appointment medicament for two weeks to eliminate residual Gram-negative bacteria and their endotoxins.

How does calcium hydroxide kill bacteria and alter tissue?

  1. A. Inhibiting bacterial DNA gyrase and topoisomerase IV enzymes directly
  2. B. Inducing hyperosmotic cellular lysis through concentrated calcium ion influx
  3. C. Chelating iron and magnesium ions essential for bacterial metalloenzymes
  4. D. Generating reactive singlet oxygen species under photo-activation
  5. E. Releasing hydroxyl ions that create an alkaline pH and denature cell proteins
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Releasing hydroxyl ions that create an alkaline pH and denature cell proteins

Calcium hydroxide [Ca(OH)2] is an alkaline earth metal hydroxide and the gold-standard inter-appointment intracanal medicament. In an aqueous environment, it slowly dissociates into calcium (Ca2+) and hydroxyl (OH-) ions, generating an intensely alkaline environment with a pH of approximately 12.5. The lethal antibacterial effects are driven by hydroxyl ions, which induce lipid peroxidation of bacterial cytoplasmic membranes, break down bacterial DNA strands, and denature structural and metabolic enzymes. Importantly, the high alkalinity chemically hydrolyzes lipid A, the toxic core component of Gram-negative bacterial lipopolysaccharide (endotoxin), detoxifying it and eliminating periapical osteolytic signaling. However, its effectiveness against Enterococcus faecalis is variable due to bacterial proton pumps that resist alkaline stress.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Inhibiting bacterial DNA gyrase and topoisomerase IV enzymes directly: Topoisomerase inhibition is the mechanism of fluoroquinolone antibiotics, not of alkaline earth metal hydroxides.
  • B. Inducing hyperosmotic cellular lysis through concentrated calcium ion influx: Bacterial destruction by calcium hydroxide is mediated by hydroxyl ion-induced chemical damage, not by osmotic dehydration.
  • C. Chelating iron and magnesium ions essential for bacterial metalloenzymes: Mineral chelation is the antimicrobial action of agents like EDTA, whereas calcium hydroxide provides an excess of calcium.
  • D. Generating reactive singlet oxygen species under photo-activation: Reactive singlet oxygen generation is the mechanism of antimicrobial photodynamic therapy (aPDT), not of calcium hydroxide paste.

Key point: Explain the mechanism of action of calcium hydroxide as an antimicrobial and endotoxin-neutralising medicament.

Question 5 · Local anaesthetics, pain control, sedation and emergency drugs · medium

A 28-year-old male attends a dental clinic in Abbottabad for surgical extraction of an impacted lower molar. Five minutes after local infiltration of 2% lidocaine with 1:80,000 adrenaline, he complains of sudden severe itching, difficulty in breathing, and dizziness. On examination, he has generalized urticaria, bilateral expiratory wheezing, inspiratory stridor, and facial oedema. His pulse is 132/min, blood pressure is 74/42 mmHg, and respiratory rate is 28/min.

Which emergency drug and administration route is indicated first?

  1. A. Intramuscular adrenaline (0.5 mg)
  2. B. Intravenous hydrocortisone (200 mg)
  3. C. Intravenous chlorphenamine (10 mg)
  4. D. Nebulised salbutamol (5 mg)
  5. E. Intravenous adrenaline (1 mg)
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Intramuscular adrenaline (0.5 mg)

In acute anaphylaxis presenting with airway compromise (stridor), breathing difficulty (wheeze), or circulatory collapse (hypotension), intramuscular adrenaline 1:1,000 (1 mg/mL) at a dose of 0.5 mg (500 micrograms) injected into the anterolateral aspect of the middle third of the thigh is the immediate treatment of choice. Adrenaline acts via alpha-1 adrenoceptors to cause vasoconstriction and reduce mucosal oedema, beta-1 receptors to increase myocardial contractility, and beta-2 receptors to produce bronchodilation and inhibit mast-cell mediator release. Antihistamines and corticosteroids are strictly second-line therapies given only after adrenaline stabilises haemodynamics. Intravenous adrenaline boluses are dangerous in non-arrest patients.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Intravenous hydrocortisone (200 mg): Glucocorticoids have an onset of action delayed by several hours and will not relieve acute stridor or hypotension.
  • C. Intravenous chlorphenamine (10 mg): Antihistamines act as second-line adjuvant agents for cutaneous symptoms but fail to reverse acute bronchospasm or cardiovascular collapse.
  • D. Nebulised salbutamol (5 mg): Beta-2 agonists reduce bronchospasm but do not relieve severe laryngeal stridor or profound distributive hypotension seen.
  • E. Intravenous adrenaline (1 mg): Intravenous bolus adrenaline at this dose is reserved for cardiac arrest; in spontaneous circulation it risks fatal ventricular arrhythmias or intracranial haemorrhage.

Key point: Identify intramuscular adrenaline as the definitive first-line emergency drug for severe anaphylaxis in the dental chair.

Question 6 · Antimicrobials, antiseptics and drugs used in the mouth · easy

A 29-year-old woman attends a dental teaching hospital in Peshawar with a 3-day history of worsening swelling on the left side of her mandible following pain in her lower left third molar (38). On examination, she has a firm, diffuse swelling extending over the left angle of the mandible with marked trismus (mouth opening 12 mm), elevated floor of the mouth, temperature 38.8°C, pulse 108/min, and tender submandibular lymphadenopathy.

Which feature most clearly calls for antibiotics as well?

  1. A. Mild tender submandibular lymphadenopathy
  2. B. Reduced mouth opening secondary to muscle spasm
  3. C. Duration of dental symptoms exceeding forty-eight hours
  4. D. Spreading facial swelling with fever and severe trismus
  5. E. Severe localized pain on percussion of the molar
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. Spreading facial swelling with fever and severe trismus

In dental practice, systemic antibiotics are adjuncts rather than primary therapy and should never be used as substitutes for local operative intervention (such as pulpectomy, incision and drainage, or extraction). According to the Scottish Dental Clinical Effectiveness Programme (SDCEP) and Faculty of General Dental Practice (FGDP) guidelines, antibiotics are indicated only when there is evidence of spreading infection (diffuse cellulitis, facial swelling crossing fascial planes) or systemic involvement (pyrexia above 38°C, tachycardia, malaise, lymphadenopathy, or trismus threatening airway patency). Localised dental abscesses without systemic spread resolve reliably with surgical drainage or tooth debridement alone.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Mild tender submandibular lymphadenopathy: Reactive regional lymphadenopathy alone without spreading tissue infection or systemic toxic signs does not mandate systemic antimicrobial treatment.
  • B. Reduced mouth opening secondary to muscle spasm: Isolated trismus from local muscle soreness following prolonged opening or mild inflammation does not require antibiotics without signs of spreading fascial space infection.
  • C. Duration of dental symptoms exceeding forty-eight hours: Symptom duration alone does not dictate antibiotic need; surgical drainage and cause elimination remain paramount regardless of whether symptoms lasted 2 or 5 days.
  • E. Severe localized pain on percussion of the molar: Localized percussion tenderness reflects periapical inflammation, which is managed effectively by local dental measures rather than systemic antibiotics.

Key point: Identify spreading infection and systemic compromise as the primary clinical indications for systemic antibiotics in dentistry.

Question 7 · Local anaesthetics, pain control, sedation and emergency drugs · easy

A 68-year-old man attends a dental clinic in Mirpur for extraction of a loose upper left second molar (tooth 27). He had a myocardial infarction four months ago and experiences frequent ventricular premature beats. The dentist decides to avoid adrenergic vasoconstrictors entirely and selects a plain local anaesthetic that produces the least intrinsic vasodilation to secure adequate operating anaesthesia.

Which local anaesthetic agent is the most appropriate choice for this patient?

  1. A. Bupivacaine 0.5% plain
  2. B. Lidocaine 2% plain
  3. C. Procaine 2% plain
  4. D. Articaine 4% plain
  5. E. Mepivacaine 3% plain
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Mepivacaine 3% plain

Most local anaesthetics are vasodilators at clinical concentrations. Lidocaine and procaine induce substantial peripheral vasodilation, leading to rapid systemic reabsorption, short duration of action, and low pulpal success when used without adrenaline. Mepivacaine, an amino-amide, is unique in possessing minimal intrinsic vasodilator activity. Formulated as a 3% plain solution (30 mg/mL), it produces adequate pulpal anaesthesia (20 to 40 minutes) and soft tissue anaesthesia (2 to 3 hours) in infiltration and nerve blocks. It is the premier choice when catecholamines are contraindicated.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Bupivacaine 0.5% plain: Bupivacaine causes marked vasodilation and without adrenaline provides variable, unpredictable depth of operative anaesthesia.
  • B. Lidocaine 2% plain: Plain lidocaine is a potent vasodilator that is washed out rapidly into the circulation, providing brief and unreliable pulpal block.
  • C. Procaine 2% plain: Procaine exhibits the most profound vasodilation of all local anaesthetics and cannot provide effective surgical anaesthesia without adrenaline.
  • D. Articaine 4% plain: Plain articaine induces significant vasodilation and is not commercially formulated or recommended without a vasoconstrictor.

Key point: Identify mepivacaine plain as the local anaesthetic with minimal intrinsic vasodilation suitable when vasoconstrictors are contraindicated.

Question 8 · Antimicrobials, antiseptics and drugs used in the mouth · easy

A 31-year-old man presents to the dental emergency service in Mardan with severe facial swelling of 24 hours' duration. Examination reveals an acute canine space infection originating from a non-vital upper right canine (13). The right upper lip and nasolabial fold are obliterated, with swelling extending toward the lower eyelid. He has a temperature of 38.5°C and pulse of 96/min. He has no known drug allergies and has never had an adverse reaction to antibiotics. Local debridement and drainage are initiated immediately.

Which systemic antibiotic regimen is the first-line empirical choice for this patient?

  1. A. Oral erythromycin 250 mg every 6 hours
  2. B. Oral ciprofloxacin 500 mg every 12 hours
  3. C. Oral doxycycline 100 mg every 12 hours
  4. D. Oral vancomycin 125 mg every 6 hours
  5. E. Oral amoxicillin 500 mg every 8 hours
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Oral amoxicillin 500 mg every 8 hours

According to the British National Formulary (BNF) Dental Practitioners' Formulary and SDCEP guidelines, amoxicillin (500 mg orally every 8 hours) is the first-line empirical antimicrobial agent for severe, spreading odontogenic infections with systemic involvement in patients without a history of penicillin hypersensitivity. Amoxicillin has bactericidal activity against the predominant odontogenic pathogens, including facultative gram-positive streptococci and many oral anaerobes, excellent oral bioavailability (greater than that of ampicillin or phenoxymethylpenicillin), and a favourable safety profile. The duration should be kept short (typically up to 5 days, reviewed at 48-72 hours) and used strictly as an adjunct to primary surgical drainage and tooth debridement.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Oral erythromycin 250 mg every 6 hours: Erythromycin is an alternative strictly reserved for patients with true penicillin allergy; it exhibits higher rates of gastrointestinal intolerance and resistance.
  • B. Oral ciprofloxacin 500 mg every 12 hours: Fluoroquinolones have poor activity against oral streptococci and mouth anaerobes and are inappropriate for acute odontogenic infections.
  • C. Oral doxycycline 100 mg every 12 hours: Tetracyclines are bacteriostatic agents with unreliable coverage against viridans streptococci and are not first-line drugs for acute facial cellulitis.
  • D. Oral vancomycin 125 mg every 6 hours: Oral vancomycin is non-absorbable from the gastrointestinal tract and is indicated exclusively for Clostridioides difficile enterocolitis.

Key point: Identify oral amoxicillin as the first-line empirical systemic antibiotic for severe spreading odontogenic infection.

Question 9 · Local anaesthetics, pain control, sedation and emergency drugs · easy

A dental surgeon in Bannu is planning quadrant restorative procedures for a healthy 65 kg adult under local anaesthesia. He selects 4% articaine with 1:100,000 adrenaline and consults standard dental pharmacopoeias to confirm the maximum recommended dose per unit body weight before commencing treatment.

What is the maximum dose of articaine for a healthy adult?

  1. A. 2.0 mg/kg
  2. B. 4.4 mg/kg
  3. C. 5.0 mg/kg
  4. D. 6.0 mg/kg
  5. E. 7.0 mg/kg
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. 7.0 mg/kg

Articaine 4% with adrenaline (1:100,000 or 1:200,000) is widely used in dental practice for infiltration and regional nerve blocks. According to standard pharmacological authorities including Malamed and manufacturer prescribing information, the maximum recommended dose (MRD) of articaine in healthy adults is 7.0 mg/kg of body weight, up to an absolute adult maximum of 500 mg. For a 4% solution (which contains 40 mg of articaine per millilitre), a 70 kg individual can receive up to 490 mg, representing approximately 12.2 mL or roughly five and a half standard 2.2 mL cartridges.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. 2.0 mg/kg: This is the maximum per-kilogram dose for bupivacaine, a much more potent and cardiotoxic amino-amide agent.
  • B. 4.4 mg/kg: This represents the conservative recommended dose ceiling for plain lidocaine or mepivacaine without vasoconstrictor.
  • C. 5.0 mg/kg: This value is the standard adult dose ceiling for plain prilocaine rather than 4% articaine with adrenaline.
  • D. 6.0 mg/kg: This is an arbitrary figure below the accepted international guideline limit for articaine with adrenaline.

Key point: State the maximum recommended dose of articaine per kilogram of body weight in an adult dental patient.

Question 10 · Antimicrobials, antiseptics and drugs used in the mouth · easy

A 54-year-old male presents to a dental teaching hospital in Peshawar for the surgical extraction of a retained root of his lower right first molar (46). His medical history includes a mechanical aortic valve replacement performed five years ago. He has no history of drug allergies. In consultation with his cardiologist, antibiotic prophylaxis against infective endocarditis is planned before this invasive dentoalveolar procedure in accordance with international guidelines (such as the American Heart Association and European Society of Cardiology).

Which prophylactic antibiotic regimen is the standard recommended oral choice?

  1. A. Oral clindamycin 600 mg two hours before the procedure
  2. B. Oral ciprofloxacin 500 mg one hour before the procedure
  3. C. Oral amoxicillin 2 g thirty to sixty minutes before the procedure
  4. D. Oral metronidazole 800 mg thirty minutes before the procedure
  5. E. Oral cefalexin 500 mg two hours before the procedure
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Oral amoxicillin 2 g thirty to sixty minutes before the procedure

According to the American Heart Association (AHA), the European Society of Cardiology (ESC), and SDCEP guidance, patients at the highest risk of adverse outcomes from infective endocarditis include those with prosthetic cardiac valves, prosthetic material used for cardiac valve repair, previous infective endocarditis, or congenital heart disease involving cyanotic lesions or prosthetic conduits. For invasive dental procedures that involve manipulation of gingival tissue, the periapical region of teeth, or perforation of the oral mucosa, systemic antibiotic prophylaxis is indicated for high-risk patients. The standard first-line oral regimen for non-allergic adults is amoxicillin 2 g administered as a single dose 30 to 60 minutes prior to the procedure.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Oral clindamycin 600 mg two hours before the procedure: Clindamycin is no longer recommended as a first-line alternative for endocarditis prophylaxis due to severe risks of Clostridioides difficile colitis.
  • B. Oral ciprofloxacin 500 mg one hour before the procedure: Ciprofloxacin has inadequate spectrum against viridans group oral streptococci and is never used for endocarditis prophylaxis.
  • D. Oral metronidazole 800 mg thirty minutes before the procedure: Metronidazole is ineffective against oral streptococci (the primary drivers of dental-origin infective endocarditis) and is not used for prophylaxis.
  • E. Oral cefalexin 500 mg two hours before the procedure: Cefalexin at a low dose of 500 mg is subtherapeutic; when oral cephalosporins are used in non-immediate penicillin allergy, the recommended adult dose is 2 g.

Key point: Specify the standard oral amoxicillin regimen for infective endocarditis prophylaxis in high-risk cardiac patients.
