Question 1 · Behavioural sciences and medical ethics · easy

A doctor has just told a 38-year-old mother of three that her cervical cancer has spread and cannot be cured. She begins to cry quietly and stops speaking. The doctor feels uncomfortable and is tempted to start explaining the palliative treatment options straight away to fill the silence. Her husband holds her hand.

What is the most appropriate response?

  1. A. Continue with treatment details
  2. B. Pause and acknowledge her feelings
  3. C. Reassure her that she still has plenty of time
  4. D. Offer to finish the conversation another day
  5. E. Hand her the information leaflet to read
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Pause and acknowledge her feelings

After bad news, patients often experience shock, sadness, anger or disbelief, and cannot take in further information. The doctor should allow silence, offer tissues, and respond with empathy: noticing the emotion, naming it ("I can see this is very hard to hear"), and showing understanding of its cause. Empathic statements and simple presence help the patient feel supported. Only when she is ready should the conversation move to what happens next, focusing on her priorities, symptom control and support for her family. Information may need to be repeated later, and a follow-up meeting and written information should be offered.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Continue with treatment details: She has just heard that her cancer cannot be cured and is crying; she will not take in palliative treatment options now.
  • C. Reassure her that she still has plenty of time: False or early reassurance closes the conversation; her feelings should be acknowledged first.
  • D. Offer to finish the conversation another day: Ending the meeting avoids her distress; a pause and acknowledgement come first.
  • E. Hand her the information leaflet to read: A leaflet at this moment ignores her emotion.

Key point: When a patient reacts emotionally to bad news, pause, allow silence and acknowledge the emotion before moving on.

Question 2 · Behavioural sciences and medical ethics · easy

A medical representative tells a general practitioner in Faisalabad that if he prescribes their brand of an antihypertensive to enough patients over the next 3 months, the company will pay for him and his wife to attend a "conference" at a holiday resort abroad. A cheaper, equally effective generic is available and widely used.

What is the most appropriate response?

  1. A. Accept, since the drug works
  2. B. Decline the offer
  3. C. Accept but prescribe as usual
  4. D. Accept, and tell his patients about it
  5. E. Accept for himself but not his wife
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Decline the offer

Doctors must make prescribing decisions based on patients' interests and the best available evidence, not on gifts, hospitality or financial rewards from companies. Incentives linked to prescribing volumes are clearly improper, and accepting them damages patients (who may pay more for no extra benefit), trust in the profession and fair use of resources. Professional codes, including the PM&DC code of ethics, prohibit accepting such inducements. Doctors may attend genuine educational meetings with modest, transparent sponsorship, should declare conflicts of interest, and should prefer cost-effective generic medicines where appropriate.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Accept, since the drug works: The drug works, but the trip is tied to how many prescriptions he writes, so his pen is being bought rather than his judgement used.
  • C. Accept but prescribe as usual: Taking the trip while intending to prescribe as before still accepts a reward offered for prescribing, and the obligation it creates is hard to shake off.
  • D. Accept, and tell his patients about it: Declaring an inducement does not make it acceptable; the trip is still a reward tied to how many prescriptions he writes.
  • E. Accept for himself but not his wife: Halving the holiday does not change what is being bought; the inducement is still tied to his prescribing.

Key point: Doctors must refuse gifts or rewards linked to prescribing; decisions must be based on patients' interests.

Question 3 · Behavioural sciences and medical ethics · easy

A 26-year-old woman attends a male doctor at a rural health centre with lower abdominal pain and vaginal discharge. A pelvic examination is needed to make a diagnosis. She seems anxious and keeps looking at the door. No female doctor is available today, but a female nurse works in the next room.

What should the doctor do before examining her?

  1. A. Explain; offer a chaperone
  2. B. Examine her quickly without explanation
  3. C. Ask her husband to do the examination
  4. D. Refuse to see her at all
  5. E. Examine her with the door open
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Explain; offer a chaperone

Before any intimate examination (of the breasts, genitals or rectum), the doctor should explain why it is needed and what it involves, obtain consent, offer a chaperone (preferably a trained health worker of the same sex as the patient), provide privacy for undressing and a cover, and stop if asked. A chaperone supports the patient, protects her dignity and protects the doctor from misunderstanding. In Pakistan, many women prefer a female doctor or chaperone; the doctor should respect cultural and religious preferences and, where possible, offer to arrange a female doctor. The presence of a chaperone, or her refusal of one, is recorded.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Examine her quickly without explanation: A quick examination without explanation gives her no chance to consent and ignores how anxious she plainly is.
  • C. Ask her husband to do the examination: Her husband is not trained to examine her, and an intimate examination is not something a relative performs.
  • D. Refuse to see her at all: Her abdominal pain and discharge need a diagnosis, and refusing to see her leaves a possibly serious infection untreated.
  • E. Examine her with the door open: An open door removes the privacy that an intimate examination most requires, and would increase her distress.

Key point: Before an intimate examination, explain it, get consent and offer a chaperone, respecting the patient's dignity and preferences.

Question 4 · Behavioural sciences and medical ethics · easy

A 45-year-old woman comes to an outpatient clinic in Rawalpindi. The doctor, who has a long queue waiting, wants to understand her problem fully without missing important concerns, and has been taught that the opening question of a consultation strongly shapes what the patient says. He is deciding how to begin after greeting her.

Which opening question is best?

  1. A. "How can I help you today?"
  2. B. "Is it your chest that hurts?"
  3. C. "You have high blood pressure, don't you?"
  4. D. "Have you taken any tablets?"
  5. E. "Do you smoke?"
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. "How can I help you today?"

Open questions ("How can I help you?", "Tell me about the pain") invite the patient to describe her problem in her own words, revealing her main concerns, ideas and expectations, and often the diagnosis. Patients who are allowed to speak without interruption usually finish within a couple of minutes. Closed questions (answered yes or no) are useful later to clarify details and check specific features, but used too early they narrow the conversation and can miss the real problem. Leading questions suggest the expected answer and should be avoided. A good consultation moves from open to closed questions ("open-to-closed cone").

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. "Is it your chest that hurts?": Naming the chest first steers her towards it and may bury the symptom she came about.
  • C. "You have high blood pressure, don't you?": Telling her she has high blood pressure before she has spoken both leads her and assumes the diagnosis.
  • D. "Have you taken any tablets?": Asking about tablets at the start narrows the consultation to medicines before her problem is even known.
  • E. "Do you smoke?": Smoking matters later in the history; as an opening it closes down what she was about to say.

Key point: Begin consultations with open questions, then use closed questions to clarify details.

Question 5 · Behavioural sciences and medical ethics · easy

An elderly consultant tells his students that he does not explain treatment options to his patients because they "would not understand" and "trust the doctor to know best". He decides on the treatment himself and tells the patient what to do, without asking about the patient's preferences or values, though he genuinely wants the best for them.

Which model of the doctor-patient relationship does he follow?

  1. A. Shared decision-making model
  2. B. Informative (consumer) model
  3. C. Paternalistic model
  4. D. Deliberative model
  5. E. Mutual participation model
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Paternalistic model

In the paternalistic (guardian) model, the doctor uses expertise to decide what is best for the patient, who is expected to comply, with little discussion of options or values. It may be appropriate in emergencies or when a patient genuinely prefers it, but as a routine approach it fails to respect autonomy and can lead to poor adherence and dissatisfaction. Modern practice favours models in which the patient participates: the informative model (doctor gives facts, patient chooses), the interpretive and deliberative models (doctor helps the patient clarify values and choose), and shared decision-making. Szasz and Hollender similarly described activity-passivity, guidance-cooperation and mutual participation relationships.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Shared decision-making model: Shared decision-making would mean discussing the options with the patient, which is exactly what he refuses to do.
  • B. Informative (consumer) model: The informative model gives the patient the facts and lets them choose; he gives neither the facts nor the choice.
  • D. Deliberative model: The deliberative model means helping patients examine their own values; he never asks what they value.
  • E. Mutual participation model: Mutual participation means doctor and patient decide together, which is the opposite of telling the patient what to do.

Key point: In the paternalistic model, the doctor decides what is best with little patient involvement; modern practice favours shared decisions.

Question 6 · Behavioural sciences and medical ethics · easy

A 50-year-old businessman was discharged a week ago after a confirmed myocardial infarction treated with a stent. At his follow-up appointment, he says the chest pain was "only indigestion", that the doctors overreacted, and that he has gone back to jogging and smoking and stopped his tablets because he does not need them.

Which defence mechanism is he using?

  1. A. Projection
  2. B. Sublimation
  3. C. Denial
  4. D. Displacement
  5. E. Reaction formation
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Denial

Defence mechanisms are unconscious mental processes that protect a person from anxiety or painful thoughts. Denial is the refusal to accept a reality that is too threatening, such as a serious diagnosis. It is common soon after a frightening event and can initially reduce distress, but when it persists it can be dangerous, leading to non-adherence and risky behaviour, as here. Doctors should respond with patience and empathy rather than confrontation, give clear, repeated information, involve family with permission, and explore his fears (such as loss of work or masculinity). Cardiac rehabilitation and follow-up help with adjustment.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Projection: Projection would mean accusing others of the fear he will not admit, but he is dismissing the event itself.
  • B. Sublimation: Sublimation would turn the feeling into something useful, such as throwing himself into a cardiac rehabilitation class.
  • D. Displacement: Displacement would mean venting his fear on his family or the nurses instead of the illness itself.
  • E. Reaction formation: Reaction formation would show as exaggerated concern about his heart, which is the opposite of what he is doing.

Key point: Denial is refusing to accept a threatening reality, such as a serious diagnosis; persistent denial can lead to non-adherence.

Question 7 · Behavioural sciences and medical ethics · easy

A 40-year-old patient admitted for a minor operation receives a phone call from his employer, who criticises him harshly for being away from work. He feels he cannot answer back to his boss. A few minutes later, he shouts angrily at the nurse because his tea is not hot enough, though she has done nothing wrong.

Which defence mechanism is this?

  1. A. Displacement
  2. B. Denial
  3. C. Projection
  4. D. Regression
  5. E. Intellectualisation
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Displacement

Displacement is redirecting feelings, usually anger, from the person or situation that caused them to a safer, less threatening target. A patient angry with his boss, his illness or himself may shout at a nurse, doctor or family member. Recognising displacement helps staff respond calmly rather than personally, acknowledging the patient's frustration and exploring its real source. Other defence mechanisms include projection (attributing one's own unacceptable feelings to others), regression (returning to earlier, childlike patterns of behaviour under stress, common in illness), rationalisation (giving plausible but false reasons) and intellectualisation (focusing on facts to avoid emotions).

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Denial: Denial would mean insisting the phone call never upset him or that his job is not at risk.
  • C. Projection: Projection would mean accusing the nurse of being angry with him rather than being angry himself.
  • D. Regression: Regression would show as childlike behaviour such as refusing to speak or wanting his mother, not focused anger.
  • E. Intellectualisation: Intellectualisation would mean discussing employment law and sick leave in detail to keep the feeling at a distance.

Key point: Displacement redirects feelings, often anger, from their source to a safer target.

Question 8 · Behavioural sciences and medical ethics · easy

A 45-year-old man with incurable lung cancer was angry at first that he had been diagnosed late. Now he tells the chaplain that if he can just live long enough to see his daughter married next spring, he will give generously to charity and never complain again. He has not yet shown signs of deep sadness or acceptance.

Which stage of the Kübler-Ross model does this show?

  1. A. Denial
  2. B. Anger
  3. C. Depression
  4. D. Acceptance
  5. E. Bargaining
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Bargaining

Elisabeth Kübler-Ross described five common reactions of people facing death or loss: denial ("This cannot be true"), anger ("Why me?"), bargaining (trying to negotiate, often with God, for more time or a different outcome in exchange for promises), depression (sadness at the losses to come) and acceptance (coming to terms with the situation). The stages are not fixed or sequential: people may move back and forth, skip stages or experience several at once. The model reminds clinicians that these reactions are normal and helps them respond with empathy, patience and good palliative care.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Denial: Denial would mean insisting the diagnosis is wrong, but he accepts that his cancer is incurable.
  • B. Anger: Anger was where he began, blaming the late diagnosis; he has moved past it.
  • C. Depression: Depression would bring the deep sadness that the vignette says he has not yet shown.
  • D. Acceptance: Acceptance would mean a settled readiness, not an offer made in exchange for more time.

Key point: In the Kübler-Ross model, bargaining is trying to negotiate for more time or a different outcome.

Question 9 · Behavioural sciences and medical ethics · easy

A 40-year-old man who smokes 20 cigarettes a day says he knows smoking is bad for him and he is thinking about stopping in the next few months, but he enjoys it and worries about weight gain and stress at work. He has not set a date or made any plan to quit, and has not tried before.

Which stage of change is he in?

  1. A. Precontemplation
  2. B. Preparation
  3. C. Action
  4. D. Maintenance
  5. E. Contemplation
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Contemplation

The transtheoretical (stages of change) model describes how people change behaviour. In precontemplation, they are not considering change in the next 6 months; in contemplation, they are thinking about change within 6 months but are ambivalent, weighing pros and cons; in preparation, they intend to act within the next month and may have taken small steps; in action, they have made a change within the past 6 months; in maintenance, the change has been sustained for over 6 months. Relapse is common and people may cycle through the stages. Interventions should match the stage: for contemplators, exploring ambivalence with motivational interviewing helps move them towards preparation.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Precontemplation: Precontemplation means seeing no reason to stop, but he accepts smoking is harmful and is thinking about quitting.
  • B. Preparation: Preparation means intending to act within a month, usually with a date set; he has set none.
  • C. Action: Action means he has already stopped, and he is still smoking twenty a day.
  • D. Maintenance: Maintenance means having stayed stopped for months, and he has never made an attempt.

Key point: Contemplation is thinking about change within 6 months while still ambivalent; interventions should match the stage.

Question 10 · Behavioural sciences and medical ethics · easy

A 22-year-old university student has had difficulty falling asleep for 3 months. He studies in bed until 2 am on his laptop, drinks several cups of tea and energy drinks in the evening, naps for 2 hours after classes and sleeps until noon at weekends. He is not depressed or anxious otherwise. He asks for sleeping tablets.

What is the most appropriate first step?

  1. A. A benzodiazepine every night
  2. B. A sedating antihistamine at night
  3. C. Sleep hygiene advice
  4. D. Longer daytime naps
  5. E. A glass of warm milk and reassurance
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Sleep hygiene advice

Insomnia is often maintained by habits that disrupt the body clock and associate bed with wakefulness. Sleep hygiene advice includes regular sleep and wake times (including weekends), avoiding caffeine and energy drinks after the afternoon, avoiding screens and work in bed, keeping the bedroom quiet, dark and cool, regular daytime exercise, avoiding long daytime naps, and getting up if unable to sleep. Cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia (CBT-I), including stimulus control and sleep restriction, is the most effective long-term treatment. Hypnotics such as benzodiazepines and Z-drugs are for short-term use only because of tolerance, dependence, daytime drowsiness and falls.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. A benzodiazepine every night: A nightly benzodiazepine brings tolerance and dependence and leaves the late studying, caffeine and long naps untouched.
  • B. A sedating antihistamine at night: Sedating antihistamines cause next-day drowsiness and tolerance; they are not first-line.
  • D. Longer daytime naps: Longer naps take more sleep pressure out of the evening and will make falling asleep harder still.
  • E. A glass of warm milk and reassurance: Reassurance alone does not address the habits that keep insomnia going.

Key point: Insomnia is managed first with sleep hygiene and CBT for insomnia; hypnotics are for short-term use only.
