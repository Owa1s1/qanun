Question 1 · Lower limb · easy

A doctor finds enlarged, hard, painless lymph nodes just below the inguinal ligament in a 65-year-old man, in the horizontal group. He has had bleeding and pain on passing stool for 3 months, and has no lesions on his legs, feet, penis or scrotum.

A cancer in which site would most likely spread first to these nodes?

  1. A. Testis
  2. B. Rectum
  3. C. Prostate
  4. D. Anal canal below the pectinate line
  5. E. Urinary bladder, including its neck
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. Anal canal below the pectinate line

The superficial inguinal nodes receive lymph from the skin of the lower limb, buttock and abdominal wall below the umbilicus, the perineum, the scrotum (but not the testis), the penis, the lower vagina and vulva, and the anal canal below the pectinate line. Enlarged inguinal nodes therefore call for examination of all these areas. The testis drains along the gonadal vessels to the para-aortic nodes because it develops near the kidney and descends.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Testis: The testis drains to the para-aortic nodes, along its vessels.
  • B. Rectum: The rectum drains to pararectal, inferior mesenteric and internal iliac nodes.
  • C. Prostate: The prostate drains to the internal iliac nodes.
  • E. Urinary bladder, including its neck: The bladder drains to the external and internal iliac nodes.

Key point: The anal canal below the pectinate line drains to the superficial inguinal nodes; the testis drains to the para-aortic nodes.

Question 2 · Neuroanatomy · easy

A 40-year-old woman from Mardan has a midbrain lesion. Her right pupil is dilated and does not react to light, while her left pupil is normal.

Which nucleus is damaged?

  1. A. Right Edinger-Westphal nucleus
  2. B. Left Edinger-Westphal nucleus
  3. C. Right oculomotor nucleus (somatic part)
  4. D. Left oculomotor nucleus (somatic part)
  5. E. Right ciliary ganglion
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Right Edinger-Westphal nucleus

The Edinger-Westphal nucleus is the parasympathetic part of the oculomotor complex in the midbrain. Its fibres leave with the oculomotor nerve, synapse in the ciliary ganglion in the orbit, and then supply the sphincter pupillae muscle to constrict the pupil. Pupil size reflects the balance between parasympathetic constriction and sympathetic dilation. Damage to the right Edinger-Westphal nucleus removes constriction, so the right pupil becomes dilated and reacts poorly to light. The somatic oculomotor nucleus moves the extraocular muscles and does not affect pupil size. This distinction helps localise brainstem lesions without needing drug knowledge.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Left Edinger-Westphal nucleus: Damage here would dilate the left pupil, not the right pupil described in the case.
  • C. Right oculomotor nucleus (somatic part): The somatic part moves the extraocular muscles and does not control pupil size.
  • D. Left oculomotor nucleus (somatic part): This nucleus controls left eye movements and would not cause a dilated right pupil.
  • E. Right ciliary ganglion: The ciliary ganglion is in the orbit, not the midbrain, and the stem localises the lesion to the midbrain.

Key point: Identify the right Edinger-Westphal nucleus as the cause of a dilated right pupil after a midbrain lesion.

Question 3 · Thorax and mediastinum · easy

A large baby born to a mother with poorly controlled diabetes is deeply cyanosed within hours of birth, with oxygen saturations of 60% that do not improve with oxygen. There is no murmur and he is not breathless. Chest X-ray shows an "egg on a string" heart. Echocardiography shows the aorta arising from the right ventricle and the pulmonary artery from the left.

What developmental error causes this?

  1. A. Failure of the septum primum to fuse
  2. B. Anterosuperior shift of the outlet septum
  3. C. Persistence of the ductus arteriosus
  4. D. Failure of the endocardial cushions to fuse
  5. E. The aorticopulmonary septum fails to spiral
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. The aorticopulmonary septum fails to spiral

The truncus arteriosus and conus are divided by the aorticopulmonary (conotruncal) septum, formed by ridges that grow with the help of neural crest cells and normally spiral through 180 degrees. If the septum does not spiral, the aorta arises from the right ventricle and the pulmonary trunk from the left, creating two parallel circulations. Survival depends on mixing through a patent foramen ovale, ductus arteriosus or VSD; prostaglandin E1 keeps the ductus open until balloon septostomy and the arterial switch operation. It is more common in infants of diabetic mothers. Failure of the septum to form at all leaves a persistent truncus arteriosus.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Failure of the septum primum to fuse: This causes an ostium primum defect.
  • B. Anterosuperior shift of the outlet septum: This shift causes tetralogy of Fallot.
  • C. Persistence of the ductus arteriosus: A patent ductus is not the primary defect.
  • D. Failure of the endocardial cushions to fuse: This causes atrioventricular septal defects.

Key point: Transposition of the great arteries results from failure of the aorticopulmonary septum to spiral.

Question 4 · Upper limb · medium

A 24-year-old man from Mansehra dislocates his right shoulder anteriorly while wrestling. After the shoulder is reduced, he has a flattened deltoid contour and dull sensation over the regimental badge area. He cannot abduct his arm beyond the first 15 degrees.

Which nerve is most likely injured?

  1. A. Suprascapular nerve
  2. B. Musculocutaneous nerve
  3. C. Radial nerve
  4. D. Median nerve
  5. E. Axillary nerve
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Axillary nerve

The axillary nerve runs backward through the quadrangular space and winds around the surgical neck of the humerus. In an anterior shoulder dislocation, the humeral head stretches this nerve against the neck of the humerus. The nerve supplies the deltoid muscle and the skin over the regimental badge area, the patch over the lower part of the deltoid. Injury therefore flattens the shoulder contour and removes the first 15 degrees of abduction, which is started by supraspinatus before deltoid takes over. Suprascapular nerve injury would not cause sensory loss over the regimental badge area. This matters because most axillary nerve injuries after dislocation recover within a few months.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Suprascapular nerve: This nerve supplies supraspinatus and infraspinatus, so injury affects the start of abduction and lateral rotation but not the regimental badge area.
  • B. Musculocutaneous nerve: This nerve flexes the elbow and supplies sensation over the lateral forearm, not the lateral shoulder.
  • C. Radial nerve: The radial nerve extends the wrist and fingers and lies in the spiral groove of the humerus, not around the shoulder joint.
  • D. Median nerve: The median nerve supplies the front of the forearm and the thenar muscles, not the deltoid or the regimental badge area.

Key point: Identify the axillary nerve as the nerve injured in anterior shoulder dislocation.

Question 5 · Abdomen and pelvis · easy

A 22-year-old man first has a dull, poorly localised ache around his umbilicus, with nausea and loss of appetite. Twelve hours later the pain becomes sharp and settles in the right iliac fossa, worse on coughing and walking. He has a fever of 37.9 °C and tenderness with guarding at McBurney's point. His white count is 15 x 10^9/L.

Why does the early pain appear around the umbilicus?

  1. A. The appendix lies behind the umbilicus
  2. B. Visceral afferents from the midgut enter the cord at about T10
  3. C. The parietal peritoneum at the umbilicus is the first to be inflamed
  4. D. The vagus nerve carries the pain
  5. E. The obturator nerve is irritated
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Visceral afferents from the midgut enter the cord at about T10

Early in appendicitis, distension and inflammation of the appendix wall stimulate visceral afferent fibres, which travel with sympathetic nerves to about the T10 segment. Visceral pain is poorly localised and is felt in the dermatome of that segment, around the umbilicus. When the inflammation reaches the parietal peritoneum, which has somatic innervation from the overlying wall, the pain becomes sharp and localised to the right iliac fossa. Foregut pain is felt in the epigastrium, midgut pain at the umbilicus and hindgut pain in the suprapubic region.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. The appendix lies behind the umbilicus: The appendix lies in the right iliac fossa.
  • C. The parietal peritoneum at the umbilicus is the first to be inflamed: The parietal peritoneum becomes involved later, in the right iliac fossa.
  • D. The vagus nerve carries the pain: The vagus does not carry this pain.
  • E. The obturator nerve is irritated: Obturator irritation causes pain in the medial thigh.

Key point: Early appendicitis pain is visceral and referred to the umbilicus (T10); later pain is somatic and localised.

Question 6 · General anatomy, embryology and histology · medium

A student in a laboratory in Abbottabad (KP) watches a model of a 22-day embryo. The cranial neuropore closes on day 25, and the caudal neuropore closes on day 28.

Which defect results from failure of the caudal neuropore to close?

  1. A. Iniencephaly
  2. B. Anencephaly
  3. C. Encephalocele
  4. D. Craniorachischisis
  5. E. Spina bifida
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Spina bifida

The neural tube closes in two openings called neuropores. The cranial neuropore closes around day 25, and the caudal neuropore closes around day 28. If the cranial neuropore fails, the brain and skull do not form, causing anencephaly. If the caudal neuropore fails, the vertebral arches over the spinal cord do not fuse, leaving an opening in the lower back; this is spina bifida. Encephalocele is a hernia of brain tissue through a skull defect. Craniorachischisis means the whole tube stays open. Iniencephaly is a rare defect with the head bent back. Knowing the timing of neuropore closure explains which defect follows which failure.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Iniencephaly: This is a rare severe defect with the head bent sharply backward, not simple caudal neuropore failure.
  • B. Anencephaly: This follows failure of the cranial neuropore; the forebrain and skull are absent.
  • C. Encephalocele: This is a midline defect where brain tissue herniates through a skull defect, usually from cranial neuropore failure.
  • D. Craniorachischisis: This is failure of the entire neural tube to close, affecting both cranial and caudal ends.

Key point: Link caudal neuropore closure failure to spina bifida.

Question 7 · Head and neck · easy

A dentist gives a left inferior alveolar nerve block, injecting on the inner side of the ramus of the mandible, to extract a lower molar. As well as the lower teeth, gums and lower lip, the patient's left half of the tongue becomes numb, front two thirds only. His taste on that side is also reduced for a few hours.

Why is the tongue also numb?

  1. A. The inferior alveolar nerve supplies the tongue
  2. B. The lingual nerve lies close by and is blocked too
  3. C. The hypoglossal nerve is blocked at the same time
  4. D. The glossopharyngeal nerve is blocked by spread
  5. E. The chorda tympani carries general sensation to the tongue
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. The lingual nerve lies close by and is blocked too

For an inferior alveolar nerve block, local anaesthetic is placed near the mandibular foramen on the medial surface of the ramus, where the inferior alveolar nerve enters the mandibular canal. The lingual nerve lies just in front and medial to it in the pterygomandibular space, so it is usually blocked as well, numbing the anterior two-thirds of the tongue and the lingual gum. The inferior alveolar nerve supplies the lower teeth and, through its mental branch, the lower lip and chin. Injecting too far back can reach the parotid and cause temporary facial palsy.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. The inferior alveolar nerve supplies the tongue: It supplies the lower teeth, lip and chin.
  • C. The hypoglossal nerve is blocked at the same time: This would paralyse the tongue, not numb it.
  • D. The glossopharyngeal nerve is blocked by spread: This supplies the back third of the tongue, far from the injection.
  • E. The chorda tympani carries general sensation to the tongue: The chorda carries taste, which joins the lingual nerve.

Key point: An inferior alveolar nerve block usually also blocks the nearby lingual nerve, numbing the tongue.

Question 8 · Lower limb · medium

A 55-year-old overweight woman with diabetes has a painful swelling behind her medial malleolus for 6 months, and her foot has slowly become flat. Seen from behind, more of her toes are visible on the affected side, and her heel turns outwards. She cannot rise onto the toes of that foot alone, and when she tries, her heel does not swing inwards.

Failure of which tendon explains this?

  1. A. Tibialis anterior
  2. B. Fibularis longus
  3. C. Tibialis posterior
  4. D. Flexor hallucis longus
  5. E. Tendo calcaneus (Achilles tendon)
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Tibialis posterior

Tibialis posterior passes behind the medial malleolus and inserts mainly into the tuberosity of the navicular. It inverts and plantarflexes the foot and is the main dynamic support of the medial longitudinal arch. Degeneration or rupture of its tendon, often in middle-aged overweight women, causes progressive acquired flat foot. Seen from behind, the forefoot points outwards so that more toes are visible ("too many toes" sign), and the patient cannot do a single-leg heel raise.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Tibialis anterior: This dorsiflexes and inverts the foot from the front.
  • B. Fibularis longus: This everts the foot and supports the lateral arch.
  • D. Flexor hallucis longus: This flexes the big toe and gives only minor arch support.
  • E. Tendo calcaneus (Achilles tendon): Achilles rupture weakens plantarflexion but does not flatten the arch.

Key point: Tibialis posterior dysfunction causes acquired flat foot with the "too many toes" sign and loss of single heel raise.

Question 9 · Neuroanatomy · medium

A 30-year-old woman has had headaches at the back of her head for a year that come on with coughing, laughing and straining, lasting minutes. She has burned her hands on the stove without feeling it. She has loss of pain and temperature over both shoulders and arms in a cape, with normal touch. MRI shows the cerebellar tonsils 8 mm below the foramen magnum.

What is the diagnosis?

  1. A. Chiari I malformation with a syrinx
  2. B. Chiari II malformation
  3. C. Dandy-Walker malformation
  4. D. Tonsillar herniation from a brain tumour
  5. E. Anterior spinal artery stroke
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Chiari I malformation with a syrinx

In Chiari I malformation the cerebellar tonsils extend more than about 5 mm below the foramen magnum. It is often found in young adults. The tonsils block CSF flow at the foramen magnum, so pressure rises with coughing and straining, causing occipital headache. A fluid-filled cavity (syrinx) often forms in the cervical cord. As the syrinx enlarges, it damages the crossing pain and temperature fibres in the anterior white commissure, giving a cape-like loss over the shoulders and arms. Surgery to enlarge the foramen magnum may help.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Chiari II malformation: This is found in babies with myelomeningocele and involves the vermis and medulla.
  • C. Dandy-Walker malformation: This has a large posterior fossa cyst and a missing vermis.
  • D. Tonsillar herniation from a brain tumour: There is no mass, and she is not drowsy.
  • E. Anterior spinal artery stroke: This comes on suddenly and causes weakness.

Key point: Chiari I malformation: low cerebellar tonsils, cough headache and often a cervical syrinx.

Question 10 · Thorax and mediastinum · medium

A 58-year-old heavy smoker in Rawalpindi has had severe pain down the inner arm and into the little finger for 4 months, not helped by painkillers. He has wasting of the small muscles of the hand, weak finger spreading, and a small pupil with drooping of the right eyelid; the right side of his face does not sweat. Chest X-ray shows a shadow at the right lung apex.

Which structures does a tumour at the right lung apex compress to cause these signs?

  1. A. The C5 and C6 roots and the phrenic nerve
  2. B. Lower brachial plexus and stellate ganglion
  3. C. The vagus and recurrent laryngeal nerves
  4. D. The long thoracic and suprascapular nerves
  5. E. The superior vena cava and the azygos vein
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Lower brachial plexus and stellate ganglion

The lung apex rises into the root of the neck above the first rib, covered by the cervical pleura and suprapleural membrane. Nearby lie the lower trunk of the brachial plexus (C8 and T1) passing over the first rib, the cervicothoracic (stellate) sympathetic ganglion on the neck of the first rib, and the subclavian vessels. A superior sulcus (Pancoast) tumour, usually non-small cell lung cancer, causes shoulder and ulnar-side arm pain, wasting of the small hand muscles, and Horner syndrome (ptosis, miosis, anhidrosis).

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. The C5 and C6 roots and the phrenic nerve: C5 and C6 would weaken the shoulder; the phrenic nerve would paralyse the diaphragm.
  • C. The vagus and recurrent laryngeal nerves: These would cause hoarseness, not hand wasting.
  • D. The long thoracic and suprascapular nerves: These would cause winging and weak shoulder rotation.
  • E. The superior vena cava and the azygos vein: This would cause facial and arm swelling.

Key point: A Pancoast tumour compresses the lower brachial plexus (C8, T1) and the stellate ganglion, causing hand weakness and Horner syndrome.
