Question 1 · Metals, polymers, ceramics and implant materials · easy

An implant surgeon in Peshawar places a Grade 4 commercially pure titanium (CpTi) root-form endosseous dental implant into the lower right first molar extraction socket (46) of a 40-year-old man. The patient has a known history of severe skin allergies to nickel and chromium jewellery.

Why is commercially pure titanium so well tolerated by bone?

  1. A. Its rough machined surface alone
  2. B. Its high nickel content
  3. C. It is stiffer than cobalt-chromium
  4. D. It slowly dissolves in tissue fluid
  5. E. Its stable oxide surface film
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Its stable oxide surface film

Titanium and its alloys (most notably Grade 1–4 Commercially Pure Titanium [CpTi] and the titanium-aluminium-vanadium alloy **Ti-6Al-4V**) are the premier materials for endosseous dental implants, mini-implants, and metal frameworks. 1) **Biocompatibility and Passivation**: When exposed to minute traces of oxygen or tissue fluid, titanium instantly forms a highly stable, adherent, self-healing **titanium dioxide (TiO2, mainly rutile)** surface film. This passive oxide layer prevents metallic ion leaching, explaining why titanium exhibits zero allergic cross-reactivity in patients sensitive to nickel or chromium, and allows direct structural and functional connection between living bone and the implant surface (**osseointegration**, pioneered by Pirmin Brånemark). 2) **Elastic Modulus and Stress Shielding**: CpTi has an elastic modulus of ~100–110 GPa (and Ti-6Al-4V around 110 GPa, though modified low-modulus titanium-niobium/zirconium alloys drop as low as 55 GPa). While still stiffer than human cortical bone (~15–20 GPa), titanium is significantly more flexible than Co-Cr or Ni-Cr (~200 GPa) or gold (~100 GPa), substantially reducing **stress-shielding** (bone resorption caused by rigid implants carrying 100% of the load and leaving surrounding bone unstressed). 3) **Casting/Processing Challenge**: Titanium has an extremely high melting point (~1668 °C) and is chemically reactive when molten, reacting vigorously with oxygen, nitrogen, carbon, and standard silica/gypsum investment moulds to form a brittle surface layer (alpha-case). Therefore, casting must be performed under high vacuum / inert argon arc conditions, or frameworks must be fabricated via CAD/CAM milling or selective laser melting (SLM) 3D printing.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Its rough machined surface alone: Roughness helps bone attachment but the oxide film is the basis of biocompatibility.
  • B. Its high nickel content: Commercially pure titanium contains no nickel.
  • C. It is stiffer than cobalt-chromium: Titanium is less stiff than cobalt-chromium, which is helpful but is not the reason for tolerance.
  • D. It slowly dissolves in tissue fluid: Titanium does not dissolve; its oxide film makes it inert.

Key point: State the passivating TiO2 surface film mechanism of titanium osseointegration, its allergy-free biocompatibility, and its lower elastic modulus (~100 GPa) compared to Co-Cr (~200 GPa).

Question 2 · Direct restorative materials, cements and adhesives · easy

An operative dentist in D.I. Khan places a non-bonded high-copper amalgam restoration in a lower left second molar (37). Because dental amalgam does not adhere chemically to enamel or dentine, a microscopic 10–20 µm interfacial gap initially exists at the cavosurface margin, causing mild cold sensitivity during the first week. Six months later, the cold sensitivity has completely disappeared and dye-penetration tests on similar aged restorations show a dramatic reduction in microleakage.

What accounts for this progressive reduction in marginal microleakage around an amalgam restoration over time?

  1. A. Hygroscopic expansion of the gamma phase
  2. B. Corrosion products plugging the gap
  3. C. Polymerisation between amalgam and collagen
  4. D. Enamel crystals growing into the amalgam
  5. E. Creep of enamel rods over the margin
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Corrosion products plugging the gap

Dental amalgam is unique among direct restorative materials in that its marginal seal actually *improves* as it ages in the oral cavity. When freshly condensed, amalgam forms only a mechanical adaptation to the cavity walls, leaving a microscopic 5–20 µm interfacial space that permits fluid and bacterial percolation (which is why cavity varnish or dentine bonding agent is applied initially). Over the first several months in the mouth, oral fluids penetrate this narrow crevice and set up a mild differential-aeration electrochemical cell. In low-copper amalgams the γ2 (Sn7-8Hg) phase corrodes rapidly, whereas in high-copper amalgams the η′ (Cu6Sn5) phase corrodes at a much slower, controlled rate. The resulting insoluble corrosion products—primarily tin oxides (SnO, SnO2), tin hydroxychloride [Sn4(OH)6Cl2] and copper oxides/chlorides—precipitate within the interfacial gap, mechanically plugging the space and sealing the margin against microleakage.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Hygroscopic expansion of the gamma phase: The silver-tin gamma phase is a dense intermetallic compound that does not absorb water or mucins to swell.
  • C. Polymerisation between amalgam and collagen: Dental amalgam is purely metallic and forms no covalent polymer bonds with collagen.
  • D. Enamel crystals growing into the amalgam: Mature acellular enamel cannot grow new hydroxyapatite crystals into a metallic restoration.
  • E. Creep of enamel rods over the margin: Enamel is a rigid, brittle mineral tissue that does not creep at mouth temperature.

Key point: Explain how the deposition of insoluble tin and copper corrosion products at the tooth-amalgam interface provides a beneficial self-sealing effect that reduces microleakage over time.

Question 3 · General properties of dental materials · easy

A 29-year-old man visits a dental clinic in Swabi complaining of a sharp, transient jolt of pain in his lower right second molar (47) whenever he drinks ice-cold water or hot green tea. The tooth was restored three days ago with a deep occlusal silver amalgam filling placed directly on a thin 0.4 mm floor of remaining dentine without an insulating base.

Which physical property of dental amalgam explains the rapid transmission of temperature changes to the pulp?

  1. A. High water sorption
  2. B. Low linear coefficient of thermal expansion
  3. C. High modulus of resilience
  4. D. High thermal conductivity
  5. E. Low specific heat capacity alone
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. High thermal conductivity

Thermal conductivity (κ) is the rate at which heat flows through a material of unit thickness per unit area per unit temperature gradient. Metals such as dental amalgam (~22.6 W/m·K) and gold alloys (~297 W/m·K) have delocalised free electrons that transfer kinetic thermal energy nearly forty times faster than human dentine (~0.57 W/m·K) or enamel (~0.92 W/m·K). When a deep cavity leaves less than 0.5–1.0 mm of remaining dentine thickness, an unlined metallic restoration transmits cold or hot stimuli almost instantly to the odontoblastic processes, causing hydrodynamic fluid shift and sharp transient pulpal pain. Placing a low-conductivity cement base such as glass ionomer or zinc oxide eugenol restores thermal insulation.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. High water sorption: Dental amalgam does not absorb water, and fluid sorption does not conduct thermal energy across a metal lattice.
  • B. Low linear coefficient of thermal expansion: Governs dimensional volume changes with temperature rather than the rate of heat transfer to the pulp.
  • C. High modulus of resilience: Measures elastic energy absorption under mechanical loading, unrelated to thermal transfer.
  • E. Low specific heat capacity alone: Specific heat alone does not drive heat flux unless combined with thermal conductivity in the thermal diffusivity equation.

Key point: Relate high thermal conductivity in metallic restorations to transient thermal sensitivity in deep cavities.

Question 4 · Impression, model, wax and casting materials · easy

In a prosthodontics laboratory in Gilgit, a technician uses reversible agar hydrocolloid to duplicate a refractory cast. He liquefies the agar gel into a fluid sol by heating it in boiling water at 100 °C, stores it at 65 °C, and tempers it at 45 °C. Even though 45 °C is far below the 100 °C melting point, the agar remains a fluid sol and does not solidify into a gel until it is cooled with circulating water to about 37–43 °C.

What term describes this large temperature difference between the liquefaction temperature (~100 °C) and gelation temperature (~37–43 °C) of agar?

  1. A. Syneresis
  2. B. Imbibition
  3. C. Hysteresis
  4. D. Thixotropy
  5. E. Retrogradation
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Hysteresis

Agar (reversible hydrocolloid) is a sulphuric acid ester of a linear galactan polysaccharide extracted from marine red algae (seaweed), mixed with ~85% water and borax (to increase gel strength) plus potassium sulphate (to counter the gypsum-retarding effect of borax). Its sol-to-gel conversion is purely physical, driven by secondary hydrogen bonding between polysaccharide micelle fibrils. Because breaking the established three-dimensional brush-heap network requires high thermal energy, the gel liquefies (gel → sol) only at 98–100 °C; however, once dispersed, the chains do not re-associate into a rigid gel (sol → gel) until cooled to 37–45 °C. This 60 °C lag—called thermal hysteresis—is clinically indispensable, allowing liquefied agar to be tempered to 45 °C and placed intraorally without burning the oral mucosa.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Syneresis: Refers to the exudation of a serous fluid droplet layer onto the surface of a set hydrocolloid gel accompanied by shrinkage.
  • B. Imbibition: Refers to the uptake of water and dimensional swelling when a hydrocolloid gel is immersed in water.
  • D. Thixotropy: Describes a mechanical shear-thinning viscosity change at constant temperature rather than a thermal sol-gel transition lag.
  • E. Retrogradation: Describes slow realignment of starch chains over days rather than the reversible thermal sol-gel cycle of galactose sulphate agar.

Key point: Define thermal hysteresis in agar reversible hydrocolloid and explain why it enables intraoral use without thermal injury.

Question 5 · Metals, polymers, ceramics and implant materials · easy

A dental metallurgy technician in Peshawar is organizing his laboratory burnout and casting equipment. He notes that gold-alloy casting rings are placed in a burnout furnace at 650–700 °C, whereas nickel-chromium and cobalt-chromium investment rings require higher burnout temperatures (850–950 °C) and a much hotter heat source (induction or multi-orifice gas-oxygen torch) to melt the alloy.

How do the melting ranges of high noble gold alloys compare with base metal alloys?

  1. A. Gold alloys melt lower
  2. B. Gold alloys melt higher (about 1400 °C)
  3. C. Both melt at about 1063 °C
  4. D. Base metal alloys melt below 900 °C
  5. E. Base metal alloys melt below 650 °C
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Gold alloys melt lower

The fusion (melting) temperature and liquidus-solidus range of dental casting alloys dictate laboratory equipment selection, investment type, and burnout protocols: 1) **High-Noble and Noble Gold Alloys**: Have relatively low melting ranges, typically between **870 °C and 1050 °C** (depending on gold, silver, copper and platinum/palladium ratios). Because of this moderate melting range, they can be easily melted using a standard compressed air-gas or multi-orifice gas-oxygen blowtorch and cast using centrifugal machines into gypsum-bonded investment rings burned out at 650–700 °C. 2) **Base Metal Alloys (Ni-Cr and Co-Cr)**: Have much higher melting ranges, typically between **1250 °C and 1350 °C**. Because conventional gypsum investments decompose above 700 °C to release sulphur and ruin the alloy, base metal castings require refractory **phosphate-bonded or silica-bonded investments**, high-heat burnout furnaces (850–950 °C), and high-energy **induction casting machines** or oxygen-acetylene torches to achieve complete liquid flow.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Gold alloys melt higher (about 1400 °C): Gold alloys melt lower than base metal alloys.
  • C. Both melt at about 1063 °C: 1063 °C is the melting point of pure gold, not of these alloys.
  • D. Base metal alloys melt below 900 °C: Base metal alloys need much higher temperatures.
  • E. Base metal alloys melt below 650 °C: This is lower than burnout temperature; base alloys melt far higher.

Key point: Compare the lower melting range of high-noble gold alloys (~900–1000 °C) with the higher melting range of base-metal Ni-Cr/Co-Cr alloys (~1250–1350 °C) and their laboratory equipment requirements.

Question 6 · Direct restorative materials, cements and adhesives · easy

During an operative dentistry practical in Gilgit, an old reusable screw-top amalgamator capsule cracks open during high-speed trituration, scattering small silver droplets of liquid elemental mercury across the linoleum floor and near a warm sterilizer. The clinic supervisor immediately evacuates the students from the bay, opens the windows for ventilation, and forbids the cleaner from using a household vacuum cleaner or broom.

What is the main route of occupational mercury poisoning in dental staff?

  1. A. Absorption through the conjunctiva
  2. B. Absorption through intact skin
  3. C. Swallowing set amalgam scrap
  4. D. Inhaling mercury vapour
  5. E. Inhaling silver oxide fumes
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. Inhaling mercury vapour

Mercury exists in three chemical forms with distinct toxicological profiles: 1) Elemental (metallic) mercury (Hg⁰), used in dentistry; it is poorly absorbed through the skin or gastrointestinal tract (<0.01%), but is volatile at room temperature (vapour pressure doubles for every 10 °C rise), and ~80% of inhaled colourless, odourless Hg⁰ vapour is rapidly absorbed across pulmonary alveoli into the bloodstream, crossing the blood-brain barrier before oxidation to Hg²⁺ to cause tremor (erethism), peripheral neuropathy and renal tubular damage. 2) Inorganic mercury salts (Hg⁺, Hg²⁺), which are nephrotoxic. 3) Organic mercury (methylmercury from contaminated seafood), which is >90% absorbed in the gut. Therefore, occupational mercury hygiene in dental clinics focuses strictly on preventing Hg⁰ vapour inhalation: using sealed single-use pre-proportioned capsules, high-volume evacuation with water spray when drilling old amalgam, storing scrap amalgam under water or photographic fixer (thiosulphate) in tightly sealed unbreakable jars, and never using a vacuum cleaner on spills (which heats and disperses Hg⁰ vapour through the exhaust). Spills are collected with sticky tape/syringes and dusted with sulphur or Ag-Sn alloy/zinc powder.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Absorption through the conjunctiva: The eye is not a significant route.
  • B. Absorption through intact skin: Little liquid mercury passes through intact skin.
  • C. Swallowing set amalgam scrap: Set amalgam and elemental mercury are poorly absorbed from the gut.
  • E. Inhaling silver oxide fumes: Silver oxide fumes are not the hazard; mercury vapour is.

Key point: Identify inhalation of elemental mercury vapour (Hg⁰) as the primary occupational hazard in dentistry and state the correct protocol for managing mercury spills and scrap.

Question 7 · General properties of dental materials · easy

During a bisque-bake try-in at a dental hospital in Abbottabad, a prosthodontist finds that a lithium disilicate crown for an upper left lateral incisor (22) has the correct reddish-brown hue and the right lightness, but looks too pale and washed out compared to the deep, rich saturation of the adjacent natural canine (23).

In the Munsell colour system, which dimension of colour needs to be increased to make the crown look more saturated?

  1. A. Hue
  2. B. Value
  3. C. Chroma
  4. D. Translucency parameter
  5. E. Metameric index
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Chroma

The Munsell colour order system defines colour in three cylindrical coordinates: Hue, Value and Chroma. Hue is the dominant wavelength family (red, yellow, green, blue); on the Vita Classical shade guide, A is reddish-brown, B is reddish-yellow, C is grey and D is reddish-grey. Value is the relative lightness or darkness (luminance) from 0 (pure black) to 10 (pure white); because human retinal rods are highly sensitive to lightness differences, Value is the most important dimension to match clinically. Chroma is the degree of saturation, intensity or concentration of the hue (for example, A1 has low chroma while A4 has high chroma within the same A hue family).

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Hue: Describes the dominant wavelength family of the colour (such as red, yellow or green), which is already correct for this crown.
  • B. Value: Describes the lightness or darkness of a colour on a scale from black (0) to white (10), which is the most critical factor in vitality but already matches here.
  • D. Translucency parameter: Measures the fraction of light transmitted through the ceramic rather than one of the three Munsell colour dimensions.
  • E. Metameric index: Quantifies the degree of colour shift between two illuminants rather than the saturation of a single hue.

Key point: Distinguish Hue, Value and Chroma in the Munsell colour system and relate them to dental shade guides.

Question 8 · Impression, model, wax and casting materials · easy

A dental house officer in Peshawar prepares to take a two-stage putty-wash polyvinyl siloxane impression of a crown preparation on a lower right first molar (46). Wearing powdered natural rubber latex examination gloves, she kneads the equal scoops of base and catalyst putty in her palms for 30 seconds before seating the tray. Five minutes later, the putty in contact with the tray and teeth remains soft, greasy and completely unpolymerised.

Which chemical interaction prevented the polyvinyl siloxane putty from setting?

  1. A. Chelation of the terminal silane hydride groups by ferric sulphate
  2. B. Glove powder hydrolysing the vinyl siloxane chains
  3. C. Premature consumption of stannous octoate by cutaneous sweat electrolytes
  4. D. Sulphur in latex poisoning the platinum catalyst
  5. E. Evaporation of the volatile ethyl alcohol crosslinker into the rubber gloves
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. Sulphur in latex poisoning the platinum catalyst

Polyvinyl siloxane (addition silicone) sets via a free-radical-free hydrosilylation addition reaction between a polymethylhydrosiloxane (silane hydride, Si-H) base polymer and a divinylpolydimethylsiloxane (vinyl, -CH=CH2) polymer, catalysed by a platinum salt—specifically chloroplatinic acid (H2PtCl6). Natural rubber latex gloves are vulcanised using sulphur and zinc dithiocarbamate accelerators. Even brief contact between the unset putty (or even touching the prepared tooth or gingival retraction cord with latex gloves prior to impressioning) transfers trace sulphur compounds that bind irreversibly to the platinum atom, poisoning the catalyst and leaving the silicone permanently unset and tacky. Nitrile, vinyl or polyethylene gloves must always be used when handling addition silicones.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Chelation of the terminal silane hydride groups by ferric sulphate: Ferric sulphate can inhibit setting if left unwashed in the sulcus, but here the putty was kneaded directly in powdered latex gloves.
  • B. Glove powder hydrolysing the vinyl siloxane chains: Cornstarch is an inert carbohydrate absorbable dusting powder that does not hydrolyse covalent silicon-oxygen or vinyl bonds.
  • C. Premature consumption of stannous octoate by cutaneous sweat electrolytes: Stannous octoate is the catalyst for condensation silicones, whereas polyvinyl siloxane uses a chloroplatinic acid catalyst.
  • E. Evaporation of the volatile ethyl alcohol crosslinker into the rubber gloves: Polyvinyl siloxane sets by addition hydrosilylation without any ethyl alcohol crosslinker or volatile by-product.

Key point: Explain how sulphur compounds in natural rubber latex gloves poison the chloroplatinic acid catalyst in polyvinyl siloxane impressions.

Question 9 · Metals, polymers, ceramics and implant materials · easy

A dental porcelain manufacturer in Islamabad produces feldspathic porcelain powders for layering onto metal-ceramic copings. The raw formulation contains potassium feldspar (K2O·Al2O3·6SiO2), quartz, and kaolin. When fired at high temperature (900–1000 °C), the feldspar melts into an amorphous glassy matrix while precipitating millions of tiny tetragonal leucite (KAlSi2O6) crystals throughout the microstructure.

What is the main function of leucite crystals in feldspathic porcelain for metal-ceramic crowns?

  1. A. To make it etchable with phosphoric acid
  2. B. To lower its firing temperature
  3. C. To make it more translucent
  4. D. To bond chemically to the metal oxides
  5. E. Raise its expansion to match the metal
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Raise its expansion to match the metal

Feldspathic dental porcelains are traditional veneering ceramics used on metal copings (PFM) or refractory dies. 1) **Composition**: Composed of three main minerals—potash or soda **feldspar** (flux and glass former), **quartz** (crystalline silica refractory skeleton), and **kaolin** (clay binder for handling in the green state, though largely burned out or reduced in modern porcelains). 2) **Leucite Microstructure**: During high-temperature firing, incongruent melting of potassium feldspar precipitates fine tetragonal **leucite (KAlSi2O6)** crystals suspended inside the amorphous potassium-aluminosilicate glass matrix. Leucite serves two vital roles: first, its exceptionally high thermal expansion coefficient (~25 × 10⁻⁶ /°C) raises the overall CTE of the brittle porcelain matrix (~13–14 × 10⁻⁶ /°C) so it matches the metal coping without crazing; second, the mismatch in thermal contraction between leucite and the surrounding glass creates tiny tangential compressive stresses around each crystal that deflect and arrest propagating microcracks (transformation toughening / stress-field crack deflection), increasing flexural strength.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. To make it etchable with phosphoric acid: Porcelain is etched with hydrofluoric acid; leucite is not added for this.
  • B. To lower its firing temperature: Fluxes such as potassium and sodium oxides lower firing temperature.
  • C. To make it more translucent: Crystals reduce translucency rather than increase it.
  • D. To bond chemically to the metal oxides: Bonding to the metal depends on oxides at the interface, not leucite.

Key point: State the role of precipitated leucite crystals in feldspathic porcelain in raising the thermal expansion coefficient (CTE) and arresting microcracks.

Question 10 · Direct restorative materials, cements and adhesives · easy

During a dental materials lecture in Peshawar, a professor explains that the base monomer Bowen resin (Bis-GMA, molecular weight 512 g/mol) is a honey-like, extremely viscous liquid at room temperature because of hydrogen bonding between its hydroxyl (-OH) groups and the stiffness of its two aromatic phenyl rings. In its pure state, it cannot be blended with 75 wt% glass filler particles.

Why do manufacturers blend TEGDMA with Bis-GMA?

  1. A. To act as the blue-light photoinitiator
  2. B. To thin it so more filler fits
  3. C. To bond the matrix to the glass filler
  4. D. To scavenge radicals during storage
  5. E. To make the composite radiopaque
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. To thin it so more filler fits

The organic polymer matrix of most conventional dental composite resins is based on aromatic or aliphatic dimethacrylate monomers—principally Bis-GMA (bisphenol-A glycidyl methacrylate, synthesized by Rafael Bowen in 1962) and/or UDMA (urethane dimethacrylate). Because Bis-GMA has a high molecular weight (512 g/mol), two rigid benzene rings, and two pendant hydroxyl (-OH) groups that form strong intermolecular hydrogen bonds, its viscosity is extremely high (~1,200 Pa·s), making it impossible to mix with inorganic fillers. To achieve a workable paste viscosity, a low-viscosity, flexible aliphatic dimethacrylate diluent monomer—most commonly TEGDMA (triethylene glycol dimethacrylate, molecular weight 286 g/mol, viscosity ~0.01 Pa·s)—is blended in at 25–50% of the matrix. However, because smaller TEGDMA molecules pack more reactive carbon-carbon double bonds (C=C) per unit volume, increasing the TEGDMA ratio directly increases volumetric polymerization shrinkage and water sorption.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. To act as the blue-light photoinitiator: Camphorquinone, not TEGDMA, is the photoinitiator.
  • C. To bond the matrix to the glass filler: Silane, not TEGDMA, couples filler to resin.
  • D. To scavenge radicals during storage: Inhibitors such as BHT do this.
  • E. To make the composite radiopaque: Radiopacity comes from heavy-metal glass fillers.

Key point: Explain why TEGDMA is added as a viscosity-reducing diluent to Bis-GMA in composite resins and why excess TEGDMA increases polymerization shrinkage.
