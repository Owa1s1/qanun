ENT MCQs for MBBS

Question 1 · Otorhinolaryngology (ENT) · easy

A 19-year-old man has had pain and itching in his right ear for 3 days after swimming every day in a canal near his village. Pulling on the pinna and pressing on the tragus are very painful. The ear canal is red and swollen with some moist debris, but the eardrum, seen after gentle cleaning, is intact. He has no fever and is not diabetic.

What is the most appropriate treatment?

  1. A. Antibiotic-steroid ear drops
  2. B. Oral amoxicillin alone
  3. C. Syringing the ear canal daily
  4. D. Grommet insertion
  5. E. Incision of the canal
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Antibiotic-steroid ear drops

Acute otitis externa is inflammation of the ear canal skin, usually bacterial (Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Staphylococcus aureus are common), triggered by water exposure (swimmer's ear), scratching, cotton buds and skin conditions. Pain on moving the pinna or pressing the tragus distinguishes it from middle ear infection. Treatment is careful cleaning of debris (aural toilet) and topical drops combining an antibiotic and a steroid, for about 7 days; if the canal is too swollen for drops to enter, a wick is inserted first. Keeping water out of the ear helps healing. Oral antibiotics are used only if infection spreads beyond the canal or in high-risk patients such as diabetics.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Oral amoxicillin alone: Oral amoxicillin reaches the canal skin poorly and is reserved for spreading infection, which he does not have.
  • C. Syringing the ear canal daily: Syringing a red, swollen, painful canal irritates it further and can drive debris against the drum.
  • D. Grommet insertion: Grommets drain fluid from behind the drum, and his drum is intact with a normal middle ear.
  • E. Incision of the canal: There is no abscess to incise; the canal is diffusely inflamed after repeated canal water exposure.

Key point: Acute otitis externa is treated with aural toilet and topical antibiotic-steroid drops, with a wick if the canal is swollen.

Question 2 · Otorhinolaryngology (ENT) · easy

A 3-year-old girl has had a foul-smelling, greenish discharge from her left nostril only for 3 weeks, sometimes blood-stained. Antibiotic syrup from a local doctor helped briefly. She is otherwise well, with no fever. The right nostril is normal. Her mother noticed a strong smell when she leans close to her.

What is the most likely cause?

  1. A. Allergic rhinitis
  2. B. Adenoid enlargement
  3. C. Nasopharyngeal carcinoma
  4. D. A nasal foreign body
  5. E. Viral cold
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. A nasal foreign body

A one-sided, foul-smelling, purulent and sometimes blood-stained nasal discharge in a young child is a nasal foreign body until proven otherwise: beads, seeds, paper, foam or small toys pushed in and forgotten. Organic objects cause more inflammation and infection. Removal is done with a hook, suction or forceps under good light, or the "parent's kiss" (blowing into the mouth while blocking the other nostril), and under general anaesthesia if needed. Button batteries are an emergency because they cause rapid burns and septal perforation, and magnets on both sides of the septum can do the same. Long-standing foreign bodies may calcify into a rhinolith.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Allergic rhinitis: Allergic rhinitis gives clear watery discharge from both nostrils with sneezing and itch.
  • B. Adenoid enlargement: Large adenoids block and discharge from both sides and would not cause a foul smell from one nostril.
  • C. Nasopharyngeal carcinoma: Nasopharyngeal carcinoma is extremely rare at three years and would not present with a smell from one nostril.
  • E. Viral cold: A viral cold affects both sides, lasts about a week and does not smell foul.

Key point: One-sided foul nasal discharge in a young child suggests a nasal foreign body; button batteries need urgent removal.

Question 3 · Otorhinolaryngology (ENT) · medium

A 28-year-old woman has had a foul-smelling discharge from her left ear for 2 years. There is a pars flaccida perforation filled with keratin debris. CT of the temporal bone shows a soft tissue mass in the attic eroding the scutum and part of the incus, extending into the mastoid antrum. She asks whether drops alone can cure it, because her sister's ear dried up with drops.

What is the most appropriate treatment?

  1. A. Long-term antibiotic ear drops
  2. B. Mastoid surgery
  3. C. Grommet insertion
  4. D. Hearing aid only
  5. E. Oral steroids
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Mastoid surgery

Cholesteatoma does not resolve with drops: antibiotics may reduce the discharge for a while, but the keratin sac keeps growing and eroding bone. The treatment is surgical removal, usually by mastoidectomy. In canal wall up (combined approach) surgery, the posterior canal wall is kept, giving a normal-looking ear, but residual or recurrent disease is more likely and a second-look operation or imaging is often needed. In canal wall down (modified radical) mastoidectomy, the canal wall is removed to create an open cavity, giving a lower recurrence rate but a cavity that needs regular cleaning. Hearing can be rebuilt by ossiculoplasty. Lifelong follow-up is needed.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Long-term antibiotic ear drops: Drops may dry the discharge for a while but leave the keratin sac eroding bone in the attic and mastoid.
  • C. Grommet insertion: A grommet ventilates the middle ear and does nothing about a mass eroding the ossicles.
  • D. Hearing aid only: A hearing aid leaves the cholesteatoma to go on eroding towards the facial nerve and inner ear.
  • E. Oral steroids: Steroids do not shrink keratin, and the erosion seen on her CT will continue.

Key point: Cholesteatoma requires surgical removal by mastoidectomy; drops only control the discharge.

Question 4 · Otorhinolaryngology (ENT) · medium

A 66-year-old woman has had severe pain in her left ear for 3 days. Today the left side of her face is completely weak, including the forehead, and she feels the room spinning. There are small vesicles on the left ear canal and the back of the pinna. Audiometry shows a new left sensorineural hearing loss. She is not immunosuppressed.

What is the most appropriate treatment?

  1. A. Steroids
  2. B. Antiviral and steroid
  3. C. Antibiotic ear drops
  4. D. Grommet insertion
  5. E. Observation
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Antiviral and steroid

Ramsay Hunt syndrome (herpes zoster oticus) is reactivation of varicella-zoster virus in the geniculate ganglion of the facial nerve. It causes severe ear pain, a vesicular rash in the ear canal, pinna or palate, and a lower motor neuron facial palsy; spread to the nearby vestibulocochlear nerve causes hearing loss, tinnitus and vertigo. The facial palsy is often more severe and recovers less completely than Bell palsy. Treatment is started early with an oral antiviral (aciclovir or valaciclovir) together with prednisolone, plus eye protection, analgesia and antiemetics for vertigo. Patients are followed up with audiometry and facial nerve assessment.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Steroids: Steroids alone leave the varicella-zoster reactivation untreated, and the outlook is worse than in Bell palsy.
  • C. Antibiotic ear drops: The vesicles and sensorineural loss are viral, so antibacterial drops treat nothing.
  • D. Grommet insertion: A grommet drains middle ear fluid and plays no part in a viral ganglionitis.
  • E. Observation: Untreated, recovery of facial function in this condition is considerably worse than in Bell palsy.

Key point: Ramsay Hunt syndrome (VZV in the geniculate ganglion) causes facial palsy with ear vesicles; treat with antivirals plus steroids.

Question 5 · Otorhinolaryngology (ENT) · medium

A 3-year-old girl who has not received any vaccines became unwell 6 hours ago with a high fever of 40°C. She is sitting forward with her chin up, drooling, and looks toxic and frightened. Her voice is muffled and she has soft inspiratory stridor but no barking cough. She refuses to lie down. Her parents ask the doctor to look in her throat with a spatula to check her tonsils.

What is the most appropriate action?

  1. A. Examine the throat with a spatula now
  2. B. Secure her airway in theatre
  3. C. Lie her flat for a chest X-ray
  4. D. Take blood samples first
  5. E. Give oral dexamethasone and send home
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Secure her airway in theatre

Acute epiglottitis is a rapidly progressing cellulitis of the epiglottis and surrounding supraglottic tissues that can completely block the airway. It was classically caused by Haemophilus influenzae type b in unvaccinated children, and is now rare where Hib vaccine is used. Typical features are sudden high fever, a toxic appearance, drooling, difficulty swallowing, a muffled voice, soft stridor without a barking cough, and a "tripod" position leaning forward. The child must be kept calm, sitting up with a parent, and must not be examined with a spatula, laid flat or distressed by procedures. The airway is secured by a senior anaesthetist in theatre, with an ENT surgeon ready for an emergency tracheostomy; antibiotics are then given intravenously.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Examine the throat with a spatula now: Pressing a spatula on the tongue of an unimmunised, drooling, toxic child can trigger complete obstruction.
  • C. Lie her flat for a chest X-ray: Lying her flat removes the position she has adopted to keep her airway open and may close it.
  • D. Take blood samples first: A needle and the crying it causes can precipitate obstruction, so blood is taken once the airway is safe.
  • E. Give oral dexamethasone and send home: She has no barking cough and is drooling and toxic, so this is not croup and she must not go home.

Key point: In suspected epiglottitis, do not examine the throat or upset the child; secure the airway in theatre with senior staff.

Question 6 · Otorhinolaryngology (ENT) · easy

A 40-year-old man has had reduced hearing in his left ear for 3 months. A 512 Hz tuning fork is heard better when it is placed on the bone behind the left ear than when it is held next to the left ear canal. On the right side, it is heard louder next to the ear canal. When the fork is placed on the middle of his forehead, the sound is heard louder in the left ear.

What do these findings show?

  1. A. Left sensorineural hearing loss
  2. B. Right conductive hearing loss
  3. C. Normal hearing
  4. D. Right sensorineural hearing loss
  5. E. Left conductive hearing loss
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Left conductive hearing loss

In the Rinne test, a 512 Hz fork is compared by air conduction (beside the ear canal) and bone conduction (on the mastoid). Normally air conduction is louder (Rinne positive). If bone conduction is louder (Rinne negative), there is a conductive hearing loss in that ear, usually of 15-20 dB or more. In the Weber test, the fork is placed in the midline of the forehead or vertex: in a conductive loss the sound is heard in the affected ear (the ear is shielded from room noise and bone conduction is relatively enhanced), whereas in a sensorineural loss it is heard in the better ear. A negative Rinne and Weber lateralising to the same side mean a conductive loss on that side, such as wax, effusion, perforation or otosclerosis.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Left sensorineural hearing loss: In a left sensorineural loss the Weber would move to the right ear, but his Weber goes to the left.
  • B. Right conductive hearing loss: A right conductive loss would give a negative Rinne on the right, and on the right air conduction is louder.
  • C. Normal hearing: Weber lateralising to the left and a negative Rinne on that side are both abnormal findings.
  • D. Right sensorineural hearing loss: A right sensorineural loss would send the Weber to the left but leave the left Rinne positive, and his left Rinne is negative.

Key point: Rinne negative and Weber lateralising to the same ear indicate a conductive hearing loss in that ear.

Question 7 · Otorhinolaryngology (ENT) · easy

A 9-year-old boy had mumps a month ago, and his mother thinks he no longer hears with his left ear. In the Rinne test, air conduction is louder than bone conduction on both sides. In the Weber test with a 512 Hz fork on his forehead, he hears the sound louder in his right ear. Both eardrums are normal.

What do these findings show?

  1. A. Left conductive hearing loss
  2. B. Left sensorineural hearing loss
  3. C. Right conductive hearing loss
  4. D. Right sensorineural hearing loss
  5. E. Normal hearing in both ears
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Left sensorineural hearing loss

In a sensorineural hearing loss, air and bone conduction are reduced equally, so air conduction remains louder and the Rinne test stays positive. In the Weber test, the sound is heard in the better-hearing cochlea, that is, away from the affected ear. So a positive Rinne on both sides with Weber lateralised to the right means a left sensorineural loss. Mumps is a classic cause of sudden, usually one-sided, severe sensorineural deafness in children, from viral damage to the cochlea; it is often noticed late because the other ear hears normally. Audiometry confirms the type and degree of loss, and MMR vaccination prevents it.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Left conductive hearing loss: A left conductive loss would make bone conduction louder than air on the left, but his Rinne is positive on both sides.
  • C. Right conductive hearing loss: A right conductive loss would give a negative Rinne on the right, and air is louder than bone there.
  • D. Right sensorineural hearing loss: A right sensorineural loss would send the Weber to the left, and he hears it louder on the right.
  • E. Normal hearing in both ears: Normal hearing would leave the Weber central, but he hears it clearly louder in the right ear.

Key point: A positive Rinne with Weber lateralising away from the affected ear indicates a sensorineural loss in that ear.

Question 8 · Otorhinolaryngology (ENT) · easy

A 4-year-old boy pushed a dried chickpea into his left ear 2 hours ago while playing. He is upset but has no pain at rest, and hearing in that ear is slightly reduced. The chickpea is visible deep in the ear canal. A health worker prepares to wash it out with a large syringe of warm water. The boy lets the doctor look in his ear.

What should be avoided?

  1. A. Removing it with a small hook
  2. B. Syringing with water
  3. C. Removal under general anaesthetic if needed
  4. D. Gentle suction
  5. E. Using a microscope for removal
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Syringing with water

Foreign bodies in the ear are common in children. The method of removal depends on the object. Vegetable matter such as seeds, beans and chickpeas absorbs water and swells, so syringing or water drops can make it larger, more painful and harder to remove, and can cause infection. These are removed dry, with a blunt hook passed behind the object, suction or fine forceps, preferably under a microscope, and under general anaesthesia if the child cannot keep still. Smooth round objects should not be grasped with forceps, which push them deeper. Live insects are first killed with oil or alcohol drops. Button batteries are an emergency because they cause rapid tissue burns.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Removing it with a small hook: A fine hook passed beyond a firm, visible chickpea under direct vision is a standard way to remove it.
  • C. Removal under general anaesthetic if needed: A general anaesthetic is the right fallback if a frightened four-year-old will not keep still.
  • D. Gentle suction: Suction lifts the chickpea out without wetting it, so it does not swell.
  • E. Using a microscope for removal: A microscope gives light and magnification, which makes removal safer and less likely to damage the drum.

Key point: Vegetable foreign bodies in the ear must not be syringed because they swell; remove them dry with a hook or suction.

Question 9 · Otorhinolaryngology (ENT) · easy

A 20-month-old boy has had a runny nose for 3 days and now has a fever of 39°C, is irritable and keeps pulling at his right ear. He woke crying at night. The right eardrum is red and bulging, with loss of the normal landmarks; the left drum is normal. There is no discharge. He has no allergies and has not had antibiotics this year.

If an antibiotic is given, which is the first-line choice?

  1. A. Ciprofloxacin
  2. B. Gentamicin drops
  3. C. Metronidazole
  4. D. Amoxicillin
  5. E. Doxycycline
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. Amoxicillin

Acute otitis media is acute infection of the middle ear, very common in young children because their Eustachian tubes are short, horizontal and easily blocked during colds. It often follows a viral upper respiratory infection, with bacteria such as Streptococcus pneumoniae, Haemophilus influenzae and Moraxella catarrhalis. A red, bulging drum with loss of landmarks, fever and ear pain are typical. Many cases settle without antibiotics, so pain relief and observation are suitable for many children. When antibiotics are needed, amoxicillin for 5-7 days is first-line, with a macrolide for penicillin allergy and amoxicillin with clavulanate if there is no response.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Ciprofloxacin: Ciprofloxacin does not reliably cover pneumococcus and is avoided by mouth in young children.
  • B. Gentamicin drops: Gentamicin drops are for a discharging ear with a perforation and can damage the inner ear; his drum is intact.
  • C. Metronidazole: Metronidazole covers anaerobes, which are not the usual organisms in acute otitis media.
  • E. Doxycycline: Doxycycline stains developing teeth and is avoided under eight years.

Key point: Acute otitis media in children: analgesia, with amoxicillin as the first-line antibiotic when one is needed.

Question 10 · Otorhinolaryngology (ENT) · easy

A 6-year-old girl had a cold followed by severe pain in her left ear and fever for 2 days, crying through the night. This morning her pillow was stained with yellow pus from the ear, and her pain has suddenly eased. The ear canal contains pus coming through a small hole in the front lower part of the drum, which is red. Her fever has fallen.

Why did her pain improve?

  1. A. The infection spread to the brain
  2. B. The Eustachian tube closed
  3. C. The eardrum perforated
  4. D. The ossicles were destroyed
  5. E. The infection moved to the ear canal
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. The eardrum perforated

In acute otitis media, pus builds up in the closed middle ear, so the drum bulges and pain becomes severe (stage of suppuration). If pressure keeps rising, the drum ruptures, usually in its pars tensa, and pus drains into the ear canal. Pain and fever then ease quickly. Most such perforations are small and heal within a few weeks once the infection settles. Treatment includes oral antibiotics (and sometimes topical quinolone drops), keeping water out of the ear and review to make sure the drum heals and hearing returns. A perforation that persists beyond about 3 months may need repair.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. The infection spread to the brain: Intracranial spread would bring headache, vomiting and drowsiness with a rising fever, and she is improving.
  • B. The Eustachian tube closed: A blocked Eustachian tube traps more pus in the middle ear and would make the pain worse, not better.
  • D. The ossicles were destroyed: The ossicles may erode in chronic disease, but that causes hearing loss over months rather than sudden relief of pain.
  • E. The infection moved to the ear canal: The pus is coming through a hole in the drum from the middle ear, so the infection has not simply moved outwards.

Key point: In acute otitis media, spontaneous perforation of the drum releases pus and suddenly relieves pain.
