Question 1 · Bacteriology · easy

A 68-year-old man in Rawalpindi has had fever, shaking chills and right-sided chest pain on breathing for 2 days, coughing up rust-coloured sputum. Chest X-ray shows consolidation of the right lower lobe. Sputum Gram stain shows many neutrophils and lancet-shaped gram-positive diplococci. On blood agar the organism forms small colonies with a green zone of partial haemolysis, some with a sunken centre. The laboratory must now tell it apart from viridans streptococci from the mouth.

Which test separates these two groups?

  1. A. Bacitracin disc
  2. B. CAMP test
  3. C. Coagulase test
  4. D. Growth in 6.5% salt
  5. E. Bile solubility
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Bile solubility

The picture is lobar pneumonia from Streptococcus pneumoniae, the commonest bacterial cause of community-acquired pneumonia. Pneumococci and viridans streptococci are both alpha-haemolytic (partial, green haemolysis from hydrogen peroxide oxidising haemoglobin). Pneumococci are separated by bile solubility (bile salts activate their autolysin, so colonies dissolve) and optochin sensitivity (a zone of inhibition around an optochin disc); viridans streptococci are bile insoluble and optochin resistant. Autolysis also gives older pneumococcal colonies a sunken "draughtsman" centre. Other tests: the quellung reaction (capsule swelling with type-specific antiserum) and urinary pneumococcal antigen. Treatment of community pneumonia follows severity, with amoxicillin or a beta-lactam plus a macrolide in more severe cases.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Bacitracin disc: Bacitracin sorts beta-haemolytic streptococci, but both organisms in question here give partial green haemolysis.
  • B. CAMP test: The CAMP test identifies group B streptococcus, which is beta-haemolytic and not confused with mouth flora.
  • C. Coagulase test: Coagulase sorts staphylococci, which grow in clusters; his sputum showed lancet-shaped diplococci.
  • D. Growth in 6.5% salt: Salt tolerance identifies enterococci, which are non-haemolytic gut organisms rather than alpha-haemolytic mouth flora.

Key point: Streptococcus pneumoniae is alpha-haemolytic, bile soluble and optochin sensitive; viridans streptococci are bile insoluble and optochin resistant.

Question 2 · Mycology · medium

A 3-week-old breastfed baby boy in Multan has white curd-like plaques on the tongue, palate and inside the cheeks for 5 days; the plaques scrape off leaving a red raw surface, and he is feeding more slowly. He was born at term, is gaining weight, and had no antibiotics. His mother has sore, cracked nipples. Microscopy of a plaque shows budding yeasts with pseudohyphae.

What is the most appropriate treatment?

  1. A. Intravenous amphotericin B
  2. B. Oral co-trimoxazole
  3. C. Oral nystatin suspension
  4. D. Topical steroid gel
  5. E. No treatment, as it clears by itself
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Oral nystatin suspension

Oral candidiasis (thrush) in a young baby is common and usually caused by Candida albicans acquired from the birth canal or skin. It appears as white adherent plaques that can be scraped off to leave a red, sometimes bleeding, surface, which separates it from milk residue; feeding may be painful. Treatment is topical: oral nystatin suspension or miconazole gel for 7-14 days, with treatment of the mother's nipples if she is breastfeeding, since infection passes back and forth. Feeding bottles and teats should be sterilised. Thrush in older children and adults is not normal and should prompt a search for a cause: antibiotics, inhaled or systemic corticosteroids, dentures, diabetes, iron or vitamin deficiency, malignancy, and HIV infection, in which oral and especially oesophageal candidiasis is an indicator condition. Oesophageal disease (pain on swallowing) needs systemic treatment with oral fluconazole, not topical therapy.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Intravenous amphotericin B: Intravenous amphotericin is for invasive disease; his plaques are confined to the mouth and he is gaining weight.
  • B. Oral co-trimoxazole: Co-trimoxazole is antibacterial and would not clear the budding yeasts and pseudohyphae in his plaque.
  • D. Topical steroid gel: A steroid gel would suppress local defences and let the yeast spread further across his tongue and palate.
  • E. No treatment, as it clears by itself: Leaving it untreated keeps him feeding slowly and passing infection back and forth with his mother's cracked nipples.

Key point: Oral thrush in babies is treated with topical nystatin or miconazole; thrush in older patients prompts a search for diabetes, steroids or HIV.

Question 3 · General microbiology · easy

In a haemodialysis unit in Multan, a bloodline disconnects and about 200 mL of blood spills onto the floor beside a 50-year-old patient. He is positive for hepatitis B surface antigen and hepatitis B e antigen. The nurse in charge puts on gloves and an apron, covers the spill with paper towels, and asks for a disinfectant to pour over it before cleaning it up.

Which disinfectant is most suitable?

  1. A. Chlorhexidine
  2. B. Sodium hypochlorite
  3. C. Cetrimide
  4. D. Ethyl alcohol 70%
  5. E. Hydrogen peroxide 3%
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Sodium hypochlorite

Hepatitis B is the most infectious of the blood-borne viruses (HBeAg-positive blood can carry over 100 million virus particles per mL) and can survive on surfaces for at least a week. Blood spills are covered with absorbent material and then treated with a chlorine-releasing agent such as sodium hypochlorite (household bleach), usually at about 10,000 ppm available chlorine (1%) for blood spills, left for the recommended contact time, then wiped up and the area cleaned. Chlorine compounds act quickly against bacteria, viruses (including HBV, HCV and HIV) and, at high strength, spores; they are cheap but corrode metals and are partly inactivated by organic matter, which is why a strong solution is used. Antiseptics such as chlorhexidine and cetrimide are meant for skin and are weak against many viruses. The unit should also check that staff are vaccinated against hepatitis B and have protective anti-HBs levels.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Chlorhexidine: Chlorhexidine is for skin preparation and is weak against blood-borne viruses in the 200 mL spilt on the floor.
  • C. Cetrimide: Quaternary ammonium; weak and inactivated by organic matter.
  • D. Ethyl alcohol 70%: Alcohol evaporates before it can act on so large a spill and fixes the protein in the blood.
  • E. Hydrogen peroxide 3%: Hydrogen peroxide is broken down by catalase in the blood itself, so it cannot be relied on for this spill.

Key point: Blood spills, especially from hepatitis B patients, are treated with a strong chlorine-releasing disinfectant such as sodium hypochlorite.

Question 4 · Parasitology · medium

A 24-year-old soldier was treated in Lahore for vivax malaria with a 3-day course of artemether-lumefantrine and recovered fully. He was not given any further medicine. Three months later, without leaving the city or any new mosquito exposure reported, he has fever with chills again, and a blood film again shows vivax parasites. His G6PD test result is now normal.

Which drug was missing from his first treatment?

  1. A. Chloroquine
  2. B. Doxycycline
  3. C. Artesunate
  4. D. Primaquine
  5. E. Mefloquine
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. Primaquine

Plasmodium vivax and Plasmodium ovale form hypnozoites, dormant forms that persist in hepatocytes after the blood infection is cured. Weeks to months later they activate and release merozoites, causing a true relapse without any new mosquito bite, which is what has happened here. Blood-stage drugs, including chloroquine and the artemisinin combinations, do not touch hypnozoites, so radical cure requires an 8-aminoquinoline: primaquine daily for 14 days, or a single dose of tafenoquine where available. Both cause haemolysis in glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase deficiency, so G6PD status should be checked before prescribing, and they are avoided in pregnancy and in infants, where weekly chloroquine prophylaxis may be used instead until they can be treated. Recrudescence is a different phenomenon: persistence of blood-stage parasites after inadequate treatment or resistance, seen with falciparum, which has no hypnozoites. Reinfection is a third possibility, excluded here by the absence of new exposure.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Chloroquine: Chloroquine clears parasites from blood, as his first treatment already did; the dormant liver forms would still wake.
  • B. Doxycycline: Doxycycline prevents infection while taken but does not clear hypnozoites, so his relapse would still occur.
  • C. Artesunate: Artesunate acts on blood stages, which is why he recovered fully the first time, yet the parasite returned.
  • E. Mefloquine: Mefloquine also acts only on blood forms and would leave the liver reservoir behind.

Key point: Vivax and ovale malaria relapse from liver hypnozoites, so radical cure needs primaquine (or tafenoquine) after checking G6PD status.

Question 5 · Virology · easy

A 7-year-old girl in Multan has had a mild fever and runny nose for 3 days, and now has bright red cheeks that look as if she has been slapped, with pallor around the mouth. A day later a lacy, net-like pink rash appears on her arms and thighs. She feels well, is eating normally, and several classmates have had the same rash.

What advice should be given about her returning to school?

  1. A. She may return now
  2. B. Keep her home for 7 more days
  3. C. Keep her home until the rash fades
  4. D. Keep her home for 48 hours
  5. E. Keep her home until antibody tests return
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. She may return now

This is erythema infectiosum (fifth disease, slapped-cheek disease) caused by parvovirus B19, a small naked single-stranded DNA virus spread by respiratory droplets. Children have a mild prodrome, during which they are infectious, then develop the bright red "slapped cheek" appearance followed by a reticular, lacy rash on the limbs that may come and go for weeks with heat or sunlight. By the time the rash appears, the immune response has cleared viraemia and the child is no longer infectious, so exclusion from school is unnecessary. In adults, especially women, the main feature is often symmetrical joint pain rather than rash. The virus infects red cell precursors through the P antigen and halts erythropoiesis for a few days, which matters in three situations: aplastic crisis in people with chronic haemolysis such as sickle cell disease and thalassaemia, chronic anaemia in the immunosuppressed, and fetal anaemia with hydrops fetalis when a woman is infected in pregnancy.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Keep her home for 7 more days: Keeping her home another week serves no purpose, as she stopped shedding virus before the rash appeared.
  • C. Keep her home until the rash fades: Her lacy rash may come and go for weeks with heat and sunlight, so this would keep her away needlessly.
  • D. Keep her home for 48 hours: No 48-hour rule applies to this illness: by the time her cheeks turned red she was no longer shedding virus.
  • E. Keep her home until antibody tests return: Antibody testing is unnecessary when the slapped-cheek appearance and the nursery outbreak make the diagnosis clear.

Key point: Parvovirus B19 causes slapped-cheek disease; the child is infectious before the rash, so no school exclusion is needed once it appears.

Question 6 · Immunology · easy

A 22-year-old male from Gilgit-Baltistan presents with acute onset pain, redness, and swelling in his right knee. He reports having had urethritis two weeks ago. Examination confirms asymmetric oligoarthritis. He also mentions recent episodes of painful eye redness. Family history is negative for autoimmune diseases.

Which HLA allele is most strongly associated with this clinical syndrome?

  1. A. HLA-DR4
  2. B. HLA-DQ2
  3. C. HLA-B27
  4. D. HLA-Cw6
  5. E. HLA-B51
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. HLA-B27

Reactive arthritis (formerly Reiter’s syndrome) is a seronegative spondyloarthropathy characterised by the triad of arthritis, urethritis, and conjunctivitis/uveitis. It often follows GI or GU infections. HLA-B27 is present in >60% of cases and predisposes to this condition.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. HLA-DR4: HLA-DR4 is linked with rheumatoid arthritis, which gives symmetrical small-joint polyarthritis, not a single swollen knee two weeks after urethritis.
  • B. HLA-DQ2: HLA-DQ2 is linked with coeliac disease, which presents with diarrhoea, weight loss and anaemia rather than arthritis following urethritis.
  • D. HLA-Cw6: HLA-Cw6 is linked with psoriasis; psoriatic arthritis would come with skin plaques or nail pitting, not recent urethritis and eye inflammation.
  • E. HLA-B51: Associated with Behçet’s disease, which causes oral/genital ulcers and uveitis but not typically this arthritis pattern.

Key point: Reactive arthritis (post-urethritis/GI) + Oligoarthritis = HLA-B27 association.

Question 7 · Bacteriology · easy

A 38-year-old woman with rheumatic mitral valve disease from Swat had a molar tooth removed 7 weeks ago without antibiotic cover. Since then she has had low-grade fever, night sweats, tiredness and weight loss. She has painless red spots on her palms, splinter haemorrhages and a louder mitral regurgitant murmur. Three blood cultures grow alpha-haemolytic streptococci that are optochin resistant and bile insoluble.

Which property helps these organisms stick to her damaged valve?

  1. A. Dextran production
  2. B. Coagulase production
  3. C. Protein A
  4. D. Exotoxin A
  5. E. Urease production
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Dextran production

This is subacute infective endocarditis from viridans streptococci (such as S. sanguinis, S. mutans and S. mitis), normal mouth flora that enter the blood during dental procedures or even brushing. Valves damaged by rheumatic disease or congenital lesions develop sterile fibrin and platelet deposits (non-bacterial thrombotic vegetations). Viridans streptococci make extracellular polysaccharides, mainly dextrans (glucans) from sucrose, and adhesins that bind fibrin, platelets and fibronectin, so they attach to these deposits and grow into an infected vegetation. The same dextrans help S. mutans form dental plaque and cause caries. They are low-virulence organisms, so illness is slow (subacute), unlike the acute destructive endocarditis of S. aureus on normal valves. Treatment is benzylpenicillin or ceftriaxone for 4 weeks (2 weeks with gentamicin in selected cases). Antibiotic prophylaxis for dental work is advised for high-risk patients according to local guidelines.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Coagulase production: Coagulase belongs to S. aureus, which grows in clusters and destroys valves within days rather than over 7 weeks.
  • C. Protein A: Protein A is also staphylococcal and blocks opsonisation rather than helping attachment to her vegetation.
  • D. Exotoxin A: Exotoxin A is made by Pseudomonas, which is an oxidase-positive rod, not an optochin-resistant streptococcus.
  • E. Urease production: Urease helps urinary organisms form stones and plays no part in sticking to her mitral valve.

Key point: Viridans streptococci cause subacute endocarditis on damaged valves; their dextrans and adhesins bind fibrin-platelet deposits.

Question 8 · Mycology · medium

A 52-year-old man in Peshawar completed treatment for pulmonary tuberculosis 6 years ago and was left with a cavity in the right upper lobe. For 4 months he has coughed up blood intermittently and lost 3 kg. Chest CT shows a rounded mass within the old cavity with an air crescent above it. His total IgE is 940 IU/mL (below 100), Aspergillus precipitins are positive, and sputum grows Aspergillus fumigatus. He is not immunosuppressed.

What does the mass in the cavity represent?

  1. A. Invasive growth into lung tissue
  2. B. An allergic mucus plug
  3. C. A tuberculous granuloma
  4. D. A lung cancer
  5. E. A ball of fungal hyphae
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. A ball of fungal hyphae

Aspergillus fumigatus is a ubiquitous environmental mould whose small spores are inhaled constantly. The disease it causes depends on the host. In a person with normal immunity but a pre-existing lung cavity, usually from treated tuberculosis in Pakistan, the fungus grows as a ball of matted hyphae within the cavity without invading tissue: an aspergilloma, seen on CT as a rounded mass with an air crescent, often moving with position, and causing haemoptysis that can be life-threatening. Chronic pulmonary aspergillosis also causes weight loss, cough and progressive cavity wall thickening with positive Aspergillus IgG. Treatment is long-term itraconazole or voriconazole, with embolisation or surgical resection for severe bleeding. In atopic patients with asthma or cystic fibrosis, allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis causes wheeze, mucus plugs, central bronchiectasis, very high IgE and eosinophilia, treated with corticosteroids and itraconazole. In the profoundly immunosuppressed, the same fungus invades blood vessels.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Invasive growth into lung tissue: Invasion happens in neutropenia or transplantation; he is not immunosuppressed and has had the mass for 4 months.
  • B. An allergic mucus plug: Allergic plugs form in bronchi of asthmatic patients; his mass sits inside an old tuberculous cavity with an air crescent.
  • C. A tuberculous granuloma: A tuberculous granuloma would not move within the cavity or grow Aspergillus from his sputum, and his treatment finished 6 years ago.
  • D. A lung cancer: Cancer must be considered with haemoptysis and weight loss, but it does not give an air crescent or positive Aspergillus precipitins.

Key point: An aspergilloma is a non-invasive fungus ball in a pre-existing lung cavity causing haemoptysis; ABPA and invasive disease are the other Aspergillus patterns.

Question 9 · General microbiology · easy

A 45-year-old man with rheumatic heart disease has had fever and tiredness for 3 weeks. He has a new murmur of mitral regurgitation, splinter haemorrhages and microscopic blood in the urine. He is alert with a blood pressure of 124/76 mmHg, and he has not taken any antibiotics. A cannula was placed in his forearm in the emergency department 6 hours ago. The registrar asks the house officer to send blood cultures.

How should the blood cultures be taken?

  1. A. Three separate venepunctures
  2. B. One set from the existing cannula
  3. C. One set at a fever spike
  4. D. Two sets after starting antibiotics
  5. E. A small sample in each bottle
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Three separate venepunctures

In infective endocarditis bacteria are shed continuously from the vegetation, so cultures do not need to be timed to fever. In a stable patient, at least three sets (each an aerobic and an anaerobic bottle) are taken from separate venepunctures over some hours before antibiotics start; a sick patient has three sets taken within an hour and treatment started. Each bottle needs about 8-10 mL of blood in adults, because the number of bacteria per mL is low and the volume cultured matters most. The skin is cleaned with chlorhexidine in alcohol (or povidone-iodine) and allowed to dry, and the bottle tops are disinfected. Blood is not taken from existing cannulas, which are often colonised. Growth of the same organism in several sets (a major Duke criterion with typical organisms) supports true infection; skin organisms such as coagulase-negative staphylococci in only one of several sets usually mean contamination.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. One set from the existing cannula: His forearm cannula was placed 6 hours ago and is often colonised, so growth from it may mislead.
  • C. One set at a fever spike: Timing to a fever spike is unnecessary, because bacteria are shed continuously from his valve vegetation.
  • D. Two sets after starting antibiotics: Starting antibiotics first would reduce the yield, and he is stable enough for cultures to be taken beforehand.
  • E. A small sample in each bottle: Small volumes lower the yield badly, as the number of bacteria per millilitre of his blood is low.

Key point: For suspected endocarditis take at least three sets of blood cultures by separate venepuncture with adequate volume before antibiotics.

Question 10 · Parasitology · medium

A 27-year-old man in Multan was treated for vivax malaria and then started a 14-day course of primaquine without any prior testing. On day 3 he becomes pale and jaundiced and passes dark brown urine. Haemoglobin has fallen from 12.6 to 7.8 g/dL, reticulocytes are 8%, and the blood film shows bite cells and Heinz bodies on supravital staining. The direct antiglobulin test is negative.

Which test should have been done before starting the drug?

  1. A. Direct antiglobulin test
  2. B. Haemoglobin electrophoresis
  3. C. G6PD enzyme assay
  4. D. Liver function tests
  5. E. Osmotic fragility test
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. G6PD enzyme assay

Glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase is the first enzyme of the pentose phosphate pathway and the red cell's only source of NADPH, which keeps glutathione reduced and protects haemoglobin from oxidative damage. In deficiency, an oxidative stress overwhelms this defence: haemoglobin precipitates as Heinz bodies, and the spleen removes bites of membrane, giving bite cells, with acute intravascular haemolysis, anaemia, jaundice and dark urine from haemoglobinuria. The common triggers are drugs (primaquine and tafenoquine, dapsone, nitrofurantoin, sulphonamides, methylene blue), infections and fava beans. The deficiency is X-linked and common in malarious regions, including parts of Pakistan, because it gives some protection against falciparum malaria. G6PD activity should therefore be measured before prescribing primaquine or tafenoquine; where testing is unavailable, weekly low-dose primaquine regimens or close monitoring are used. Note that enzyme levels can appear falsely normal during an acute haemolytic episode, because surviving young cells have higher activity, so testing is repeated later.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Direct antiglobulin test: A positive antiglobulin test would indicate antibody-coated cells; his is negative, and the trigger was an oxidant drug.
  • B. Haemoglobin electrophoresis: Electrophoresis detects sickle cell and thalassaemia, which would have caused anaemia long before this drug.
  • D. Liver function tests: Liver tests become abnormal after haemolysis but cannot predict it, and his jaundice comes from destroyed red cells.
  • E. Osmotic fragility test: Osmotic fragility detects spherocytosis, which would cause lifelong anaemia rather than haemolysis starting on day 3 of a drug.

Key point: Check G6PD before primaquine or tafenoquine: deficiency causes oxidative haemolysis with Heinz bodies and bite cells.
