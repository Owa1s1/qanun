Question 1 · Antenatal care and normal pregnancy · medium

A 29-year-old gravida 3 para 2 in Gwadar has a positive urine pregnancy test at 5 weeks and 2 days by dates. Her second baby, two years ago, was stillborn at 36 weeks with a large lumbosacral spinal defect. She did not take any supplements in that pregnancy. She has no diabetes or epilepsy, takes no regular medicines and her body mass index is 24 kg/m2. Blood pressure is 110/70 mmHg. She has been told to take a vitamin tablet and asks how much folic acid she needs this time.

What is the most appropriate folic acid regimen?

  1. A. 400 micrograms daily until 12 weeks
  2. B. 400 micrograms daily throughout pregnancy
  3. C. 1 mg daily until 12 weeks
  4. D. 5 mg daily until 12 weeks
  5. E. 5 mg daily throughout pregnancy
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. 5 mg daily until 12 weeks

The neural tube closes by about 28 days after conception, often before a woman knows she is pregnant. Women at average risk should take 400 micrograms of folic acid daily from before conception until 12 weeks. Women at higher risk take 5 mg daily, ideally starting before conception, until 12 weeks. High-risk groups include a previous baby with a neural tube defect, a personal or family history, diabetes and the use of antiepileptic drugs (NICE and RCOG). Since she is already 5 weeks, she should start 5 mg today. Fortified flour and dietary folate cannot supply this dose. A woman with a previous affected pregnancy should also have a detailed anomaly scan and counselling about recurrence risk.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. 400 micrograms daily until 12 weeks: This is the dose for women at average risk; a previous pregnancy with a neural tube defect puts her in the high-risk group, for whom 400 micrograms is inadequate.
  • B. 400 micrograms daily throughout pregnancy: Still the low-risk dose; the neural tube closes by about six weeks, so she needs a higher dose in the first trimester, not a longer course of 400 micrograms.
  • C. 1 mg daily until 12 weeks: 1 mg is not the recommended dose after a previous affected pregnancy; guidance advises 5 mg daily for this high-risk group until 12 weeks.
  • E. 5 mg daily throughout pregnancy: Continuing 5 mg beyond 12 weeks adds no neural tube benefit because the tube has closed, and a high dose may mask vitamin B12 deficiency.

Key point: Prescribe the correct folic acid dose for a woman with a previous neural tube defect.

Question 2 · Complications of later pregnancy · easy

A 29-year-old gravida 2 para 1 attends a hospital in Muzaffarabad at 36 weeks' gestation. Her fundal height is 31 cm, and ultrasound shows an estimated fetal weight below the 5th centile with a normal amniotic fluid index of 10 cm. She has chronic hypertension treated with labetalol, and her blood pressure today is 138/88 mmHg. Fetal movements are normal and the CTG is reactive. Urine protein is negative. The obstetrician wants a test that best shows placental function and the risk of fetal hypoxia.

Which test is most appropriate?

  1. A. Contraction stress test
  2. B. Umbilical artery Doppler
  3. C. Fetal fibronectin test
  4. D. Amniotic fluid creatinine
  5. E. Maternal serum alpha-fetoprotein
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Umbilical artery Doppler

Fetal growth restriction is an estimated fetal weight below the 10th centile with signs of placental insufficiency. Umbilical artery Doppler measures resistance in the placental circulation; a raised pulsatility index, and absent or reversed end-diastolic flow, signal increasing placental failure. Cochrane reviews show that using umbilical artery Doppler in high-risk pregnancies reduces perinatal deaths and unnecessary induction, and RCOG Green-top 31 recommends it as the primary surveillance tool for growth-restricted fetuses, supplemented by CTG, liquor volume and, in late-onset disease, middle cerebral artery Doppler. A biophysical profile or CTG alone is a poor predictor of outcome. Contraction stress tests and amniotic fluid creatinine have no role. Combine the findings with maternal assessment and plan the timing of delivery.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Contraction stress test: A contraction stress test requires induced contractions and can provoke fetal compromise; it is not standard and has no proven benefit in this setting.
  • C. Fetal fibronectin test: Fetal fibronectin predicts preterm labour from cervicovaginal fluid and says nothing about placental function or growth restriction.
  • D. Amniotic fluid creatinine: Amniotic creatinine was once used to assess fetal maturity or renal function; it is not a test of placental function and needs invasive amniocentesis.
  • E. Maternal serum alpha-fetoprotein: Serum AFP screens for neural tube defects and aneuploidy and is not used to assess placental function in the third trimester.

Key point: Choose umbilical artery Doppler as the test of placental function in a growth-restricted fetus.

Question 3 · Obstetric emergencies · medium

A 27-year-old gravida 3 para 2 delivered a 3.6 kg baby vaginally at a hospital in Mingora, Swat, 10 minutes ago. The placenta was still in the uterus when the attendant applied firm cord traction without confirming uterine contraction. The woman suddenly becomes pale and sweaty, with severe lower abdominal pain. Pulse is 130/min and blood pressure 78/46 mmHg. Bleeding is moderate. The fundus cannot be felt abdominally, and a smooth bluish-red mass is seen at the introitus with the cord attached to it.

What is the most appropriate immediate management?

  1. A. Remove the placenta first, then replace the uterus
  2. B. Give ergometrine to contract the uterus
  3. C. Immediate manual replacement of the uterus
  4. D. Hysterectomy
  5. E. Pack the vagina with gauze and transfer
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Immediate manual replacement of the uterus

Acute uterine inversion occurs when the fundus turns inside out through the cervix, in about 1 in 2,000-5,000 births, usually from excessive cord traction on an undelivered placenta with a fundal implantation, or from fundal pressure, a short cord or placenta accreta. It presents with sudden collapse, neurogenic shock out of proportion to blood loss, severe pain, a mass in the vagina or at the vulva and an absent fundus on palpation. Management: call for help, give intravenous fluids and blood, stop uterotonics, and replace the uterus at once by pushing the fundus through the cervical ring with a hand along the axis of the vagina (Johnson manoeuvre), leaving the placenta in place until replaced. If the cervix constricts, use a tocolytic (terbutaline, magnesium or GTN) or general anaesthesia, or hydrostatic replacement (O'Sullivan), with surgery as a last resort. Give oxytocin after replacement.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Remove the placenta first, then replace the uterus: Detaching the placenta before replacement increases bleeding; the placenta is left in place until the uterus has been replaced, unless it separates spontaneously.
  • B. Give ergometrine to contract the uterus: Uterotonics must not be given while the uterus is inverted, as they make the cervix contract around the fundus and prevent replacement.
  • D. Hysterectomy: Hysterectomy is a last resort for inversion that cannot be replaced manually or surgically; manual replacement is first-line and usually succeeds if done early.
  • E. Pack the vagina with gauze and transfer: Packing leaves the inversion in place and the cervical ring tightens over time, making replacement harder; shock worsens without immediate correction.

Key point: Replace the uterus manually without delay in acute uterine inversion.

Question 4 · Labour and delivery · easy

A 27-year-old gravida 2 para 1 is in labour at a hospital in Quetta. On vaginal examination, the cervix is fully dilated and the leading part of the fetal skull is 2 cm below the level of the ischial spines. The head is in an occipito-anterior position with a small amount of caput. Abdominal palpation shows that none of the fetal head is palpable above the pelvic brim (0/5). Contractions are 4 in 10 minutes and the CTG is normal.

What is the station of the fetal head?

  1. A. Station -2
  2. B. Station 0
  3. C. Station +1
  4. D. Station +2
  5. E. Station +4
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. Station +2

Station describes the level of the fetal presenting part in relation to the ischial spines, an imaginary plane halfway through the pelvis. Station 0 is at the spines, negative numbers are above and positive numbers are below, measured in centimetres (the DeLee system runs from -5 to +5; some units use thirds). Engagement means the widest diameter of the presenting part (biparietal diameter for the head) has passed through the pelvic inlet, which usually corresponds to station 0 or below and to a head that is no more than 2/5 palpable abdominally. A fully dilated woman with a head at +2 is near the pelvic floor and usually delivers soon, which matters when deciding on assisted birth. Station is recorded on the partograph with a circle, alongside cervical dilatation, so that descent can be followed.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Station -2: A station of minus 2 means the head is 2 cm above the ischial spines; here the head has descended below the spines, so this is wrong.
  • B. Station 0: Station 0 means the head is at the ischial spines, which is engagement; her head is 2 cm below them.
  • C. Station +1: Station +1 would be 1 cm below the spines; her head is described as 2 cm below.
  • E. Station +4: Station +4 means the head is distending the perineum; crowning is not present when the head is only 2 cm below the spines.

Key point: Determine fetal head station from the relationship to the ischial spines.

Question 5 · Medical disorders in pregnancy · medium

A 27-year-old gravida 3 para 2 at 20 weeks' gestation attends a district hospital in Tank with a six-week cough, evening fevers, night sweats and weight loss of 4 kg. A family member recently completed treatment for a chest illness. Temperature is 37.8 °C, pulse 98/min, oxygen saturation 96% on air and she has crackles at the right apex. Sputum smear is positive for acid-fast bacilli and GeneXpert detects Mycobacterium tuberculosis with no rifampicin resistance. The chest radiograph, taken with an abdominal shield, shows a right upper zone cavity. Haemoglobin is 10.2 g/dL (11-15) and ALT is 24 U/L (below 35). She is HIV negative.

Which drug should be omitted from her treatment regimen?

  1. A. Isoniazid
  2. B. Rifampicin
  3. C. Pyrazinamide
  4. D. Ethambutol
  5. E. Streptomycin
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Streptomycin

Untreated tuberculosis in pregnancy is more dangerous than its treatment: it increases maternal morbidity, preterm birth, low birth weight and perinatal death. Pakistan is among the countries with the highest tuberculosis burden. Standard first-line therapy (isoniazid, rifampicin, pyrazinamide and ethambutol for two months, then isoniazid and rifampicin for four months) is used in pregnancy. Pyridoxine 10-25 mg daily is given with isoniazid. Streptomycin and other aminoglycosides cross the placenta and cause irreversible eighth cranial nerve damage in the fetus, so they are avoided. Monitor liver function, as pregnancy increases hepatotoxicity risk. Breastfeeding is encouraged; the baby should be assessed for congenital tuberculosis and given isoniazid preventive therapy if the mother is infectious, with BCG deferred.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Isoniazid: Isoniazid is a core drug in pregnancy, given with pyridoxine 10-25 mg daily to prevent neuropathy; it is safe for the fetus.
  • B. Rifampicin: Rifampicin is essential and safe in pregnancy; it is a key drug of the regimen and should not be omitted.
  • C. Pyrazinamide: WHO and national programmes use pyrazinamide in pregnancy because benefits outweigh limited data, and it forms part of the standard intensive phase.
  • D. Ethambutol: Ethambutol is considered safe in pregnancy with no teratogenic effect at standard doses, and it forms part of the intensive phase.

Key point: Identify streptomycin as the antituberculosis drug to avoid in pregnancy.

Question 6 · Puerperium and maternal health · medium

A 30-year-old woman is seen on day 7 after a caesarean section at a hospital in Bannu. She is afebrile with a pulse of 80/min. The abdominal wound is red, swollen and tender over 4 cm, with purulent discharge from the centre of the incision. The surrounding erythema extends 3 cm. The fascia feels intact, there is no crepitus, and the uterus is non-tender with normal lochia. She is eating and mobilising. White cells are 9,800/µL and CRP is 2.4 mg/dL (below 0.5). The wound was closed with a subcuticular suture.

What is the most appropriate first step?

  1. A. Open and drain the wound; send culture
  2. B. Oral ciprofloxacin for seven days
  3. C. Immediate re-suturing of the wound under local
  4. D. Hysterectomy
  5. E. Topical antibiotic cream and a dressing
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Open and drain the wound; send culture

Surgical site infection complicates 3-15% of caesarean sections, more with obesity, diabetes, emergency surgery and prolonged labour. Superficial infection presents on day 4-8 with erythema, swelling, tenderness and purulent discharge. Management is to open the wound along its length, drain and debride it, irrigate and pack it, and send pus for culture, as drainage is the key treatment. Add antibiotics (for example cefalexin, co-amoxiclav or flucloxacillin; consider MRSA, which is common in Pakistani hospitals, with doxycycline or clindamycin per sensitivity) if there is spreading cellulitis, fever or systemic signs. Look for deeper infection: fascial dehiscence or necrotising fasciitis (severe pain, crepitus, skin necrosis, systemic toxicity) needs urgent surgical debridement. Wound care and hygiene are essential.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Oral ciprofloxacin for seven days: Antibiotics alone do not drain pus; ciprofloxacin has poor reliability against skin pathogens and is not recommended; drainage is the main treatment.
  • C. Immediate re-suturing of the wound under local: Closing an infected wound traps pus and worsens infection; it is left open to heal by secondary intention after drainage and debridement.
  • D. Hysterectomy: Hysterectomy is not indicated for superficial wound infection; she has no uterine tenderness, fever or abnormal lochia.
  • E. Topical antibiotic cream and a dressing: Topical agents do not penetrate the infected tissue or drain pus; they do not replace opening the wound.

Key point: Drain a superficial wound infection after caesarean section as the primary treatment.

Question 7 · Early pregnancy · easy

A 28-year-old woman collapses at a roadside rest stop in the Kaghan valley after one day of severe lower abdominal pain and seven weeks of amenorrhoea. Three hours later she arrives at a hospital in Mansehra. She is pale, sweaty and drowsy. Pulse is 134/min, blood pressure 76/40 mmHg and respiratory rate 28/min. The abdomen is distended with marked tenderness and guarding, and she has shoulder-tip pain. Urine pregnancy test is positive. Haemoglobin is 6.9 g/dL (12-15). Bedside ultrasound shows free fluid extending to Morison's pouch.

What is the most appropriate immediate management?

  1. A. Intramuscular methotrexate
  2. B. Formal transvaginal scan before treatment
  3. C. Resuscitation and immediate surgery
  4. D. Observation with serial beta-hCG
  5. E. Misoprostol 800 micrograms vaginally
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Resuscitation and immediate surgery

Ruptured ectopic pregnancy is a leading cause of first-trimester maternal death. Suspect it in any woman of reproductive age with abdominal pain, collapse and a positive pregnancy test; shoulder-tip pain suggests diaphragmatic irritation from blood. NICE and RCOG advise immediate resuscitation (two large-bore cannulae, crystalloid, early blood, oxygen, and activation of the major haemorrhage protocol) and urgent surgery, laparoscopy if she is stable enough and otherwise laparotomy, usually with salpingectomy. Do not delay for formal imaging or hCG. Give anti-D afterwards if RhD negative. In remote areas such as Kaghan, delay in reaching a hospital with a theatre and blood is the main danger, so early recognition, transfusion availability and rapid referral save lives. Counsel afterwards about fertility and recurrence risk.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Intramuscular methotrexate: Methotrexate is for stable, unruptured ectopic pregnancy; she is in haemorrhagic shock with rupture, so only surgery can stop the bleeding.
  • B. Formal transvaginal scan before treatment: Formal imaging would delay life-saving surgery; the bedside scan already shows haemoperitoneum, and she is too unstable for further tests.
  • D. Observation with serial beta-hCG: Observation is for a stable woman with a declining hCG and minimal symptoms; she is in shock with rupture and will die without surgery.
  • E. Misoprostol 800 micrograms vaginally: Misoprostol is for failed intrauterine pregnancy; it cannot treat a ruptured tubal ectopic and would delay surgery.

Key point: Recognise a ruptured ectopic pregnancy with shock and proceed to immediate surgery.

Question 8 · Antenatal care and normal pregnancy · easy

A 29-year-old gravida 2 para 1 at 34 weeks' gestation is having a routine ultrasound at a tertiary care hospital in Peshawar. Within five minutes of lying flat on her back she becomes pale, sweaty and light-headed and says she is about to faint. Blood pressure is 82/50 mmHg and pulse 104/min. Before lying down these were 116/72 mmHg and 84/min. The abdomen is soft and non-tender, there is no vaginal bleeding and the fetal heart rate is 142/min. Within a minute of being turned onto her left side she feels well and her blood pressure rises to 112/70 mmHg.

What is the most likely cause of her symptoms?

  1. A. Caval compression by the uterus
  2. B. Concealed placental abruption
  3. C. Acute pulmonary thromboembolism
  4. D. Hypoglycaemia after prolonged fasting
  5. E. Paroxysmal supraventricular tachycardia
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Caval compression by the uterus

From the second trimester the gravid uterus can press on the inferior vena cava and aorta when a woman lies supine, reducing venous return, stroke volume and cardiac output. In most women collateral veins compensate, but in a minority, up to about 1 in 10, supine hypotensive syndrome develops with pallor, sweating, nausea, hypotension, tachycardia or sometimes bradycardia, and a fall in uteroplacental perfusion. Symptoms resolve quickly when the woman is turned to the left lateral position. Practical measures: examine and scan women in the semi-recumbent or left lateral position, use a 15-degree left tilt during caesarean section and during resuscitation after 20 weeks, and advise against lying flat on the back in late pregnancy. Consider abruption or embolism only if posture does not fully correct the signs.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Concealed placental abruption: Would cause constant pain, a tense tender uterus or bleeding and fetal heart changes; her abdomen is soft, there is no bleeding and she recovered with posture alone.
  • C. Acute pulmonary thromboembolism: Causes sudden breathlessness, pleuritic pain and hypoxia and would not resolve within a minute of turning; she has no respiratory symptoms and recovered fully on her left side.
  • D. Hypoglycaemia after prolonged fasting: Produces tremor and hunger with a low glucose, usually after fasting and not relieved by posture; her symptoms began on lying flat and resolved immediately on turning left.
  • E. Paroxysmal supraventricular tachycardia: Gives sudden regular palpitations at rates above 150/min; her pulse is only 104/min and symptoms reversed instantly with posture, which an arrhythmia would not do.

Key point: Recognise supine hypotensive syndrome and know the left lateral position as the remedy.

Question 9 · Complications of later pregnancy · easy

A 30-year-old gravida 3 para 2 at 32 weeks' gestation is brought to a district hospital in Chitral after a sudden gush of bright red vaginal blood, about 200 mL, while sleeping. She has no abdominal pain or contractions. The bleeding has now almost stopped. Pulse is 92/min and blood pressure 112/70 mmHg. The uterus is soft and non-tender, the fetal head is high and the fetal heart rate is 144/min. No scan was done in this pregnancy. Haemoglobin is 10.8 g/dL.

What is the most appropriate next step?

  1. A. Digital vaginal examination
  2. B. Ultrasound to locate the placenta
  3. C. Artificial rupture of membranes
  4. D. Immediate caesarean section
  5. E. Induction of labour with oxytocin
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Ultrasound to locate the placenta

Painless fresh bleeding in the late second or third trimester, with a soft non-tender uterus and a high or abnormally lying fetal head, is placenta praevia until proved otherwise. A finger examination must not be done until the placental site is known, because it can provoke severe haemorrhage. Transabdominal ultrasound, followed by transvaginal ultrasound, which is safe and more accurate, locates the placenta. Initial care includes intravenous access, blood for haemoglobin and cross-match, and anti-D if she is RhD negative. If she is stable and praevia is confirmed, she is admitted for observation and given antenatal corticosteroids. In Pakistan many women are unbooked and have never had a scan, so bleeding may be the first sign; a hospital with blood and a theatre is essential.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Digital vaginal examination: A finger examination could provoke torrential bleeding if the placenta lies over the cervix, so it is avoided until the placental site is known.
  • C. Artificial rupture of membranes: Amniotomy would be dangerous if the placenta is praevia, risking haemorrhage and cord problems, and she is not in labour; the diagnosis must come first.
  • D. Immediate caesarean section: She is haemodynamically stable with a reassuring fetus; emergency delivery is for heavy ongoing bleeding or fetal compromise, and the cause has not yet been shown.
  • E. Induction of labour with oxytocin: Induction is contraindicated until placenta praevia is excluded, because vaginal birth could cause massive haemorrhage, and she is only 32 weeks.

Key point: Localise the placenta by ultrasound before any examination in third-trimester painless bleeding.

Question 10 · Obstetric emergencies · easy

A 22-year-old primigravida at 37 weeks' gestation attends a basic health unit in Chitral with a severe headache and blurred vision for 6 hours. While being examined, she has a generalised tonic-clonic seizure lasting about 90 seconds, then is drowsy. Blood pressure is 176/114 mmHg and urine dipstick shows 3+ protein. Oxygen saturation is 94% on air and the pulse is 110/min. The nearest hospital is four hours away. The health unit has magnesium sulphate, diazepam, phenytoin and labetalol available. A second seizure begins.

Which drug is the first-line treatment to stop and prevent further seizures?

  1. A. Diazepam 10 mg intravenously
  2. B. Magnesium sulphate 4 g intravenously
  3. C. Phenytoin 15 mg/kg intravenously
  4. D. Labetalol 20 mg intravenously
  5. E. Lorazepam 4 mg intravenously
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Magnesium sulphate 4 g intravenously

Eclampsia is the occurrence of generalised seizures in a woman with pre-eclampsia, and it remains a leading cause of maternal death in Pakistan, particularly among unbooked women. The Collaborative Eclampsia Trial showed that magnesium sulphate halves the risk of recurrent seizures compared with diazepam and reduces maternal mortality, so it is the drug of choice. Immediate care follows airway, breathing and circulation: place the woman in the left lateral position, protect her from injury, give oxygen, secure intravenous access, give magnesium sulphate, and treat severe hypertension with labetalol, nifedipine or hydralazine. After stabilisation, deliver in a hospital capable of caesarean section and neonatal care. A health unit should refer after giving the loading dose and, when possible, the maintenance dose during transfer with a trained attendant.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Diazepam 10 mg intravenously: Diazepam stops a seizure but has a higher rate of recurrence and more maternal respiratory depression and neonatal depression than magnesium; it is second-line.
  • C. Phenytoin 15 mg/kg intravenously: Phenytoin is less effective than magnesium sulphate in eclampsia and carries cardiac risks; it is not recommended.
  • D. Labetalol 20 mg intravenously: Labetalol lowers blood pressure but does not prevent eclamptic seizures; magnesium must be given first for seizure control.
  • E. Lorazepam 4 mg intravenously: Lorazepam can stop seizures but is second-line to magnesium sulphate for eclampsia and prevents recurrence less well.

Key point: Give magnesium sulphate as first-line treatment for eclamptic seizures.
