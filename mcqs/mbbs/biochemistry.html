Question 1 · Cell and molecular biology, genetics · medium

During DNA replication, DNA polymerase sometimes inserts a base opposite the wrong template base. Immediately after replication, a protein complex binds to this mismatched pair, signals the new strand to be cut, and directs removal of the incorrect segment.

Which protein recognises a base-base mismatch right after DNA replication?

  1. A. DNA glycosylase
  2. B. DNA polymerase
  3. C. DNA ligase
  4. D. Exonuclease
  5. E. MutS
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. MutS

MutS is the first protein that binds a base-base mismatch left by DNA polymerase after replication. It then recruits MutL and endonucleases to cut the newly synthesised strand, remove the incorrect segment, and resynthesise it correctly using the old strand as a template. DNA glycosylase removes single damaged bases, not replication mismatches, so it is not the first sensor. DNA polymerase proofreads while it works but does not initiate mismatch repair. This matters because defects in mismatch repair cause microsatellite instability and a high risk of colorectal cancer. Families with these defects often develop early-onset bowel tumours.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. DNA glycosylase: It removes a single abnormal base, such as uracil, in base excision repair, not a replication mismatch.
  • B. DNA polymerase: It synthesises DNA and proofreads, but the dedicated mismatch sensor after replication is MutS.
  • C. DNA ligase: It seals the final nick after repair synthesis but does not recognise mismatches.
  • D. Exonuclease: It removes nucleotides from the end of a strand but is not the initial mismatch-recognition protein.

Key point: Identify MutS as the protein that recognises base-base mismatches after DNA replication.

Question 2 · Nutrition, vitamins and minerals · easy

A student lists the vitamin that forms part of coenzyme A and the acyl carrier protein of fatty acid synthase.

Which vitamin is this?

  1. A. Biotin (B7)
  2. B. Folic acid (B9)
  3. C. Riboflavin (B2)
  4. D. Cyanocobalamin (B12)
  5. E. Pantothenic acid
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Pantothenic acid

Pantothenic acid (vitamin B5) is part of coenzyme A, which carries acyl groups in the TCA cycle, fatty acid oxidation and synthesis, cholesterol synthesis and acetylation reactions, and of the acyl carrier protein of fatty acid synthase. It is found in almost all foods, so deficiency is very rare; it was described in prisoners of war as "burning feet" syndrome with paraesthesia, along with dermatitis and fatigue.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Biotin (B7): Biotin carries CO2 in carboxylases such as pyruvate carboxylase.
  • B. Folic acid (B9): Folate carries one-carbon units for purine and thymidine synthesis.
  • C. Riboflavin (B2): Riboflavin forms FAD and FMN, the coenzymes of oxidation-reduction reactions.
  • D. Cyanocobalamin (B12): B12 is used by methionine synthase and methylmalonyl-CoA mutase.

Key point: Pantothenic acid forms coenzyme A and the acyl carrier protein; deficiency is very rare.

Question 3 · Amino acids, haem and nucleotides · easy

A 20-year-old man comes with his third episode of right loin pain spreading to the groin in two years. His father also had kidney stones from his twenties. Urine dipstick shows blood. CT shows several smooth stones in both kidneys. Microscopy of a morning urine sample shows flat hexagonal crystals, and a cyanide-nitroprusside test is positive. Serum calcium and uric acid are normal.

Which amino acids are lost in the urine in this condition?

  1. A. Phenylalanine and tyrosine
  2. B. Leucine, isoleucine and valine
  3. C. Glycine and proline only
  4. D. Cystine and the dibasic amino acids
  5. E. Tryptophan and histidine
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. Cystine and the dibasic amino acids

Cystinuria is an autosomal recessive defect of the transporter for cystine and the dibasic amino acids ornithine, lysine and arginine (memory aid "COLA") in the renal tubule and gut. Cystine is poorly soluble, especially in acid urine, so it forms hexagonal crystals and stones, which are faintly radio-opaque. The cyanide-nitroprusside test turns urine red. Treatment is a large fluid intake, alkalinising the urine (potassium citrate), a low-salt diet and, if needed, drugs that form soluble complexes with cystine (penicillamine, tiopronin).

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Phenylalanine and tyrosine: Phenylalanine and tyrosine are not lost in cystinuria; high blood phenylalanine is PKU.
  • B. Leucine, isoleucine and valine: Leucine, isoleucine and valine are raised in the blood in maple syrup urine disease, which causes newborn encephalopathy, not stones.
  • C. Glycine and proline only: Glycine and proline alone are lost in harmless iminoglycinuria, which forms no hexagonal crystals or stones.
  • E. Tryptophan and histidine: Hartnup disease loses neutral amino acids such as tryptophan and causes a pellagra-like rash, not cystine stones.

Key point: Cystinuria (COLA transporter defect) causes hexagonal cystine stones; treat with fluids, alkali and chelators.

Question 4 · Carbohydrate metabolism · easy

A 58-year-old man with type 2 diabetes and heart failure starts empagliflozin. His glucose improves and he loses 3 kg, and he passes more urine. His urine dipstick now shows glucose 4+ despite a blood glucose of 144 mg/dL. He later develops a genital fungal infection. Before surgery, he is told to stop it for a few days.

How does empagliflozin lower blood glucose?

  1. A. It increases insulin secretion
  2. B. It blocks renal glucose reabsorption
  3. C. It moves GLUT4 to the muscle membrane
  4. D. It blocks intestinal glucose absorption
  5. E. It stops gluconeogenesis in the liver
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. It blocks renal glucose reabsorption

In the proximal tubule, SGLT2 reabsorbs most filtered glucose together with sodium, and SGLT1 takes the rest further along. SGLT2 inhibitors (empagliflozin, dapagliflozin) block this, so glucose is lost in the urine and blood glucose falls without causing hypoglycaemia. They also protect the heart and kidneys. Side effects include genital fungal infections and urinary infections from glycosuria, dehydration, and euglycaemic diabetic ketoacidosis (ketoacidosis with near-normal glucose), especially during illness, fasting or surgery, so they are paused before operations.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. It increases insulin secretion: Sulfonylureas close beta-cell K-ATP channels to release insulin; they would not cause 4+ glycosuria at a glucose of 144 mg/dL.
  • C. It moves GLUT4 to the muscle membrane: Insulin and exercise move GLUT4 to the muscle membrane; this lowers glucose without glycosuria or extra urine.
  • D. It blocks intestinal glucose absorption: Acarbose and SGLT1 blockers act in the gut; heavy glycosuria at near-normal blood glucose shows this drug acts in the kidney.
  • E. It stops gluconeogenesis in the liver: Metformin mainly reduces hepatic gluconeogenesis; it does not cause glycosuria, extra urine or genital fungal infections.

Key point: SGLT2 inhibitors block renal glucose reabsorption; risks include genital infections and euglycaemic DKA.

Question 5 · Chemistry of biomolecules · easy

A student tests three sugars with Benedict reagent. Lactose and maltose give a positive result, but table sugar does not.

Why does sucrose give a negative Benedict test?

  1. A. It is a polysaccharide
  2. B. Both anomeric carbons are bonded
  3. C. It contains only glucose
  4. D. It has a beta-1,4 bond
  5. E. It is not soluble in water
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Both anomeric carbons are bonded

A sugar is reducing if it has a free anomeric carbon that can open to an aldehyde or keto group. In sucrose, carbon 1 of glucose is joined to carbon 2 of fructose (alpha-1, beta-2), so both anomeric carbons are tied up and sucrose is non-reducing. Maltose (glucose alpha-1,4 glucose) and lactose (galactose beta-1,4 glucose) each keep one free anomeric carbon and are reducing. Sucrose is digested by sucrase-isomaltase on the brush border.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. It is a polysaccharide: Sucrose is a disaccharide of glucose and fructose, not a polysaccharide.
  • C. It contains only glucose: Sucrose contains glucose and fructose; it is negative because both anomeric carbons are tied up in the bond.
  • D. It has a beta-1,4 bond: Lactose has a beta-1,4 bond; sucrose has an alpha-1, beta-2 bond joining both anomeric carbons.
  • E. It is not soluble in water: Sucrose dissolves easily in water; solubility has nothing to do with the result.

Key point: Sucrose is non-reducing because both anomeric carbons form the glycosidic bond; lactose and maltose are reducing.

Question 6 · Hormone action, metabolism in health and disease, clinical biochemistry · easy

A 40-year-old man with no symptoms has a fasting glucose of 131 mg/dL at a screening camp in his town. He feels well, has no thirst or weight loss, and his BMI is 29. His father has diabetes. He has never been tested before.

What is the most appropriate next step?

  1. A. Diagnose diabetes now and start metformin
  2. B. Repeat the fasting glucose or do an HbA1c on another day
  3. C. Reassure him, as this is impaired fasting glucose
  4. D. Check urine glucose; a positive result confirms diabetes
  5. E. Do a random glucose; above 140 mg/dL confirms it
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Repeat the fasting glucose or do an HbA1c on another day

Diabetes is diagnosed by fasting plasma glucose 126 mg/dL or more, 2-hour glucose 200 mg/dL or more in a 75 g oral glucose tolerance test, HbA1c 6.5% (48 mmol/mol) or more, or a random glucose 200 mg/dL or more with classic symptoms. Without symptoms, one abnormal result must be confirmed by a second test (the same test on another day, or a different test). Prediabetes includes impaired fasting glucose (5.6 or 110 to 124 mg/dL, depending on the guideline), impaired glucose tolerance (2-hour 7.8-11.0) and HbA1c 5.7-6.4%. Glucose samples should be in fluoride tubes or analysed quickly.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Diagnose diabetes now and start metformin: Without symptoms, one abnormal result is not enough; diabetes must be confirmed with a second test.
  • C. Reassure him, as this is impaired fasting glucose: Impaired fasting glucose is 100-125 mg/dL; 131 mg/dL is in the diabetic range and needs confirming.
  • D. Check urine glucose; a positive result confirms diabetes: Urine glucose depends on the renal threshold and is not used to diagnose diabetes.
  • E. Do a random glucose; above 140 mg/dL confirms it: A random glucose of 200 mg/dL or more with symptoms is diagnostic; 140 mg/dL is not.

Key point: Without symptoms, one abnormal glucose or HbA1c must be confirmed by a second test before diagnosing diabetes.

Question 7 · Enzymes, bioenergetics and free radicals · easy

A student doubles the amount of an enzyme in a test tube while keeping everything else the same.

What happens to Vmax and Km?

  1. A. Vmax doubles and Km stays the same
  2. B. Vmax stays the same and Km halves
  3. C. Both double
  4. D. Both halve
  5. E. Neither changes
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Vmax doubles and Km stays the same

Vmax is the rate when all enzyme molecules are working at full speed, so it is proportional to the amount of enzyme present. Doubling the enzyme doubles Vmax. Km reflects how the enzyme binds the substrate and is unchanged. This is why measuring enzyme activity in blood (at saturating substrate) tells the amount of enzyme released from damaged tissue, and why induction of liver enzymes by drugs raises their capacity. The turnover number (kcat) is Vmax divided by the amount of enzyme.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Vmax stays the same and Km halves: Km is a property of the enzyme-substrate pair and does not change with enzyme amount.
  • C. Both double: Only Vmax depends on the amount of enzyme; Km stays the same.
  • D. Both halve: Adding enzyme cannot lower the maximum rate, and Km does not change.
  • E. Neither changes: Doubling the enzyme doubles the number of active sites, so Vmax must rise.

Key point: Vmax is proportional to the amount of enzyme; Km is unchanged.

Question 8 · Lipid metabolism and lipoproteins · easy

Acetyl-CoA is made inside mitochondria, but fatty acids are made in the cytoplasm. The inner mitochondrial membrane does not let acetyl-CoA cross.

How does acetyl-CoA reach the cytoplasm for fat synthesis?

  1. A. It is carried by carnitine
  2. B. It diffuses freely across the inner membrane
  3. C. It leaves as ketone bodies
  4. D. It is carried by albumin
  5. E. It leaves as citrate
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. It leaves as citrate

In the mitochondria, acetyl-CoA joins oxaloacetate to form citrate, which leaves through a carrier. In the cytoplasm, ATP-citrate lyase splits it back into acetyl-CoA and oxaloacetate. The oxaloacetate is reduced to malate, which malic enzyme turns into pyruvate, making NADPH. Fatty acid synthesis needs 14 NADPH for each palmitate; most comes from the pentose phosphate pathway and the rest from malic enzyme. Citrate also activates acetyl-CoA carboxylase, linking a fed state to fat synthesis.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. It is carried by carnitine: Carnitine carries long-chain fatty acids into mitochondria for beta-oxidation, the opposite direction.
  • B. It diffuses freely across the inner membrane: The inner membrane is impermeable to acetyl-CoA, which is why a carrier is needed.
  • C. It leaves as ketone bodies: Ketone bodies are exported from the liver as fuel for other tissues, not used for fat synthesis.
  • D. It is carried by albumin: Albumin carries free fatty acids in the blood, not acetyl groups inside the cell.

Key point: Acetyl-CoA reaches the cytoplasm as citrate; NADPH for fat synthesis comes from the pentose phosphate pathway and malic enzyme.

Question 9 · Cell and molecular biology, genetics · easy

A researcher treats cancer cells with a drug that stops the removal of acetyl groups from histones. Silenced genes begin to be transcribed again.

Why does histone acetylation increase transcription?

  1. A. It adds positive charge, so DNA binds histones more tightly
  2. B. It neutralises lysine charges, loosening chromatin
  3. C. It methylates cytosine in the promoter
  4. D. It cuts the DNA between nucleosomes
  5. E. It adds a cap to the messenger RNA
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. It neutralises lysine charges, loosening chromatin

DNA is wound around histone octamers to form nucleosomes. Histones are rich in positively charged lysine and arginine, which bind the negatively charged DNA. Acetylation of lysines neutralises their charge, so the DNA is held less tightly, chromatin opens into euchromatin and transcription factors can reach the genes. Deacetylation tightens chromatin (heterochromatin) and silences genes. Histone deacetylase inhibitors such as vorinostat are used in some lymphomas. DNA methylation at CpG islands also silences genes.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. It adds positive charge, so DNA binds histones more tightly: Acetylation removes the positive charge on lysine, so DNA binds histones less tightly, not more.
  • C. It methylates cytosine in the promoter: Cytosine methylation in promoters silences genes, and it is a change to DNA, not histones.
  • D. It cuts the DNA between nucleosomes: Acetylation changes histone charge; it does not cut DNA between nucleosomes.
  • E. It adds a cap to the messenger RNA: Capping adds a methylguanosine to the 5' end of new mRNA; it is unrelated to histones.

Key point: Histone acetylation loosens chromatin and increases transcription; deacetylation and DNA methylation silence genes.

Question 10 · Nutrition, vitamins and minerals · easy

A 45-year-old man has taken 4 g of vitamin C daily for two years to prevent colds. He now has severe left loin pain coming in waves, with blood in his urine. CT shows a small radio-opaque stone in the left ureter. His calcium and uric acid are normal.

What type of stone is most likely linked to his vitamin C intake?

  1. A. Uric acid stones
  2. B. Calcium oxalate
  3. C. Cystine stones
  4. D. Struvite (infection) stones
  5. E. Xanthine stones
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Calcium oxalate

Vitamin C is water-soluble and excess is excreted, but part is converted to oxalate. Megadoses increase urinary oxalate and the risk of calcium oxalate stones, especially in people with kidney disease or previous stones. High doses also increase iron absorption (a risk in haemochromatosis and thalassaemia), may interfere with some glucose and occult blood tests, and can cause haemolysis in G6PD deficiency. Vitamin C supplements have not been shown to prevent colds in the general population.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Uric acid stones: Uric acid stones come from high urate or acidic urine; his uric acid is normal.
  • C. Cystine stones: Cystine stones occur in inherited cystinuria, usually from childhood.
  • D. Struvite (infection) stones: Struvite stones follow infection with urease-producing bacteria.
  • E. Xanthine stones: Xanthine stones are very rare and linked to xanthine oxidase deficiency or allopurinol.

Key point: High-dose vitamin C increases urinary oxalate and the risk of calcium oxalate stones.
