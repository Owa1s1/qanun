Question 1 · Paediatric cardiology · medium

An 11-year-old girl is brought to a clinic in Kalam, Swat, where her family is staying for the summer, because for three weeks her handwriting has deteriorated, she drops cups and grimaces, and she is unusually tearful. There are irregular, jerky, non-repetitive movements of the face and hands that disappear in sleep, with a milkmaid grip and a tongue that darts out. She had no fever or joint pain. ASO titre is 480 IU/mL (below 200), ESR 12 mm/h (0 to 20) and CRP 0.2 mg/dL (below 0.5). No murmur is audible.

Which investigation is most important next?

  1. A. Echocardiography
  2. B. MRI of the brain with contrast
  3. C. Serum ceruloplasmin and slit-lamp examination
  4. D. Lumbar puncture
  5. E. Electroencephalography
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Echocardiography

Sydenham chorea is a major manifestation of acute rheumatic fever and may appear weeks to months after the streptococcal infection, so acute-phase reactants may already be normal by then. Typical features are involuntary non-repetitive movements, hypotonia, emotional lability or obsessive behaviour, a milkmaid grip, a darting tongue and handwriting deterioration, which disappear in sleep. Under the 2015 Jones criteria, chorea alone is sufficient for a diagnosis when other causes are excluded. Because carditis may be subclinical, every child needs an echocardiogram. Secondary prophylaxis with benzathine penicillin follows. Mild chorea resolves in weeks to months; valproate, carbamazepine or, in severe cases, corticosteroids may be used. Wilson disease, SLE and drug effects are the other causes to exclude.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. MRI of the brain with contrast: MRI is normal or non-specific in Sydenham chorea and does not change management; the diagnosis here rests on the clinical picture and the raised streptococcal titre.
  • C. Serum ceruloplasmin and slit-lamp examination: Wilson disease is a differential, but a three-week onset with emotional lability and a raised ASO titre fits rheumatic chorea; heart assessment comes first for her.
  • D. Lumbar puncture: There is no fever, meningism or raised pressure, and cerebrospinal fluid is normal in chorea, so lumbar puncture would add risk and no information.
  • E. Electroencephalography: The movements are involuntary and disappear in sleep, with no impaired awareness or stereotyped events; an EEG would not help in this girl.

Key point: Sydenham chorea alone is enough to diagnose rheumatic fever, and echocardiography is needed to look for subclinical carditis.

Question 2 · Paediatric emergencies, poisoning and safeguarding · medium

A 4-year-old girl weighing 16 kg is brought to a hospital in Mardan. Her mother saw her drinking from a large bottle of paracetamol syrup and brought her at once; the exact time was noted and four hours have now passed. She vomited once and is otherwise well, with a normal examination. Alanine aminotransferase is 28 U/L (under 40) and INR 1.0 (0.9 to 1.2). A plasma paracetamol level drawn exactly 4 hours after the ingestion is 180 mg/L, and the UK treatment line at 4 hours is 100 mg/L.

Which is the most appropriate management?

  1. A. Discharge home with parental advice
  2. B. Repeat the level at 8 hours before deciding
  3. C. Intravenous acetylcysteine
  4. D. Oral activated charcoal alone
  5. E. Intravenous vitamin K
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Intravenous acetylcysteine

Paracetamol is normally conjugated, but in overdose the conjugation pathways saturate and more drug is oxidised by cytochrome P450 to N-acetyl-p-benzoquinone imine (NAPQI), which depletes glutathione and injures hepatocytes. For a single acute ingestion with a known time, the plasma level taken at 4 hours or later is plotted on the treatment nomogram. Since the 2012 MHRA change, the UK uses one treatment line, starting at 100 mg/L at 4 hours and falling to about 15 mg/L at 15 hours. A level above the line needs intravenous acetylcysteine, ideally started within 8 hours, when it almost completely prevents liver damage. Levels before 4 hours cannot be interpreted. Alanine aminotransferase and INR are checked at the start and at the end of treatment.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Discharge home with parental advice: Her level of 180 mg/L is well above the 100 mg/L treatment line at 4 hours, so discharge would leave a potentially hepatotoxic dose without any antidote.
  • B. Repeat the level at 8 hours before deciding: Her 4-hour level already exceeds the treatment line, so waiting for a second level only delays the antidote, which works best when started within 8 hours.
  • D. Oral activated charcoal alone: Charcoal helps only when given within about an hour of ingestion; at 4 hours with a level above the line she needs the antidote, not charcoal alone.
  • E. Intravenous vitamin K: Vitamin K treats coagulopathy from deficiency or warfarin; her INR is 1.0 and nothing is bleeding, and it neither replaces glutathione nor prevents liver injury.

Key point: In a single acute paracetamol overdose, a 4-hour level above the treatment line (100 mg/L in the UK) is an indication for intravenous acetylcysteine.

Question 3 · Paediatric endocrinology, metabolic and genetic disorders · medium

A 12-day-old girl is brought to the casualty department in Mardan by her uncle after 3 days of vomiting and limpness. She is pale and peripherally shut down with a heart rate of 188/min, blood pressure 58/30 mm Hg and capillary refill 5 seconds. Her external genitalia are masculinised. Sodium is 117 mEq/L (135 to 145), potassium 7.8 mEq/L (3.5 to 6.0), glucose 29 mg/dL (over 45), and the ECG shows tall peaked T waves. The diagnosis of an adrenal crisis is suspected.

Which is the most appropriate initial treatment?

  1. A. Oral fludrocortisone
  2. B. Intravenous 3% saline for the sodium
  3. C. Insulin with dextrose for the potassium
  4. D. Broad-spectrum antibiotics alone, awaiting cultures
  5. E. Intravenous hydrocortisone, 0.9% saline and dextrose
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Intravenous hydrocortisone, 0.9% saline and dextrose

An adrenal (salt-wasting) crisis in congenital adrenal hyperplasia is a medical emergency with hypotension, hyponatraemia, hyperkalaemia and hypoglycaemia. Blood for 17-hydroxyprogesterone, cortisol and ACTH should be drawn if this does not delay treatment, but treatment must not wait for results. Give intravenous hydrocortisone as a stress-dose bolus, a 0.9% saline bolus (10 to 20 mL/kg, repeated as needed) for shock, and dextrose for hypoglycaemia, with ECG monitoring; calcium gluconate is added if hyperkalaemic ECG changes persist. Hydrocortisone in high dose has enough mineralocorticoid effect to correct salt loss during the crisis, and fludrocortisone is started later once the infant takes feeds. Sepsis is treated in addition if suspected.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Oral fludrocortisone: It takes days to act and is oral in a vomiting, shocked infant; it contains no glucocorticoid to correct cortisol deficiency or hypoglycaemia.
  • B. Intravenous 3% saline for the sodium: Hypertonic saline is used for seizures or severe symptomatic hyponatraemia; her shock and sodium of 117 mEq/L need 0.9% saline with hydrocortisone.
  • C. Insulin with dextrose for the potassium: This lowers potassium temporarily but risks worsening hypoglycaemia at a glucose of 29 mg/dL and does not treat the cortisol deficiency causing the crisis.
  • D. Broad-spectrum antibiotics alone, awaiting cultures: Sepsis may be covered additionally, but antibiotics alone leave cortisol deficiency untreated; masculinised genitalia and the biochemistry point to adrenal crisis.

Key point: An adrenal crisis in an infant is treated at once with intravenous hydrocortisone, 0.9% saline and dextrose, without waiting for hormone results.

Question 4 · Paediatric gastroenterology and hepatology · medium

A 3-week-old boy in Karak has had forceful vomiting after feeds for four days, and he is hungry afterwards. He is the second son and is gaining weight poorly. He had phototherapy on days 3 and 4, received BCG and hepatitis B vaccines at birth and is exclusively breastfed; his mother took iron tablets throughout pregnancy. At age 5 days he was started on a 14-day course of erythromycin syrup after his older brother was diagnosed with whooping cough. Ultrasound shows a pyloric muscle 4.5 mm thick and a channel 18 mm long.

Which earlier exposure is most strongly associated with his condition?

  1. A. Phototherapy for jaundice on day 3 and day 4
  2. B. Hepatitis B and BCG vaccines given at birth
  3. C. Exclusive breastfeeding from the first day
  4. D. Maternal iron tablets taken throughout pregnancy
  5. E. The syrup started on day 5
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. The syrup started on day 5

Infantile hypertrophic pyloric stenosis occurs in about 2 to 4 per 1,000 live births and is more common in boys, firstborns and infants with a family history. Macrolide antibiotics given in the first two weeks of life, particularly erythromycin, are associated with a several-fold increase in risk, and the risk is greatest with exposure in the first 14 days. Azithromycin has been linked too. Macrolides act on motilin receptors and may cause pyloric spasm and hypertrophy. Other associations include maternal smoking and bottle feeding. For pertussis prophylaxis in infants under 1 month, azithromycin is now preferred, but parents should still be told to report vomiting promptly.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Phototherapy for jaundice on day 3 and day 4: Phototherapy treats unconjugated jaundice and has no recognised link with pyloric muscle hypertrophy.
  • B. Hepatitis B and BCG vaccines given at birth: These routine vaccines have no association with hypertrophy of the pyloric muscle in young infants.
  • C. Exclusive breastfeeding from the first day: Breastfeeding is not a risk factor; hypertrophic pyloric stenosis is, if anything, less frequent in breastfed infants.
  • D. Maternal iron tablets taken throughout pregnancy: Maternal iron supplements are not linked with infantile pyloric stenosis; the recognised maternal factor is smoking, not iron.

Key point: Oral erythromycin (and other macrolides) in the first 2 weeks of life is associated with infantile hypertrophic pyloric stenosis.

Question 5 · Growth, development and behaviour · easy

An 11-year-old boy from Timergara, Dir, is brought by his father because pubic hair has appeared and he has started to smell of sweat. He is well and has grown 5 cm in the past year. His height is at the 50th centile. Examination shows no acne, pubic hair stage 2 and testes of 5 mL on both sides. Bone age is 11 years. His father says that he himself began shaving at about 14 years.

What do these findings indicate?

  1. A. Normal onset of puberty
  2. B. Precocious puberty
  3. C. Premature adrenarche
  4. D. Delayed puberty
  5. E. An androgen-secreting adrenal tumour
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Normal onset of puberty

In boys, puberty begins with testicular enlargement to 4 mL or more (Prader orchidometer), usually between 9 and 14 years, followed by pubic hair, penile growth and the growth spurt, which peaks late at about 13.5 years when testicular volume is 10 to 12 mL. In girls, breast budding (thelarche) is the first sign, usually between 8 and 13 years, with menarche about 2 to 2.5 years later and peak height velocity early in puberty. Pubic hair without testicular enlargement is adrenarche. Precocious puberty is onset before 8 years in girls and before 9 years in boys; delayed puberty is no testicular enlargement by 14 years in boys and no breast development by 13 years in girls.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Precocious puberty: Precocious puberty in boys means testicular enlargement before 9 years; he is 11, within the normal range of 9 to 14 years.
  • C. Premature adrenarche: Adrenarche gives pubic hair and odour with testes still under 4 mL, and occurs before 9 years; his testes of 5 mL at 11 years show gonadal activation.
  • D. Delayed puberty: Delay in boys means no testicular enlargement beyond 3 mL by 14 years; his testes are already 5 mL and he is growing at a normal rate.
  • E. An androgen-secreting adrenal tumour: This produces rapid virilisation with pubic hair, acne and penile growth while the testes stay small; his testes are 5 mL, progress is gradual and growth is normal.

Key point: Puberty in boys starts with testes of 4 mL or more between 9 and 14 years, and in girls with breast budding between 8 and 13 years.

Question 6 · Paediatric haematology and oncology · easy

A 4-year-old girl from Abbottabad is brought because of bruises on her legs and tiny red spots over her trunk for 3 days. She had a viral cold 2 weeks ago. She is playing normally, has no nosebleeds, gum bleeding or blood in urine or stool, takes no medicines and has no fever, bone pain or weight loss. Examination shows scattered petechiae and bruises but no lymph nodes or organ enlargement. Platelets are 12,000/µL (150,000 to 450,000), haemoglobin 12.1 g/dL (11.5 to 14.5) and white cells 7,800/µL with a normal differential.

Which is the most appropriate management?

  1. A. Immediate platelet transfusion
  2. B. Splenectomy
  3. C. Bone marrow aspiration before any decision
  4. D. Observation with advice
  5. E. Prednisolone for 4 weeks
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. Observation with advice

Immune thrombocytopenia (ITP) in childhood is typically acute, follows a viral illness by 1 to 4 weeks and presents with petechiae and bruising in a well child with an isolated low platelet count, otherwise normal blood count and film, and no lymphadenopathy or organ enlargement. About two thirds recover within 3 months, and 80% or more within a year. The ASH 2019 guideline suggests observation rather than corticosteroids, IVIG or anti-D immunoglobulin for children with newly diagnosed ITP who have no bleeding or only mild (skin) bleeding, whatever the platelet count. Parents are advised to avoid contact sports, aspirin and intramuscular injections, and to return urgently for headache, vomiting, mucosal bleeding or head injury.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Immediate platelet transfusion: Transfusion is for life-threatening bleeding; she has only skin bleeding, and transfused platelets would be destroyed rapidly by her antibodies.
  • B. Splenectomy: Splenectomy is considered in chronic, refractory disease after at least 12 months; this is a new presentation with only mild skin bleeding.
  • C. Bone marrow aspiration before any decision: Marrow examination is not needed when the history, examination and blood count are typical, and she has no features of leukaemia.
  • E. Prednisolone for 4 weeks: ASH advises against prolonged steroid courses in children, as adverse effects outweigh benefit; and she has only mild, skin-limited bleeding.

Key point: A well child with new ITP and no or only mild bleeding is observed, whatever the platelet count (ASH 2019).

Question 7 · Infections, immunisation and IMNCI · medium

A nurse at a rural dispensary in Hunza sees a 3-year-old girl with 2 days of fever and cough. She is drowsy and hard to rouse, and her mother says she has been unable to swallow anything today. Temperature is 38.9 °C, respiratory rate 48/min, pulse 140/min, and there is mild chest indrawing. The district hospital in Gilgit is three hours away by jeep and the family agrees to go. The dispensary stocks injectable antibiotics, ORS and paracetamol.

What is the most appropriate action now?

  1. A. Give pre-referral treatment, then refer urgently
  2. B. Give oral amoxicillin at home and review in two days
  3. C. Give paracetamol and ORS and review next morning
  4. D. Advise fluids and rest, and return if fever lasts five days
  5. E. Refer only after the fever has settled with paracetamol
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Give pre-referral treatment, then refer urgently

A child with any IMNCI general danger sign is classified as having very severe disease and needs urgent referral. Referral should never be delayed to complete treatment, but pre-referral treatment is given first when it can be done quickly: the first dose of an appropriate antibiotic (intramuscular or intravenous where the child cannot swallow), treatment of low blood sugar if suspected, paracetamol for high fever, and, in malaria-risk areas, an antimalarial. The caregiver is taught to keep the child warm and to give small sips of fluid or breastmilk on the way. Communication with the receiving hospital helps. Home treatment and review are for children without danger signs.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Give oral amoxicillin at home and review in two days: She cannot swallow, so oral treatment is impossible, and a drowsy child with danger signs needs urgent hospital care, not home review after two days.
  • C. Give paracetamol and ORS and review next morning: Delaying to the next morning is unsafe in a drowsy child who cannot swallow; ORS cannot be taken orally, and her chest indrawing needs antibiotics and hospital care.
  • D. Advise fluids and rest, and return if fever lasts five days: A drowsy child who cannot swallow has severe illness; waiting five days risks death from sepsis or severe pneumonia in a place three hours from hospital.
  • E. Refer only after the fever has settled with paracetamol: Waiting for the fever to settle delays transfer; fever control does not treat the cause, and her drowsiness and inability to swallow are danger signs requiring immediate referral.

Key point: A child with a general danger sign receives pre-referral treatment, such as the first antibiotic dose, and is referred urgently without delay.

Question 8 · Paediatric orthopaedics, rheumatology and dermatology · medium

A 6-week-old girl is seen in Skardu for a routine check. She is the first child, was born by caesarean section for breech presentation at 38 weeks, and is swaddled with her legs held straight. The left leg looks slightly shorter. When the left hip is flexed to 90 degrees and gently abducted, a clunk is felt as the head moves into the socket. Hip ultrasound shows a left alpha angle of 48 degrees (60 degrees or more is normal), with the femoral head partly uncovered.

What is the most appropriate management?

  1. A. Observe and repeat the examination at 6 months
  2. B. Immediate open reduction under general anaesthesia
  3. C. Hip spica cast for 12 weeks
  4. D. Pavlik harness
  5. E. Change nappies and stop swaddling only
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. Pavlik harness

Developmental dysplasia of the hip covers a spectrum from a shallow acetabulum to subluxation and dislocation. Risk factors are female sex, breech presentation, family history, oligohydramnios, firstborn status and tight swaddling with the hips extended, which is common in some communities. All newborns are examined with the Barlow (provoking dislocation) and Ortolani (reducing a dislocated hip with a clunk) manoeuvres, and infants with risk factors or abnormal examination have ultrasound at 4 to 6 weeks (before the femoral head ossifies at about 4 to 6 months). A reducible hip under 6 months is treated in a Pavlik harness, which holds the hips flexed and abducted, with ultrasound follow-up. Failed harness treatment leads to closed or open reduction and spica casting. Late presentation brings a limp, a short leg and waddling gait.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Observe and repeat the examination at 6 months: Waiting allows the unstable hip to stay out of the socket, and delayed treatment at that age would need much more difficult correction.
  • B. Immediate open reduction under general anaesthesia: Surgery is for hips that fail splinting or present late; at 6 weeks a reducible hip responds to non-operative treatment.
  • C. Hip spica cast for 12 weeks: A spica cast is used when harness treatment fails or the hip is dislocated and irreducible; her hip reduces with the manoeuvre.
  • E. Change nappies and stop swaddling only: Loose swaddling helps prevention, but this infant already has an abnormal alpha angle and an unstable hip that needs active splinting.

Key point: A reducible dysplastic hip in an infant under 6 months is treated with a Pavlik harness; late diagnosis needs surgery.

Question 9 · Neonatology · medium

A boy is born at 39 weeks by normal delivery in a district hospital in Charsadda, through clear liquor. He is limp and not breathing. He is dried with a warm towel, the wet towel is removed, his head is placed in a neutral position and his back is rubbed, but after 30 seconds he is still not breathing. His heart rate by stethoscope is 80/min.

What is the most appropriate next step?

  1. A. Chest compressions at 3 to 1
  2. B. Adrenaline through the umbilical vein
  3. C. Bag-mask ventilation in air
  4. D. Free-flow oxygen by the face
  5. E. Intubation to suction the trachea
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Bag-mask ventilation in air

Newborn resuscitation follows a fixed order: dry, keep warm and stimulate; then, if the baby is apnoeic, gasping or has a heart rate below 100/min, open the airway and give positive-pressure ventilation by bag and mask within the first minute (the "golden minute"). Most babies who do not breathe at birth need only effective lung inflation, and the heart rate is the best sign that ventilation is working. Term and near-term babies are ventilated in air (21% oxygen), because high oxygen at birth adds oxidative injury; oxygen is titrated to pulse oximetry targets. Chest compressions are added only if the heart rate stays below 60/min after 30 seconds of effective ventilation, and adrenaline only if it stays below 60/min despite both.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Chest compressions at 3 to 1: Compressions start only if the heart rate stays below 60/min after 30 seconds of good ventilation; his rate is 80/min and he has had none.
  • B. Adrenaline through the umbilical vein: Adrenaline is for a heart rate below 60/min despite effective ventilation and compressions; he has not yet been ventilated at all.
  • D. Free-flow oxygen by the face: Free-flow oxygen does nothing for a baby who is not breathing; he needs breaths delivered to his lungs.
  • E. Intubation to suction the trachea: The liquor was clear, and suctioning the trachea delays ventilation; bag and mask is the first step for apnoea.

Key point: A newborn who is apnoeic or has a heart rate below 100/min after drying and stimulation needs bag-mask ventilation, in air at term.

Question 10 · Paediatric neurology · medium

A 3-year-old girl from Mingora, Swat, has a convulsion on the first evening of a chest infection with a temperature of 39.6 °C. Her parents describe generalised stiffening and rhythmic jerking of all four limbs that lasted about 20 minutes and stopped only when she was given a medicine by the doctor on arrival. She has had no previous seizures, her development is normal and she has no family history of seizures. She recovers to normal behaviour over two hours, and examination of the nervous system is normal.

Which feature classifies this as a complex febrile seizure?

  1. A. Duration of 20 minutes
  2. B. Generalised stiffening and jerking of all four limbs
  3. C. Onset on the first evening of the infection
  4. D. Age of 3 years
  5. E. Peak temperature of 39.6 °C
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Duration of 20 minutes

Febrile seizures are classed as simple or complex. A simple seizure is generalised, lasts less than 15 minutes and does not recur within 24 hours. A complex seizure has one or more of: duration over 15 minutes, focal onset or focal features, or recurrence within 24 hours (or within the same febrile illness). About one in five to one in three febrile seizures is complex. Complex features, an abnormal neurological state before the seizure and a family history of epilepsy raise the risk of later epilepsy. A seizure lasting longer than 5 minutes is treated as an emergency in the same way as any other prolonged seizure, so she needed prompt benzodiazepine treatment, and a child with a prolonged seizure needs observation and a search for the cause.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Generalised stiffening and jerking of all four limbs: A generalised tonic-clonic pattern is typical of a simple febrile seizure; only focal onset or focal features would make it complex.
  • C. Onset on the first evening of the infection: Seizures commonly appear in the first hours of fever, so early timing in her illness is characteristic of simple febrile seizures and not a complex feature.
  • D. Age of 3 years: Febrile seizures occur between 6 months and 5 years, with a peak at 12 to 18 months; at 3 years she is well within the usual range.
  • E. Peak temperature of 39.6 °C: Febrile seizures happen at many temperature levels; a fever of 39.6 °C does not define complexity, and a lower fever is the one linked with recurrence.

Key point: A complex febrile seizure lasts over 15 minutes, is focal, or recurs within 24 hours.
