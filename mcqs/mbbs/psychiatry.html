Question 1 · Mood, anxiety and stress disorders · easy

A 31-year-old man is brought to the emergency department of a hospital in Gwadar by his brother, who found him unconscious in a locked room beside an empty strip of sleeping tablets and a handwritten note. After medical stabilisation he is alert and oriented. He tells the doctor that he planned this for two weeks, gave away his motorcycle and is sorry to have been found. He says he will try again as soon as he is home. He lost his job two months ago. Pulse is 80/min, blood pressure is 120/76 mmHg and he is medically fit for psychiatric review.

What is the most appropriate next step?

  1. A. Admit under close observation
  2. B. Discharge with outpatient review in 1 week
  3. C. Discharge with a written safety plan only
  4. D. Refer to a counsellor within a month
  5. E. Start an SSRI and discharge
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Admit under close observation

Risk after self-harm is judged from the intent behind the act, planning, preparations (a note, giving away possessions), persisting wish to die, intent to repeat, access to means, hopelessness and protective factors, not from a single checklist. This man had a planned attempt with concealment, regrets surviving and says he will try again, so he is at high imminent risk and needs admission with close observation and treatment of any underlying depression or substance use. Involve his family in safety planning and means restriction. Stigma may make families reluctant to accept admission, so explain the risks clearly and respectfully. He should not be sent home unsupervised even where beds are scarce.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Discharge with outpatient review in 1 week: Discharge with a one-week visit is unsafe; he has persisting intent, a prior planned attempt and says he will try again immediately after leaving hospital.
  • C. Discharge with a written safety plan only: A safety plan alone is inadequate when intent persists just after a planned attempt; supervision and treatment of the underlying illness are required first.
  • D. Refer to a counsellor within a month: Counselling in a month ignores his current high risk; he needs urgent psychiatric assessment and protection from immediate repetition, not delayed supportive therapy.
  • E. Start an SSRI and discharge: An SSRI takes weeks to work and gives no protection during the next days of high intent; discharge now leaves him unsupervised with a stated plan to repeat.

Key point: Identify features of high imminent suicide risk after an attempt and the need for admission.

Question 2 · Personality, development, therapies and law · medium

A 52-year-old truck driver from Sibi is brought by his wife after he nearly crashed twice on the highway. For two years he has snored loudly, she has seen him stop breathing in his sleep, and he wakes with headaches and a dry mouth. He falls asleep during conversations. Epworth Sleepiness Scale score is 17/24, BMI is 37 kg/m², neck circumference is 45 cm and blood pressure is 156/98 mmHg. He has no cataplexy and drinks no alcohol.

Which investigation is most appropriate?

  1. A. Overnight polysomnography
  2. B. Multiple sleep latency test
  3. C. Thyroid function tests alone
  4. D. Routine EEG
  5. E. CT brain
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Overnight polysomnography

Obstructive sleep apnoea causes repeated upper airway collapse during sleep, with loud snoring, witnessed apnoeas, unrefreshing sleep, morning headache and excessive daytime sleepiness, and is linked to obesity, hypertension and road traffic accidents. An Epworth score above 10 indicates abnormal sleepiness, and a neck circumference above about 43 cm in men is a risk marker. Diagnosis needs a sleep study, either overnight polysomnography or respiratory polygraphy. Treatment is weight loss, avoiding alcohol and sedatives, and continuous positive airway pressure. A professional driver with near-misses should stop driving until treated. The multiple sleep latency test is for narcolepsy. Sleep laboratories are few in Pakistan, so referral to tertiary centres or home oximetry may be needed.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Multiple sleep latency test: The multiple sleep latency test evaluates narcolepsy and idiopathic hypersomnia; he has no cataplexy, with loud snoring, witnessed apnoeas and obesity pointing first to a breathing disorder.
  • C. Thyroid function tests alone: Hypothyroidism can add to sleepiness and snoring, but it would not by itself explain witnessed apnoeas; a sleep study is the diagnostic test for his likely condition.
  • D. Routine EEG: Routine EEG evaluates seizures; his sleepiness comes with snoring and witnessed apnoeas, and an awake EEG would not diagnose sleep-disordered breathing.
  • E. CT brain: CT brain looks for structural lesions; he has no focal neurological signs, and loud snoring, apnoeas and obesity indicate obstructive sleep apnoea needing a sleep study.

Key point: Investigate suspected obstructive sleep apnoea with a sleep study and advise on driving.

Question 3 · Psychosis, organic and substance disorders · easy

A geriatric team at a district hospital in Hangu wants to reduce delirium among older patients admitted with hip fractures, after noticing that many become confused after surgery. The ward is noisy at night, patients often lack glasses and hearing aids, and relatives are asked to leave during the evening. Staff suggest several measures to the consultant.

Which measure is most likely to reduce the incidence of delirium?

  1. A. Multicomponent non-drug care
  2. B. Prophylactic haloperidol
  3. C. Prophylactic lorazepam
  4. D. Routine physical restraint
  5. E. Prophylactic donepezil
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Multicomponent non-drug care

Multicomponent, non-drug interventions delivered by a trained team are the best-supported way to prevent delirium in at-risk older patients. They include reorientation with clocks and familiar people, glasses and hearing aids, adequate hydration and nutrition, sleep hygiene with reduced night-time noise, early mobilisation, pain control, avoiding unnecessary catheters and sedatives, and reviewing medicines. Prophylactic haloperidol, benzodiazepines and cholinesterase inhibitors do not reduce incidence and may harm, and restraint worsens agitation. Allowing relatives to stay is consistent with Pakistani family practice and helps orientation. Simple changes cost little and suit district hospitals with limited staff.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Prophylactic haloperidol: Routine haloperidol does not prevent delirium and adds sedation, falls and QT risk; it is reserved for distressing symptoms once delirium has developed.
  • C. Prophylactic lorazepam: Benzodiazepines are deliriogenic and raise fall risk in older patients; giving lorazepam routinely would increase delirium rather than prevent it.
  • D. Routine physical restraint: Physical restraint increases agitation, injury and duration of delirium; orientation, sleep and sensory aids are more effective on this ward.
  • E. Prophylactic donepezil: Cholinesterase inhibitors have not been shown to prevent delirium after surgery; donepezil would add side effects without proven benefit.

Key point: Select multicomponent non-drug interventions as the best way to prevent delirium.

Question 4 · Mood, anxiety and stress disorders · medium

A 31-year-old woman who lives in a village near Bannu has taken paroxetine 40 mg daily for five months for depression that had fully remitted. She ran out of tablets four days ago and could not reach a pharmacy. She now has dizziness, nausea, electric-shock sensations in her limbs, vivid dreams and irritability. Temperature is 36.9 °C, pulse is 84/min and blood pressure is 116/72 mmHg. Pupils, tone and reflexes are normal and there is no clonus.

What is the most likely diagnosis?

  1. A. Early relapse of depression
  2. B. Serotonin syndrome
  3. C. Discontinuation syndrome
  4. D. Generalised anxiety disorder
  5. E. Viral labyrinthitis
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Discontinuation syndrome

Antidepressant discontinuation syndrome follows abrupt stopping or missed doses, usually within two to four days, and is commonest with short half-life drugs such as paroxetine and venlafaxine. Typical features are dizziness, nausea, sensory disturbances such as electric shocks, vivid dreams, anxiety and irritability, lasting days to a few weeks. Serotonin syndrome shows clonus, hyperreflexia, tremor and fever. Management is to restart the same drug, or a longer-acting SSRI, and to taper slowly when stopping, as NICE advises. In Pakistan supply interruptions and stopping once the patient feels well are common, so counselling about adherence and gradual withdrawal is important.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Early relapse of depression: Relapse emerges gradually over weeks with low mood and loss of interest; her abrupt onset within days of stopping, with dizziness and sensory symptoms, argues against it.
  • B. Serotonin syndrome: Serotonin syndrome needs excess serotonergic drive with clonus, hyperreflexia, agitation and fever; she has stopped the drug and has normal tone, reflexes and temperature.
  • D. Generalised anxiety disorder: Generalised anxiety disorder develops over months with persistent worry; it does not explain shock-like sensations starting days after abruptly stopping a short half-life drug.
  • E. Viral labyrinthitis: Viral labyrinthitis causes vertigo and nausea after an infection, not electric-shock sensations, vivid dreams or irritability; she has no fever or infective prodrome.

Key point: Recognise antidepressant discontinuation syndrome and distinguish it from relapse and serotonin syndrome.

Question 5 · Personality, development, therapies and law · easy

A 16-year-old girl from Swat is brought to a clinic by her mother. Over eight months she has lost a large amount of weight and her periods stopped five months ago. She is terrified of gaining weight, believes she is fat despite being thin, eats very little and exercises for hours daily. She has no binge eating or vomiting. Her BMI is 15.1 kg/m² (below the 2nd centile for age), pulse is 46/min, blood pressure is 86/54 mmHg and there is fine downy lanugo hair on her back. TSH is 1.9 mIU/L (0.4-4.0).

What is the most likely diagnosis?

  1. A. Anorexia nervosa
  2. B. Bulimia nervosa
  3. C. Hyperthyroidism
  4. D. Major depressive episode
  5. E. Coeliac disease
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Anorexia nervosa

ICD-11 anorexia nervosa requires significantly low body weight for height, age and developmental stage (BMI below 18.5 kg/m² in adults, below the 5th centile in children), a persistent pattern of restricted eating or other behaviours that prevent weight gain, and a preoccupation with body weight or shape, often with fear of gaining weight. Physical signs include bradycardia, hypotension, hypothermia, lanugo and amenorrhoea. Assess medical risk, including ECG and electrolytes, and decide whether admission is needed. For adolescents, NICE recommends family-based treatment. In Pakistan eating disorders are under-recognised, often being attributed to dieting or physical illness, so ask directly about body image and involve the whole family.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Bulimia nervosa: Bulimia nervosa involves recurrent binge eating with compensatory behaviours and usually normal or higher weight; she has a very low BMI, no bingeing or vomiting, and prominent restriction.
  • C. Hyperthyroidism: Hyperthyroidism causes weight loss with tachycardia, heat intolerance and tremor; she has bradycardia (pulse 46/min), a normal TSH of 1.9 mIU/L and a fear of gaining weight.
  • D. Major depressive episode: Depression may reduce appetite, but she has a fear of weight gain, distorted body image and active restriction with excessive exercise, which define an eating disorder.
  • E. Coeliac disease: Coeliac disease causes malabsorption and weight loss without intense fear of fatness, body-image distortion or deliberate restriction and exercise as seen here.

Key point: Diagnose anorexia nervosa from low BMI, fear of weight gain and physical signs of starvation.

Question 6 · Psychosis, organic and substance disorders · medium

An 80-year-old woman is on the orthopaedic ward of a hospital in Abbottabad two days after surgery for a hip fracture. Yesterday she was cheerful and chatty. Today she is drowsy at times, then agitated, picks at the bedclothes and says that children are climbing on her bed at night. She cannot say the day or the place and loses track of questions within seconds. Temperature is 37.9 °C, pulse is 96/min, SpO2 is 94% on room air, white cells are 13,200/µL and urine shows nitrites and leucocytes. Her family report normal memory and independence before the fracture.

What is the most likely diagnosis?

  1. A. Dementia with Lewy bodies
  2. B. Delirium
  3. C. Late-onset schizophrenia
  4. D. Depressive pseudodementia
  5. E. Alzheimer disease
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Delirium

Delirium is an acute disturbance of attention and awareness that develops over hours to days and fluctuates, with additional cognitive disturbance such as disorientation, memory loss or perceptual disturbance, and a medical cause. It is very common after hip fracture surgery in older people. Causes here include infection, pain, hypoxia, medication and urinary retention, so look for and treat each. The hypoactive form is often missed, so screen with a tool such as the Confusion Assessment Method. The history of normal memory before the fracture and the abrupt onset exclude dementia as the main explanation. In Pakistan family members at the bedside often provide valuable reorientation and should be encouraged.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Dementia with Lewy bodies: Dementia with Lewy bodies progresses over months with parkinsonism and fluctuations; her change was abrupt within a day after surgery and her memory was normal before the fracture.
  • C. Late-onset schizophrenia: Late-onset schizophrenia is a chronic psychosis in clear consciousness; she has fluctuating drowsiness, inattention and disorientation with a urinary infection and recent surgery.
  • D. Depressive pseudodementia: Depressive pseudodementia has low mood with effortful, apathetic answers and no fluctuating consciousness; she is drowsy then agitated with visual hallucinations.
  • E. Alzheimer disease: Alzheimer disease causes progressive memory decline over years in clear consciousness; her normal memory before the fracture and abrupt change indicate an acute organic state.

Key point: Recognise delirium from acute fluctuating inattention and disorientation with a medical precipitant.

Question 7 · Mood, anxiety and stress disorders · easy

A 34-year-old farmer from a village in Lower Dir is brought to a district hospital clinic by his brother. For six weeks he has felt persistently low, has lost interest in tending his fields, wakes at 3 a.m. and cannot return to sleep, and has lost 5 kg in weight. He says he is a burden to his family and cannot concentrate on conversation. The family first took him to a faith healer. He takes no medicines and denies alcohol or drug use. He has never had a period of unusually elevated mood. Examination is normal and TSH is 2.1 mIU/L (0.4-4.0).

What is the most likely diagnosis?

  1. A. Dysthymia
  2. B. Adjustment disorder
  3. C. Depressive episode
  4. D. Bipolar II disorder
  5. E. Generalised anxiety disorder
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Depressive episode

ICD-11 diagnoses a depressive episode when at least five symptoms from a list of ten, including depressed mood or loss of interest, last at least two weeks with functional impairment, after bipolar disorder and organic causes are excluded. This man has low mood, lost interest, early waking, weight loss, guilt and poor concentration for six weeks, a normal thyroid test and no history of mania. Dysthymia needs two years of symptoms, and adjustment disorder follows a stressor with milder symptoms. In Pakistan many patients first see faith healers or present with somatic complaints, so a short structured symptom review in primary care, as taught in the WHO mhGAP guide, allows earlier diagnosis and treatment.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Dysthymia: Dysthymia requires at least two years of low mood; his illness is only six weeks old and includes marked weight loss and early waking.
  • B. Adjustment disorder: Adjustment disorder follows an identifiable stressor with milder symptoms; here no stressor is given and he has the full picture of low mood, early waking and weight loss.
  • D. Bipolar II disorder: Bipolar II needs a past hypomanic episode; he has never had unusually elevated mood, so a unipolar depressive illness is more likely.
  • E. Generalised anxiety disorder: Generalised anxiety disorder means months of excessive worry with tension; he describes low mood, lost interest and self-blame rather than apprehension.

Key point: Diagnose a depressive episode and distinguish it from dysthymia, adjustment disorder and bipolar disorder.

Question 8 · Personality, development, therapies and law · easy

An 8-year-old boy from Charsadda is brought to a paediatric clinic by his mother because his teacher says he cannot sit still, calls out in class, loses his belongings and rarely finishes work. The same behaviour occurs at home, at the mosque school and when playing with cousins, and has been present since he was four. He is affectionate, makes eye contact and plays imaginatively. Hearing, vision and growth are normal, there are no staring spells and his general ability is average.

What is the most likely diagnosis?

  1. A. Oppositional defiant disorder
  2. B. ADHD
  3. C. Autism spectrum disorder
  4. D. Absence epilepsy
  5. E. Specific learning disorder
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. ADHD

ICD-11 attention deficit hyperactivity disorder is a persistent pattern of inattention and/or hyperactivity-impulsivity with onset during the developmental period, typically early to mid childhood, present in more than one setting and causing impairment; DSM-5 requires onset before 12 years and at least six months of symptoms. Exclude hearing and vision problems, epilepsy, autism, learning disorders, anxiety and abuse. NICE advises parent training and environmental changes first, with medication, usually methylphenidate, for persistent significant impairment in school-age children. In Pakistan affected children are often labelled naughty or lazy and punished, so explaining the condition to parents and teachers is a key first step.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Oppositional defiant disorder: Oppositional defiant disorder shows persistent anger, defiance and rule-breaking towards authority; he is affectionate, and his problems are inattention, restlessness and impulsivity across all settings.
  • C. Autism spectrum disorder: Autism involves deficits in social communication and restricted repetitive behaviours; he makes eye contact and plays imaginatively, so social reciprocity appears intact.
  • D. Absence epilepsy: Absence seizures cause brief staring spells with unresponsiveness and spike-wave EEG; he has no staring spells and his difficulties are pervasive hyperactivity and impulsivity.
  • E. Specific learning disorder: Specific learning disorder is difficulty with reading, writing or arithmetic despite adequate teaching and ability; his problem is inattention and hyperactivity in all settings, not one academic skill.

Key point: Diagnose ADHD from pervasive inattention, hyperactivity and impulsivity with early onset.

Question 9 · Psychosis, organic and substance disorders · easy

A 24-year-old man from Mardan is brought to a clinic by his uncle. For eight months he has heard two voices discussing him in the third person and commenting on what he is doing. He believes a neighbour is removing his thoughts from his head by radio waves. He has stopped bathing, keeps his shop closed and rarely speaks, with a blunted expression. He denies low or elevated mood, urine drug screen is negative, and examination and CT head are normal.

What is the most likely diagnosis?

  1. A. Brief psychotic disorder
  2. B. Schizophrenia
  3. C. Delusional disorder
  4. D. Schizoaffective disorder
  5. E. Cannabis-induced psychosis
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Schizophrenia

ICD-11 diagnoses schizophrenia when at least two core symptoms are present for at least one month: persistent delusions, persistent hallucinations, disorganised thinking, experiences of influence, passivity or control, negative symptoms, grossly disorganised behaviour or psychomotor disturbance. At least one must be a delusion, hallucination or disorganised thinking, and the picture must not be due to substances, a medical condition or a primary mood disorder. This man has third-person commenting voices, thought withdrawal and negative symptoms over eight months, a negative drug screen and no mood syndrome. In Pakistan families often seek faith healers first, so untreated psychosis is long; early antipsychotic treatment with family support improves outcome.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Brief psychotic disorder: Brief psychotic episodes resolve within weeks to three months; his symptoms have lasted eight months with progressive withdrawal and self-neglect.
  • C. Delusional disorder: Delusional disorder has delusions without prominent hallucinations or negative symptoms; he has third-person voices, thought removal and marked social withdrawal.
  • D. Schizoaffective disorder: Schizoaffective disorder requires prominent mood episodes alongside psychotic symptoms; he denies low or elevated mood and has no mood episode.
  • E. Cannabis-induced psychosis: Substance-induced psychosis occurs during or soon after intoxication and settles within weeks of abstinence; his urine drug screen is negative and symptoms have persisted for eight months.

Key point: Diagnose schizophrenia from characteristic positive and negative symptoms lasting more than one month.

Question 10 · Mood, anxiety and stress disorders · easy

A 41-year-old shopkeeper from Mardan attends his family doctor because he has felt flat and tired on most days for about three years. He reports poor self-esteem, difficulty making decisions and little hope for the future, and says his longest good spell lasted about three weeks. He continues to run his shop and has not missed work. He has not had episodes of raised mood or overactivity and takes no regular medicines. Examination and full blood count are normal and TSH is 2.4 mIU/L (0.4-4.0).

What is the most likely diagnosis?

  1. A. Cyclothymia
  2. B. Depressive episode
  3. C. Adjustment disorder
  4. D. Generalised anxiety disorder
  5. E. Dysthymia
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Dysthymia

Dysthymia (persistent depressive disorder in ICD-11) is chronic low mood lasting at least two years, with fatigue, low self-esteem, poor decision-making and hopelessness, usually milder than a depressive episode and with few symptom-free intervals. This man has three years of unremitting mild symptoms, preserved functioning, no manic history and a normal thyroid test. Depressive episodes are time-limited and more severe, adjustment disorder follows a stressor, and cyclothymia involves mood elevation. Treatment is psychological therapy such as CBT, an SSRI, or both, and benefit often needs longer courses. In Pakistan stigma often delays care for such chronic, quieter presentations.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Cyclothymia: Cyclothymia involves chronic alternation between mild highs and lows; he has never had episodes of raised mood or overactivity, so a bipolar spectrum disorder does not fit.
  • B. Depressive episode: A depressive episode is usually time-limited and more severe; his symptoms are mild and unremitting for years while he still runs his shop without missing work.
  • C. Adjustment disorder: Adjustment disorder arises within weeks of an identifiable stressor and settles within months; no stressor is described and his low mood has persisted for three years.
  • D. Generalised anxiety disorder: Generalised anxiety disorder shows widespread worry with tension and autonomic symptoms; he describes flat mood, fatigue and hopelessness rather than anxious apprehension.

Key point: Recognise dysthymia as chronic, mild, persistent depression lasting at least two years.
