Forensic medicine MCQs for MBBS

Question 1 · Legal procedure, identification and medical ethics · medium

A 38-year-old man with a deep stab wound of the abdomen is admitted to a district hospital in Mirpur. Pulse is 130/min, blood pressure is 80/48 mmHg, and haemoglobin is 7.2 g/dL (13-17). The surgeon plans emergency laparotomy and warns that he may not survive. He is conscious and oriented, and he asks to say who attacked him. No magistrate is available in the hospital, and the police officer is still on the way.

What should the doctor do about his request?

  1. A. Refuse, since only a magistrate may record it
  2. B. Certify fitness, then record his exact words
  3. C. Wait and let police record it after surgery
  4. D. Ask only yes or no questions about a suspect
  5. E. Record a short summary in medical language
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Certify fitness, then record his exact words

A dying declaration is a statement by a person about the cause or circumstances of his own death, made when he expects to die. In Pakistan it is admissible under the Qanun-e-Shahadat Order, 1984, even though the maker cannot be cross-examined, because a dying person is presumed unlikely to lie. If the patient may die soon, it should be taken without delay, preferably by a magistrate, but if none can come in time the doctor records it. The doctor first certifies that the patient is conscious, oriented and fit to speak, takes it down in his exact words, avoids leading questions, has it witnessed, and obtains the signature or thumb impression with the time, adding a note that he remained fit. Courts treat such statements with care, so doubts about fitness or tutoring weaken them.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Refuse, since only a magistrate may record it: A magistrate is preferred, but if none can come in time the doctor may record it; refusing could lose evidence from a man at high risk of dying.
  • C. Wait and let police record it after surgery: He may die during or after surgery, so deferring risks losing the statement; it should be recorded now while he is fit and conscious.
  • D. Ask only yes or no questions about a suspect: Leading questions that suggest a suspect reduce the value of the statement; it should come from his own account in answer to neutral, open questions.
  • E. Record a short summary in medical language: A summary replaces his words with those of the doctor; the statement must be taken down as spoken, in his own words, in question-and-answer form where needed.

Key point: Describe how a dying declaration is recorded by a doctor.

Question 2 · Death, injuries and forensic pathology · medium

A 42-year-old man is found dying at the edge of a road near Sukkur after being struck by a vehicle at dusk. There were no witnesses. He has a transverse fracture of the right tibia 45 cm above the heel, with a triangular wedge of bone separated from the shaft. The base of the wedge lies on the back of the leg and its apex points to the front. A crescentic bruise with abrasion crosses the back of the calf at the same level, and the skin is otherwise intact.

From which direction was he struck?

  1. A. From the front
  2. B. From his left
  3. C. From his right
  4. D. From behind
  5. E. From above (run over)
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. From behind

Pedestrian injuries are produced in stages: the bumper strikes the legs, the body is thrown onto the bonnet or windscreen, and finally it falls to the ground. A bumper fracture of the tibia or fibula is usually transverse and often shows a triangular wedge (butterfly) fragment. The base of the wedge lies on the side that was struck, and its apex points in the direction in which the force travelled. The bruise and abrasion on the skin lie over the point of impact. The height of the fracture above the heel, measured at autopsy, gives an estimate of the bumper height, which can be compared with the suspect vehicle. In this man the wedge base and bruise lie on the back of the leg, so the force came from behind.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. From the front: A frontal strike puts the base of the wedge on the front of the tibia, with its apex pointing backward; here the base and bruise lie on the back.
  • B. From his left: A strike from his left would place the bruise and wedge base on a side of the leg; here both lie on the back of the calf.
  • C. From his right: A strike from his right would leave a bruise and wedge base on the outer side of the right leg; instead, both lie on the back of the calf.
  • E. From above (run over): A wheel run-over shows tyre patterns and extensive crushing across the limb; here a transverse fracture with a bruise on the back of the calf suggests a bumper strike.

Key point: Use a wedge fragment to determine the direction of impact in a pedestrian bumper fracture.

Question 3 · Toxicology · easy

A 24-year-old woman is brought to a district hospital in Mirpur after swallowing about 30 tablets of a common pain and fever medicine (500 mg each) following a family quarrel. She took them 9 hours ago and has had mild nausea only. She is alert, with pulse 92/min, blood pressure 118/76 mmHg and a normal examination. The serum level of the drug at 9 hours is 90 mg/L, well above the treatment line for that time. ALT is 28 U/L (7-40) and INR is 1.0.

Which treatment should be started now?

  1. A. Activated charcoal by mouth
  2. B. Fomepizole infusion
  3. C. Urgent haemodialysis
  4. D. N-acetylcysteine
  5. E. Forced alkaline diuresis
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. N-acetylcysteine

Paracetamol overdose is silent at first: nausea may be the only symptom, and liver injury appears after 24 to 72 hours. N-acetylcysteine replenishes glutathione and is given when the level lies above the treatment line, when more than about 150 mg/kg was taken, or when the time of ingestion is unknown. It works best within 8 hours but still helps later, so treatment is not withheld at 9 hours. The usual regimen is a 21-hour three-bag intravenous infusion, with ALT, INR and creatinine monitored. Charcoal is useful only within about an hour. Because her ingestion was intentional she also needs psychiatric assessment before discharge. In Pakistan paracetamol is sold over the counter in large packs, which is why overdose is common.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Activated charcoal by mouth: Charcoal works best within about an hour of ingestion; at 9 hours little tablet remains in the gut, so it is not the priority for this patient.
  • B. Fomepizole infusion: Fomepizole blocks alcohol dehydrogenase in methanol or ethylene glycol poisoning; it has no action on this drug, which damages the liver through a toxic metabolite.
  • C. Urgent haemodialysis: Dialysis is kept for massive overdose with acidosis, coma or very high levels; she is alert with normal ALT and INR, so it is not needed.
  • E. Forced alkaline diuresis: Alkaline diuresis speeds elimination of salicylates, not this drug, and it risks fluid overload and electrolyte disturbance without helping her liver.

Key point: Select N-acetylcysteine for a late paracetamol overdose with a level above the treatment line.

Question 4 · Legal procedure, identification and medical ethics · medium

A 50-year-old man undergoes inguinal hernia repair under spinal anaesthesia at a private hospital in Larkana. The operating team does not review the pre-operative blood tests, which show platelets 38,000/µL (150,000-400,000). Surgery is uneventful with no bleeding, and there is no spinal haematoma or neurological deficit. Three weeks later he dies suddenly from a massive myocardial infarction, and a previously unknown severe coronary stenosis is found at autopsy. His family files a complaint of negligence against the operating team.

Which element of negligence is missing from the family claim?

  1. A. Duty of care owed to the patient
  2. B. Breach of the expected standard of care
  3. C. Damage suffered by the patient
  4. D. Causal link between breach and harm
  5. E. Consent of the patient to surgery
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. Causal link between breach and harm

To succeed in negligence a patient must prove four elements: a duty of care owed by the doctor, a breach of the standard expected of a reasonably competent doctor, damage, and a causal link between the breach and the damage. Here the team accepted the patient and so owed a duty, and giving spinal anaesthesia without reviewing a platelet count of 38,000/µL probably breached the standard, because it exposes the patient to spinal haematoma. The patient also died, which is damage. However, the death came from a coronary stenosis unrelated to the omission, so the breach did not cause the harm. Where the link is missing, a claim for compensation fails even though the conduct was careless, although the doctor may still face disciplinary action by the PMDC.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Duty of care owed to the patient: A team that accepts a patient for operation owes him a duty of care, so this element is clearly present in the case.
  • B. Breach of the expected standard of care: Proceeding to spinal anaesthesia without reviewing a platelet count of 38,000/µL falls below the expected standard, so a breach is likely present here.
  • C. Damage suffered by the patient: The patient did die, so damage exists; the difficulty lies in whether that harm was caused by the failure, not in whether harm occurred.
  • E. Consent of the patient to surgery: Consent concerns whether the operation was authorised; nothing suggests he refused, and lack of consent would be a separate wrong, not the gap in this claim.

Key point: Identify the four elements of medical negligence and recognise a missing causal link.

Question 5 · Death, injuries and forensic pathology · easy

A 68-year-old man with biopsy-proven lung adenocarcinoma and bone metastases dies on a medical ward in Kohat after three weeks of worsening breathlessness. The duty doctor completes the death certificate, entering cardiorespiratory arrest as the only cause and leaving all other lines blank. The hospital registrar returns the form for correction before it is sent for registration. His family needs the certificate for burial and for the pension office.

What is the main fault in this entry?

  1. A. The time of death is not given to the nearest minute
  2. B. It names a mode of dying, not a disease
  3. C. A magistrate must sign every hospital death certificate
  4. D. Cancer cannot be written on a death certificate
  5. E. It omits whether death was accidental or homicidal
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. It names a mode of dying, not a disease

A death certificate must give the disease or injury that started the chain of events leading to death. Cardiorespiratory arrest, coma, asthenia and syncope describe how death occurred. They are present in every death, so they have no value as a cause. Here the correct entry would list the immediate cause, such as respiratory failure, followed by the underlying cause on the lowest used line: metastatic lung adenocarcinoma. A natural death in a patient the doctor has attended, and whose cause is known, does not require a medicolegal inquest. In Pakistan the certificate is later used for registration with the local council and NADRA, so errors cause real delay for the family. If the cause is uncertain or the death unnatural, the case is referred to the police for a medicolegal post-mortem.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. The time of death is not given to the nearest minute: Certificates do not demand minute-level timing, and a patient dying slowly on a ward does not need it; the problem lies in the cause entered.
  • C. A magistrate must sign every hospital death certificate: A magistrate is involved when death is unnatural or suspicious; this man had known cancer and died on a ward, so the treating doctor can certify without a magistrate.
  • D. Cancer cannot be written on a death certificate: Naming the disease is exactly what a certificate is for; leaving out cancer would hide the true cause, and confidentiality does not stop a treating doctor certifying it.
  • E. It omits whether death was accidental or homicidal: Natural deaths do not need a statement of accident or homicide; manner matters when injury or poisoning is suspected, and nothing in this history suggests either.

Key point: Distinguish the cause of death from the mode of death on a certificate.

Question 6 · Toxicology · medium

A 38-year-old woman and her two children are brought to a hospital in Ziarat, Balochistan, at 7 am in winter after sleeping in a closed room with a burning coal brazier. She has headache, nausea and confusion, and is drowsy but rousable. Pulse is 112/min, blood pressure 118/74 mmHg and respiratory rate 20/min. Her skin is pink. Pulse oximetry shows SpO2 99% on room air and arterial PaO2 is 96 mmHg.

Which test is most useful to confirm the diagnosis?

  1. A. Repeat pulse oximetry
  2. B. Arterial PaO2 measurement
  3. C. Serum lactate level
  4. D. Methaemoglobin level
  5. E. Co-oximetry
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Co-oximetry

Carbon monoxide binds haemoglobin about 240 times more strongly than oxygen, shifting the dissociation curve to the left and impairing cellular respiration. Symptoms range from headache and nausea to confusion, coma and cardiac ischaemia, while cherry-pink skin is uncommon and unreliable. Pulse oximetry and PaO2 remain normal because they do not detect carboxyhaemoglobin, so diagnosis needs co-oximetry. Treatment is 100% oxygen via a tight non-rebreather mask until symptoms settle and carboxyhaemoglobin falls; hyperbaric oxygen is considered for loss of consciousness, neurological deficit, pregnancy or very high levels, but it is rarely available in Pakistan. In winter, coal braziers and gas heaters cause family clusters in Balochistan and the northern areas, so other occupants need testing.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Repeat pulse oximetry: Standard pulse oximeters read carboxyhaemoglobin as oxyhaemoglobin and give falsely normal values, so repeating the test cannot confirm gas exposure.
  • B. Arterial PaO2 measurement: PaO2 reflects dissolved oxygen, which stays normal in carbon monoxide poisoning even when oxygen content is critically low; her value of 96 mmHg does not exclude it.
  • C. Serum lactate level: Lactate may be raised by tissue hypoxia but is non-specific and also rises in many illnesses; it neither confirms nor identifies the gas exposure.
  • D. Methaemoglobin level: Methaemoglobin is raised by nitrites and some drugs, causing chocolate-brown blood and a low SpO2; here the skin is pink and the saturation reads normal.

Key point: Choose co-oximetry to confirm carbon monoxide poisoning when pulse oximetry is normal.

Question 7 · Legal procedure, identification and medical ethics · easy

A 24-year-old woman is found dead in a village house in Battagram with a cloth tied loosely around her neck. Relatives tell the police station that she may have taken her own life. Nobody accuses anyone, and the cause of death is unclear. The station house officer visits, examines the body in front of local residents, records the marks on it, and takes statements from neighbours. He sends the body for a post-mortem examination and prepares a report for the magistrate.

Under which section of the Code of Criminal Procedure is the officer acting?

  1. A. Section 154
  2. B. Section 161
  3. C. Section 164
  4. D. Section 174
  5. E. Section 176
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. Section 174

Pakistan has no coroner system. When a person dies by suicide, by accident, by homicide or in suspicious circumstances, the police officer in charge of the station, or another empowered officer, holds an inquest under Section 174 of the Code of Criminal Procedure. The officer examines the body at the scene in the presence of respectable residents of the neighbourhood, records the apparent marks of injury and the apparent cause of death, and sends the body to the nearest qualified medical officer for post-mortem examination. A magistrate may also inquire into the cause of death under Section 176, either instead of or in addition to the police inquiry, and may order exhumation. The doctor conducts the post-mortem on a written requisition from the police or the magistrate, not on the request of relatives.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Section 154: This section records first information of a cognizable offence; here nobody accuses anyone and the cause of death is unclear, so the officer is making a preliminary inquiry.
  • B. Section 161: This section lets police examine witnesses during an investigation; it does not authorise the inquest on the body and the report on cause of death that the officer prepares.
  • C. Section 164: This section covers recording of confessions and statements before a magistrate; here police themselves examine the body and question neighbours, and no confession is involved.
  • E. Section 176: This section lets a magistrate inquire into a death, including by exhumation; here the police officer is acting on the spot and only sends a report to the magistrate.

Key point: Name the section under which police hold an inquest into a suspicious death.

Question 8 · Death, injuries and forensic pathology · easy

A body is recovered from the river at Chakdara, Swat, by rescue workers about 45 minutes after the man was last seen alive at the riverbank. The 30-year-old has a clenched right fist holding blades of grass and a few small roots. All other muscles are flaccid, the jaw is loose, and the limbs move freely at every joint. There is no injury on the body. Rectal temperature is 36.0 °C.

Which post-mortem phenomenon explains the grip of the hand?

  1. A. Rigor mortis
  2. B. Heat stiffening
  3. C. Cadaveric spasm
  4. D. Cold stiffening
  5. E. Primary muscular flaccidity
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Cadaveric spasm

Cadaveric spasm, also called instantaneous rigor or cataleptic rigidity, is stiffening of a single group of muscles at the moment of death, without the preceding flaccidity. It is usually associated with intense emotion or extreme physical activity and fixes the last act of life, so a victim of drowning may hold weeds, grass or mud. It is rare, can affect any voluntary muscle, and is medicolegally valuable because it cannot be imitated after death. Rigor mortis, by contrast, begins one to two hours after death in the eyelids, face and jaw and then spreads to the whole body. Cadaveric spasm helps decide the manner of death: a weapon gripped in a suicide, or hair or cloth clutched in a struggle, cannot easily be placed in the hand afterwards.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Rigor mortis: Rigor affects all muscles together, starting in the eyelids and jaw after one to two hours; here only 45 minutes have passed and the jaw and other limbs are flaccid.
  • B. Heat stiffening: Heat stiffening follows burns or high temperatures, with muscle protein coagulation and flexed limbs; this man was recovered from cold river water with no burns on the body.
  • D. Cold stiffening: Cold stiffening needs freezing of body fat and muscle after prolonged exposure and affects the whole body; he was recovered within about 45 minutes and his limbs move freely.
  • E. Primary muscular flaccidity: Primary flaccidity is the first stage after death, when all muscles relax; here one hand grips tightly while the other muscles are relaxed, which excludes it.

Key point: Recognise cadaveric spasm and distinguish it from rigor mortis.

Question 9 · Toxicology · easy

A 38-year-old farmer from Swabi is brought to a hospital 45 minutes after spraying his tobacco crop without gloves or a mask. He is vomiting and drooling, with sweating, abdominal cramps and twitching of the muscles. His clothes are soaked. Pupils are 1 mm, pulse 46/min, blood pressure 92/58 mmHg and respiratory rate 30/min. There are bilateral wheezes with coarse crackles, and SpO2 is 86% on room air.

Which drug should be given first?

  1. A. Pralidoxime
  2. B. Neostigmine
  3. C. Atropine
  4. D. Diazepam
  5. E. Physostigmine
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Atropine

Atropine is the first-line antidote, because muscarinic excess, particularly bronchorrhoea and bradycardia, causes early death. After oxygen and removal of soaked clothing, with staff wearing gloves, the dose is 1 to 3 mg intravenously, doubled every 5 minutes until the chest is clear. The skin must be washed with soap and water. Pralidoxime, if used, follows atropine; its benefit is still debated. Diazepam treats seizures and agitation but does not reverse bronchorrhoea. Neostigmine and physostigmine inhibit cholinesterase and would worsen the poisoning. Organophosphate pesticides are among the most common causes of poisoning in rural Pakistan, including Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, and the same compounds are used for self-harm, so psychiatric review follows recovery.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Pralidoxime: Pralidoxime may follow atropine, but it reactivates enzyme slowly and does not clear the bronchorrhoea or bradycardia that threaten his oxygenation right now.
  • B. Neostigmine: Neostigmine is a cholinesterase inhibitor and would worsen the cholinergic excess; he already has pinpoint pupils, bradycardia and a wet chest.
  • D. Diazepam: Diazepam treats seizures and agitation in this poisoning, but he is not fitting and it does nothing for bronchorrhoea or bradycardia, so it is not the first drug.
  • E. Physostigmine: Physostigmine inhibits cholinesterase and treats anticholinergic poisoning; given here it would add to the excess of acetylcholine and worsen wheeze and bradycardia.

Key point: Identify atropine as the first drug in organophosphate poisoning with muscarinic features.

Question 10 · Legal procedure, identification and medical ethics · easy

A boy of unknown age is found abandoned at a bus stand in Mansehra and is brought to a hospital. He has no birth record and no vaccination card. He walks steadily and speaks in short sentences. Examination shows all 20 deciduous teeth present, with no permanent teeth erupted. The police ask the doctor for an approximate age.

What is his most likely age?

  1. A. 6 to 12 months
  2. B. 1 to 2 years
  3. C. 2.5 to 6 years
  4. D. 7 to 9 years
  5. E. 10 to 12 years
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. 2.5 to 6 years

Teeth are a reliable guide to age in childhood because eruption is less affected by nutrition and illness than bone growth. The deciduous teeth begin to appear at about 6 months, usually with the lower central incisors, and the full set of 20 is complete by about 2.5 years, the second molars being the last. The first permanent molars erupt at about 6 years behind the last deciduous molar, and the central incisors follow at about 7 years. A child with a full deciduous set and no permanent teeth is therefore between about 2.5 and 6 years old. Eruption varies by a few months between individuals and may be delayed in severe malnutrition, so the age is given as a range. Radiographs of the teeth and wrist bones refine the estimate.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. 6 to 12 months: At this age only the first few incisors have erupted; a child with all 20 deciduous teeth already present is much older.
  • B. 1 to 2 years: By two years the deciduous set is still incomplete, since the second molars come later; a child with a full set of 20 teeth is older.
  • D. 7 to 9 years: By seven years the first permanent molars and lower central incisors have erupted and deciduous teeth are being shed; none has erupted here, so he is younger.
  • E. 10 to 12 years: At 10 to 12 years many deciduous teeth have been replaced by premolars and canines; this child still has all 20 deciduous teeth and no permanent tooth.

Key point: Estimate the age of a child from the stage of dental eruption.
