Question 1 · Skin infections and infestations · easy

A 14-year-old boy living in a madrasa dormitory in Bannu has had an intensely itchy rash for three weeks that is worst at night and keeps him awake. Several other boys who sleep in the same hall are also scratching. He is afebrile and otherwise well. Examination shows small excoriated papules, a few thin, grey, 3-mm wavy tracks between the fingers and on both wrists, and papules around the waist and on the penis. The face and scalp are spared. There is no scaling plaque and no lymphadenopathy.

What is the most likely cause of his rash?

  1. A. Atopic eczema flare
  2. B. Sarcoptes scabiei infestation
  3. C. Papular urticaria from bed bugs
  4. D. Body louse infestation
  5. E. Allergic contact dermatitis
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Sarcoptes scabiei infestation

Scabies is caused by the mite Sarcoptes scabiei var hominis, which burrows into the stratum corneum and spreads by prolonged skin contact, especially in crowded settings such as dormitories, camps and large households. The intense itch, worse at night, is a delayed hypersensitivity reaction that appears three to six weeks after a first infestation. Favoured sites are the finger webs, flexor wrists, waist, axillae and genitals; the face and scalp are usually spared in older children and adults. A burrow is pathognomonic, and the diagnosis is mainly clinical, with dermoscopy or a skin scraping for confirmation when available. Treat the patient and all close contacts at the same time, whether or not they itch.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Atopic eczema flare: Eczema gives dry flexural patches in atopic people; it does not produce thin tracks between the fingers or night-time itch shared by boys in one dormitory.
  • C. Papular urticaria from bed bugs: Bites cluster in groups on exposed skin with central puncta; they do not form thin burrow tracks in the finger webs or papules on the penis.
  • D. Body louse infestation: Body lice cause excoriations concentrated at clothing seams on the shoulders and waist, with lice in the clothing, but not thin tracks between the fingers or penile papules.
  • E. Allergic contact dermatitis: Contact allergy gives a patchy eczematous rash where an allergen touched the skin; it does not cause thin burrow tracks or night itch spreading among dormitory mates.

Key point: Recognise the clinical features and distribution of scabies in a crowded setting.

Question 2 · Inflammatory and blistering disease · medium

A 40-year-old woman from Muzaffarabad attends a hospital with fever, a painful sore mouth and red burning eyes for four days. Nine days ago she started a sulfonamide antibiotic for a urinary infection. Examination shows widespread dusky red macules and flat atypical targets on the trunk and limbs, erosions of the lips, oral mucosa and conjunctivae, and genital erosions. Gentle lateral pressure makes the epidermis slide off in several areas. Careful mapping shows that 18% of her body surface has detached epidermis. Temperature is 38.7°C and blood pressure 114/72 mmHg.

Which diagnosis best fits this presentation?

  1. A. Morbilliform drug eruption
  2. B. Staphylococcal scalded skin syndrome
  3. C. Stevens-Johnson syndrome
  4. D. Toxic epidermal necrolysis
  5. E. SJS/TEN overlap
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. SJS/TEN overlap

Stevens-Johnson syndrome (SJS) and toxic epidermal necrolysis (TEN) are one spectrum of severe cutaneous adverse reactions, separated by the maximum body surface area of detached epidermis: SJS involves less than 10%, SJS/TEN overlap 10 to 30%, and TEN more than 30%. Mucosal erosions of at least two sites, painful skin, fever and atypical targets or dusky macules are typical, and the Nikolsky sign is positive. Sulfonamides are among the commonest triggers, along with allopurinol, antiepileptics and NSAIDs, usually 4 to 28 days after starting. The body surface area at presentation guides the setting of care and the SCORTEN prognostic score, and the area can still increase over several days. Management is the same across the spectrum: stop the drug and give supportive care, ideally in a burns or intensive care unit.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Morbilliform drug eruption: A simple exanthem gives a pink rash without mucosal erosions or sliding skin; she has mouth, eye and genital erosions with epidermal detachment, which excludes it.
  • B. Staphylococcal scalded skin syndrome: Scalded skin syndrome is toxin-mediated, mostly in infants, splits the superficial epidermis and spares mucosae; her mouth, eyes and genitals are eroded after a sulfonamide.
  • C. Stevens-Johnson syndrome: It is diagnosed when less than 10% of body surface has detached epidermis; she has 18%, which exceeds the limit and places her in the overlap category.
  • D. Toxic epidermal necrolysis: It requires detachment of more than 30% of body surface; she has only 18%, which falls below that threshold and fits the intermediate group.

Key point: Classify SJS, SJS/TEN overlap and TEN by the percentage of body surface with epidermal detachment.

Question 3 · Pigment, hair, nails, tumours and skin signs · easy

A 35-year-old woman from Dera Ismail Khan attends with tiredness and breathlessness on climbing stairs for six months. She has heavy periods lasting nine days, a craving for ice and brittle hair. Examination shows pale conjunctivae and thin nails with raised edges and a concave, spoon-like shape on several fingers. Pulse is 96/min and blood pressure is 106/68 mmHg. Haemoglobin is 7.9 g/dL (12-15) and mean corpuscular volume is 66 fL (80-100).

What is the most likely cause of the nail changes?

  1. A. Nail psoriasis
  2. B. Iron deficiency
  3. C. Chronic fungal infection
  4. D. Lichen planus
  5. E. Hypothyroidism
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Iron deficiency

Koilonychia, or spoon nails, is a thin concave nail with raised edges and is most often a sign of long-standing iron deficiency. This woman has several clues: heavy menstrual loss, pica for ice, brittle hair, pallor and microcytic anaemia. In Pakistan iron deficiency is common in women of reproductive age, and hookworm infection and low dietary iron add to menstrual loss. Confirm with serum ferritin, look for the source of blood loss, treat with oral iron with a haemoglobin check after a few weeks, and treat the cause. Less often koilonychia occurs with haemochromatosis, hypothyroidism, trauma or solvent exposure, and in infants it can be normal. Consider Plummer-Vinson syndrome if there is difficulty swallowing.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Nail psoriasis: Nail psoriasis gives pitting, oil-drop discolouration and onycholysis, usually with skin or joint disease; her nails are thin and spoon-shaped, with microcytic anaemia and no pitting.
  • C. Chronic fungal infection: Onychomycosis thickens and yellows nails with crumbling debris under them; her nails are thin and concave, and fungal infection would not explain her microcytic anaemia or pallor.
  • D. Lichen planus: Nail lichen planus causes thinning, longitudinal ridging and pterygium, often with mouth or skin lesions; it would not cause concave spoon shaping with microcytic anaemia and heavy periods.
  • E. Hypothyroidism: Hypothyroidism can make nails brittle and cause fatigue, but haemoglobin of 7.9 g/dL with microcytosis, heavy periods, ice craving and spoon-shaped nails point to iron loss instead.

Key point: Link koilonychia to iron deficiency anaemia.

Question 4 · Skin infections and infestations · medium

A 27-year-old woman is seen in Mansehra two days after returning from a hotel in Naran, where she spent an evening in a heated indoor pool. She reports an itchy rash that began about 36 hours after swimming. She feels well. Temperature is 36.8°C and pulse 72/min. Examination shows scattered red follicular papules and a few small pustules over the trunk, buttocks and upper thighs, sparing the face, palms and soles. There are no nodules, no lymphadenopathy and no deep tenderness. Her swimming companion has a similar rash.

What is the most appropriate management?

  1. A. Reassurance and observation
  2. B. Oral flucloxacillin for seven days
  3. C. Oral ciprofloxacin for seven days
  4. D. Topical mupirocin 2% to all lesions
  5. E. Oral fluconazole 150 mg single dose
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Reassurance and observation

Folliculitis acquired after a heated pool or hot tub is classically caused by Pseudomonas aeruginosa that survives in inadequately chlorinated water. Itchy follicular papules and pustules appear from hours to a few days after exposure, concentrated where swimwear holds water against the skin, and often affect several people who used the same pool. The condition is self-limiting, usually clearing in one to two weeks, so reassurance is correct. Antibiotics are not needed unless there is fever, deep lesions or an immunosuppressed host. Advise washing after swimming, changing out of wet clothes and avoiding the water until clear. Staphylococcal folliculitis, by contrast, is painful, appears on hairy sites without a water history and may need topical or oral antistaphylococcal treatment.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Oral flucloxacillin for seven days: Suits staphylococcal folliculitis with painful pustules or spreading infection; here the rash is itchy, afebrile and linked to pool exposure, with no abscess or deep tenderness.
  • C. Oral ciprofloxacin for seven days: Pool folliculitis settles without antibiotics; a fluoroquinolone adds toxicity and promotes resistance, so it is unnecessary in a well, afebrile woman with mild disease.
  • D. Topical mupirocin 2% to all lesions: Used for localised staphylococcal infection and MRSA decolonisation; this is a widespread water-related rash that resolves alone, and topical antibiotics would only add resistance.
  • E. Oral fluconazole 150 mg single dose: Pityrosporum folliculitis is fungal and affects the upper trunk of sweaty adults; this rash followed pool exposure within two days, involves the buttocks and fits a bacterial cause.

Key point: Recognise self-limiting Pseudomonas hot-tub folliculitis and manage it without antibiotics.

Question 5 · Inflammatory and blistering disease · medium

A 46-year-old woman from Chitral has had painful mouth erosions for three months that made eating difficult. Over the past month, flaccid blisters have appeared on her trunk and scalp and break easily to leave raw, weeping, non-healing erosions. She is not itchy and takes no regular medicines. Gentle sliding pressure on normal-looking skin next to a blister makes the epidermis separate. There are no target lesions and no eye involvement. Before reaching hospital she saw a faith healer and a local pharmacy. Temperature is 36.9°C and blood glucose is 98 mg/dL.

Which investigation is the most appropriate to confirm the diagnosis?

  1. A. Tzanck smear from the base of a blister
  2. B. Perilesional biopsy for immunofluorescence
  3. C. Serum total IgE
  4. D. Patch testing to a standard allergen series
  5. E. Skin scraping for potassium hydroxide microscopy
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Perilesional biopsy for immunofluorescence

Pemphigus vulgaris is an autoimmune blistering disease in which IgG antibodies against desmoglein 3, and often desmoglein 1, detach keratinocytes from each other (acantholysis), producing a suprabasal split. Painful mouth erosions often precede flaccid, easily ruptured blisters by months, and the Nikolsky sign is positive. Diagnosis needs a biopsy of a fresh lesion for histology plus a perilesional biopsy for direct immunofluorescence, which shows IgG and C3 between keratinocytes in a net-like pattern; serum desmoglein ELISA or indirect immunofluorescence supports it. Delay is common in Pakistan because patients first visit faith healers or pharmacies. Untreated, the disease can be fatal through sepsis and fluid loss, so early referral matters. Treatment is oral prednisolone with an adjuvant such as rituximab, azathioprine or mycophenolate.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Tzanck smear from the base of a blister: It shows acantholytic cells, but these also appear in herpes and other blistering disease, so it cannot confirm the disease; tissue immunofluorescence is needed.
  • C. Serum total IgE: Raised total IgE reflects atopy or parasites and is not specific to any blistering disease; it would not explain her flaccid blisters or mouth erosions.
  • D. Patch testing to a standard allergen series: Patch testing identifies contact allergens in eczema; her flaccid blisters with sliding skin and mouth erosions are not an eczematous or allergic contact process.
  • E. Skin scraping for potassium hydroxide microscopy: KOH microscopy detects fungal hyphae in scaly infections; she has flaccid blisters and mucosal erosions with no scaling and no itch, so a fungal cause is not plausible.

Key point: Confirm pemphigus vulgaris with perilesional biopsy for direct immunofluorescence.

Question 6 · Pigment, hair, nails, tumours and skin signs · easy

A 19-year-old woman from Mardan comes to a clinic with her mother because of pale patches that began on her hands a year ago and have since appeared around her mouth and on both elbows. The patches are chalk-white, sharply marginated, symmetrical, and neither scaly nor itchy. Sensation to light touch and pinprick is normal and no nerve is thickened. Some hairs within the patches are white. Under a Wood lamp the patches look bright milky-white. Her relatives fear an infectious disease and have put marriage proposals on hold.

What is the most likely diagnosis?

  1. A. Pityriasis alba
  2. B. Pityriasis versicolor
  3. C. Tuberculoid leprosy
  4. D. Vitiligo
  5. E. Piebaldism
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. Vitiligo

Non-segmental vitiligo is an acquired autoimmune loss of melanocytes. It produces sharply demarcated, chalk-white macules, typically symmetrical, on the hands, around the mouth and eyes, and over the elbows and knees, and hairs within the patches may turn white. A Wood lamp makes the patches look bright milky-white, which shows that pigment is absent and not merely reduced. Normal sensation and absent scale separate it from leprosy, pityriasis versicolor and pityriasis alba. It is not infectious. In Pakistan the stigma is heavy, with delayed marriage proposals and visits to faith healers first, so explaining that it is not contagious is part of care. Adults should also be assessed for associated autoimmune disease, especially thyroid disease.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Pityriasis alba: Gives ill-defined, scaly, hypopigmented (not fully depigmented) patches on the cheeks of atopic children; her patches are sharply marginated, chalk-white and symmetrical, and extend to elbows and hands.
  • B. Pityriasis versicolor: Gives fine bran-like scale and yellow-gold Wood lamp fluorescence on the upper trunk, with hypopigmented rather than depigmented patches; her patches have no scale and glow milky-white.
  • C. Tuberculoid leprosy: Gives a numb, hypopigmented, often solitary patch with thickened nerves; her sensation is normal, no nerve is thickened, and her patches are symmetrical and chalk-white.
  • E. Piebaldism: Is present from birth, is stable, and typically has a white forelock; her patches began a year ago at age 18 and have been spreading.

Key point: Recognise non-segmental vitiligo and separate it from other causes of pale patches.

Question 7 · Skin infections and infestations · easy

A 5-year-old boy is brought to a basic health unit in the Swat valley with sores around his nose for four days. His mother says they began as small blisters that burst and left yellow-brown crusts, which have spread to the upper lip. He is playing normally, eats well and has no fever. Temperature is 36.9°C. Examination shows four crusted lesions, each under 2 cm, around the nostrils and upper lip, with honey-coloured crusts, no bullae and no enlarged neck nodes. His younger sister has similar sores.

What is the most appropriate treatment?

  1. A. Topical clotrimazole 1% cream twice daily
  2. B. Oral flucloxacillin for seven days
  3. C. Topical hydrogen peroxide 1%
  4. D. Oral ciprofloxacin twice daily for seven days
  5. E. Topical hydrocortisone 1% cream twice daily
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Topical hydrogen peroxide 1%

Non-bullous impetigo is a superficial infection with Staphylococcus aureus, sometimes with Streptococcus pyogenes, that spreads easily among children in crowded households. When it is localised and the child is well, current NICE guidance prefers topical hydrogen peroxide 1% cream for a short course, because it avoids the resistance linked with repeated topical antibiotic use. Where it is not stocked, as in many basic health units in Pakistan, a short course of topical fusidic acid is the usual alternative. Oral flucloxacillin is kept for widespread disease, bullous impetigo or systemic upset. Treat affected contacts, keep the child away from school until the lesions are healed or 48 hours after starting treatment, and wash hands, towels and bedding.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Topical clotrimazole 1% cream twice daily: An antifungal suits ringworm with an advancing scaly edge; these weeping, crusted sores with honey-coloured crusts and spread to his sister indicate bacterial infection.
  • B. Oral flucloxacillin for seven days: Reserved for widespread impetigo, bullous disease or a child who is unwell; this boy has only four small lesions, no fever and is eating normally.
  • D. Oral ciprofloxacin twice daily for seven days: Fluoroquinolones have poor streptococcal activity and local resistance is high; oral therapy of any kind is unnecessary for four small lesions in a well child.
  • E. Topical hydrocortisone 1% cream twice daily: A mild steroid suits eczema, but on infected crusted skin it does nothing against the bacteria spreading between the two children and may worsen the infection.

Key point: Choose the first-line treatment for localised non-bullous impetigo in a well child.

Question 8 · Inflammatory and blistering disease · easy

A 4-year-old boy from Skardu is brought with itchy, dry, red patches on both cheeks and in the creases of the elbows and knees, present on and off since infancy. His father has asthma. He scratches at night. Examination shows scaly, mildly red patches on the cheeks and flexures with a few excoriations, with no weeping, crusting or fever. A plain moisturiser used daily has not cleared the cheeks and he needs a topical steroid for them. He is otherwise well.

Which topical steroid is the most appropriate for the facial patches?

  1. A. Clobetasol propionate 0.05% ointment
  2. B. Betamethasone valerate 0.1% cream
  3. C. Hydrocortisone 1% cream
  4. D. Mometasone furoate 0.1% cream
  5. E. Fluticasone propionate 0.05% cream
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Hydrocortisone 1% cream

Emollients are the foundation of atopic eczema care and should be used liberally every day, with a topical steroid added for flares. Steroids are chosen by site, age and severity: mild potency (hydrocortisone 1%) for the face, neck and flexures in children, moderate potency for the trunk and limbs, and potent or very potent preparations only for thick lichenified or palmoplantar disease under supervision. Use the lowest potency that works, once or twice daily, for the shortest time, and stop when the flare settles. Fingertip units help parents measure the amount. Facial skin absorbs steroid far more than limb skin, so strong steroids on a child face cause atrophy, telangiectasia and perioral dermatitis.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Clobetasol propionate 0.05% ointment: This very potent steroid is used for thick palm or sole eczema; on a child face it risks atrophy, telangiectasia and systemic absorption with adrenal suppression.
  • B. Betamethasone valerate 0.1% cream: A potent steroid is unsuitable on thin facial skin in a young child, where it can cause atrophy and perioral dermatitis; a milder potency should be tried first.
  • D. Mometasone furoate 0.1% cream: Mometasone is potent despite once-daily use; facial skin of a four-year-old absorbs steroid readily, so the risk of atrophy and absorption outweighs any convenience.
  • E. Fluticasone propionate 0.05% cream: Fluticasone is potent and intended for body flares in older children and adults, not routinely for the face of a small child with mild patches.

Key point: Choose a mild-potency topical steroid for facial atopic eczema in a young child.

Question 9 · Pigment, hair, nails, tumours and skin signs · easy

A 70-year-old farmer from Swat has a slowly growing lesion on the side of his nose for 14 months. It is a 7 mm pearly, translucent papule with a rolled edge, fine branching vessels across the surface and a small central ulcer that crusts and bleeds but never fully heals. It is painless. He has worked outdoors without a hat all his life. There are no enlarged lymph nodes.

What is the most likely diagnosis?

  1. A. Squamous cell carcinoma
  2. B. Keratoacanthoma
  3. C. Amelanotic melanoma
  4. D. Basal cell carcinoma
  5. E. Sebaceous hyperplasia
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. Basal cell carcinoma

Basal cell carcinoma is the commonest skin cancer, typically on sun-exposed skin of the head and neck in older people, and outdoor workers in Pakistan are also at risk. It grows slowly as a pearly, translucent papule with a rolled edge, telangiectasia and often a central ulcer that crusts and fails to heal. It invades locally and rarely metastasises. Management depends on risk: low-risk lesions can be treated by surgical excision with a 4 mm margin, curettage and cautery, or topical imiquimod, whereas high-risk sites such as the nose, eyelids and ears, and morpheaform types, are best treated by Mohs micrographic surgery where available or wider excision. Advise sun protection and regular skin checks.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Squamous cell carcinoma: Usually a firm, scaly or crusted, indurated nodule that grows over weeks to months and may be tender; this lesion is pearly, translucent and has grown slowly for 14 months.
  • B. Keratoacanthoma: Grows explosively over weeks into a dome with a central keratin plug, then may regress; a lesion that has crept along for 14 months with a pearly rolled edge does not fit.
  • C. Amelanotic melanoma: Presents as a pink or red nodule that enlarges over weeks to months without a pearly rolled edge; this lesion has slow growth, branching vessels and a translucent border.
  • E. Sebaceous hyperplasia: Gives soft, yellowish, umbilicated papules on the forehead and cheeks of older people that never ulcerate or bleed; this lesion has a rolled edge and a crusting central ulcer.

Key point: Recognise the clinical features of basal cell carcinoma.

Question 10 · Skin infections and infestations · easy

A 13-year-old girl from Skardu has had a painful lesion on the sole of her left foot for five months. It hurts when she walks and is tender to side-to-side squeezing. Examination shows a 1 cm rough papule with a hyperkeratotic surface, loss of normal skin lines and several pinpoint black dots after paring. She is otherwise healthy and takes no medication. Her mother wants treatment she can apply at home.

Which treatment is the most appropriate first-line option?

  1. A. Oral isotretinoin 0.5 mg/kg daily
  2. B. Intralesional bleomycin injections
  3. C. Surgical excision with suturing
  4. D. Oral aciclovir 400 mg three times daily
  5. E. Topical salicylic acid daily
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Topical salicylic acid daily

Plantar warts are caused by human papillomavirus, mostly types 1, 2 and 4, and look like keratotic papules with disrupted skin lines and thrombosed capillaries visible as black dots when pared, unlike corns, which keep normal skin lines and have a glassy core. Many warts in children clear without treatment within two years, so watchful waiting is acceptable, but this one is painful. First-line treatment is topical salicylic acid (for example a 40% plaster or paint), applied daily after soaking and paring the surface for up to 12 weeks, in line with British Association of Dermatologists guidance. Cryotherapy is an alternative that is no more effective and is painful in children. Systemic or intralesional therapy is reserved for resistant disease.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Oral isotretinoin 0.5 mg/kg daily: Reserved for severe recalcitrant conditions under specialist care, and teratogenic; a single plantar lesion in a healthy 13-year-old does not justify a systemic retinoid.
  • B. Intralesional bleomycin injections: Used for resistant or extensive warts after first-line options fail; injection is painful on the sole and is not a first step for one lesion.
  • C. Surgical excision with suturing: Excision risks a painful scar on a weight-bearing sole and recurrence; it is not recommended for a simple viral wart likely to respond to topical therapy.
  • D. Oral aciclovir 400 mg three times daily: Aciclovir is active against herpesviruses, not human papillomavirus; it would give no benefit for this lesion.

Key point: Choose first-line treatment for a plantar wart in a child.
