Question 1 · Antibacterial drugs · easy

A 55-year-old man with MRSA cellulitis is given vancomycin 1.5 g intravenously over 20 minutes. Near the end of the infusion he develops itching and redness of his face, neck and upper chest, with a blood pressure of 104/66 mmHg. He has no wheeze, lip swelling or stridor. He has had vancomycin before without problems when it was given more slowly.

What is the best way to prevent this next time?

  1. A. Never give vancomycin again
  2. B. Infuse it more slowly
  3. C. Give it as an intramuscular injection
  4. D. Give adrenaline before each dose
  5. E. Switch to oral vancomycin
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Infuse it more slowly

Vancomycin infusion reaction (previously called "red man syndrome") is caused by direct release of histamine from mast cells and basophils when vancomycin is infused quickly, not by IgE-mediated allergy. It causes flushing and itching of the face, neck and upper body, sometimes with hypotension. It is treated by stopping or slowing the infusion and giving an antihistamine. It is prevented by infusing at no more than about 10 mg/minute (at least 1 hour per gram), and an antihistamine beforehand if needed. It does not stop future use. Other vancomycin effects are kidney injury (more with high troughs and with piperacillin-tazobactam) and, rarely, ototoxicity.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Never give vancomycin again: This is a rate-related infusion reaction (red man), not a true allergy, so vancomycin can be given again.
  • C. Give it as an intramuscular injection: Vancomycin is not given intramuscularly; it is painful and poorly absorbed.
  • D. Give adrenaline before each dose: Adrenaline is for anaphylaxis; this histamine release is prevented by slowing the infusion.
  • E. Switch to oral vancomycin: Not absorbed; useless for cellulitis.

Key point: Vancomycin infusion reaction is rate-related histamine release; infuse slowly (at least 1 hour per gram).

Question 2 · Antifungal drugs · easy

A 62-year-old woman using an inhaled steroid has white plaques in her mouth that scrape off, and she is given nystatin oral suspension to swish and swallow four times daily, which clears it. Months later, in intensive care, she develops Candida albicans in her blood cultures. A student asks whether nystatin tablets could be used for this too.

Why is nystatin not used for bloodstream candida infection?

  1. A. It is not absorbed from the gut
  2. B. Candida is resistant to it
  3. C. It is destroyed by stomach acid
  4. D. It acts against bacteria, not fungi
  5. E. It causes liver failure
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. It is not absorbed from the gut

Nystatin is a polyene antifungal like amphotericin B: it binds ergosterol and forms pores in fungal membranes. It is far too toxic to be given by injection and is not absorbed from the gut or through skin. It is therefore used only topically: oral suspension or pastilles for oral and oesophageal candidiasis (it acts on contact as it passes), creams for skin and vaginal candidiasis. Because it is not absorbed, it has few systemic side effects or interactions. Candidaemia needs a systemic drug: an echinocandin first-line, with fluconazole as step-down once the species is known to be sensitive, and removal of central lines.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Candida is resistant to it: Most Candida are sensitive.
  • C. It is destroyed by stomach acid: Stomach acid is not the main reason; nystatin is simply not absorbed from the gut.
  • D. It acts against bacteria, not fungi: Nystatin is an antifungal polyene; it has no antibacterial action.
  • E. It causes liver failure: Nystatin does not cause liver failure; it fails because none reaches the blood.

Key point: Nystatin is not absorbed and too toxic to inject, so it is used only topically for mucosal and skin candidiasis.

Question 3 · Autonomic drugs · easy

A student studies transmission in sympathetic and parasympathetic ganglia. Acetylcholine released by preganglionic neurons makes the postganglionic neuron fire within a millisecond, by opening a channel that lets sodium in. The same receptor type, in a slightly different form, is found at the neuromuscular junction.

Which receptor carries this fast ganglionic transmission?

  1. A. Nicotinic channels
  2. B. Muscarinic M2 receptors
  3. C. Beta-1 receptors
  4. D. Alpha-2 receptors
  5. E. Dopamine D1 receptors
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Nicotinic channels

Nicotinic receptors are ligand-gated cation channels of five subunits. The neuronal type (NN) is found in all autonomic ganglia (sympathetic and parasympathetic), the adrenal medulla and the CNS; the muscle type (NM) is at the neuromuscular junction. Ganglion blockers (hexamethonium, mecamylamine, historically used for hypertension) block both arms of the autonomic system, so the effect on each organ depends on which tone dominates (for example, heart rate rises because parasympathetic tone dominates the heart, while vessels dilate because sympathetic tone dominates them). Neuromuscular blockers act on the muscle type.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Muscarinic M2 receptors: These are slow, G-protein receptors in the heart.
  • C. Beta-1 receptors: Adrenergic, on the heart.
  • D. Alpha-2 receptors: Alpha-2 receptors are presynaptic adrenergic receptors, not fast ganglionic channels.
  • E. Dopamine D1 receptors: Dopamine D1 receptors do not carry fast ganglionic transmission.

Key point: Fast ganglionic transmission uses neuronal nicotinic (ligand-gated) receptors; the NMJ uses the muscle nicotinic type.

Question 4 · Antiprotozoal and anthelmintic drugs · easy

A 26-year-old woman from Hunza has giardiasis confirmed by stool antigen, with foul-smelling bloating and loose stools for ten days. She works long shifts and often misses doses. She is not pregnant, does not breastfeed and takes no other medicine. Her doctor offers either metronidazole 400 mg three times daily for five days or a single 2 g dose of tinidazole, and she prefers the single-dose option.

Which property of tinidazole makes single-dose treatment possible?

  1. A. A longer elimination half-life than metronidazole
  2. B. It is not metabolised and persists in the body for weeks
  3. C. It is a benzimidazole that blocks tubulin in Giardia
  4. D. It is activated by human enzymes into a more potent drug
  5. E. It stays in the intestinal lumen and is not absorbed
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. A longer elimination half-life than metronidazole

Tinidazole and metronidazole are 5-nitroimidazoles. Their nitro group is reduced inside anaerobes and protozoa such as Giardia, Entamoeba and Trichomonas, forming radicals that fragment DNA; human cells do not activate them. Tinidazole has a longer half-life and good bioavailability, so a single 2 g dose cures most cases of giardiasis, which helps adherence compared with five days of metronidazole. Adverse effects include metallic taste, nausea and a disulfiram-like reaction with alcohol (avoid alcohol for three days after tinidazole). It is avoided in the first trimester and in breastfeeding (suspend for three days). Nitazoxanide and albendazole are alternatives in treatment failure, and safe water and hygiene prevent reinfection.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. It is not metabolised and persists in the body for weeks: Tinidazole is metabolised in the liver and is eliminated within a few days.
  • C. It is a benzimidazole that blocks tubulin in Giardia: Tinidazole is a nitroimidazole; tubulin binding is the mechanism of albendazole and mebendazole.
  • D. It is activated by human enzymes into a more potent drug: Activation occurs by reduction in the parasite, not in human cells.
  • E. It stays in the intestinal lumen and is not absorbed: Tinidazole is well absorbed and reaches tissues; it is not a luminal-only drug.

Key point: Relate the half-life of tinidazole to single-dose treatment of giardiasis.

Question 5 · Antiviral drugs · medium

A 23-year-old woman has painful grouped vesicles on her lower lip that recur when she has fever, and she starts aciclovir at the first tingling. She asks why the drug harms the virus but not her own dividing cells, as she has read it acts on DNA. Her doctor explains that the drug must first be changed by an enzyme made by the virus.

Which enzyme first activates aciclovir?

  1. A. Human thymidine kinase
  2. B. Viral DNA polymerase
  3. C. Viral thymidine kinase
  4. D. Viral protease
  5. E. Human cytosine deaminase
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Viral thymidine kinase

Aciclovir (and its prodrug valaciclovir, and famciclovir/penciclovir) is a guanosine analogue. Herpes simplex and varicella-zoster viruses make their own thymidine kinase, which adds the first phosphate to aciclovir; human kinases then complete the conversion to aciclovir triphosphate. This inhibits viral DNA polymerase and, once incorporated, terminates the growing DNA chain because it lacks a 3'-hydroxyl group. Uninfected human cells hardly phosphorylate aciclovir, so it has little toxicity. Resistance usually comes from loss or alteration of viral thymidine kinase (mostly in immunocompromised patients), and is treated with foscarnet or cidofovir. Aciclovir does not eliminate latent virus in nerve ganglia.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Human thymidine kinase: Phosphorylates it very poorly.
  • B. Viral DNA polymerase: This is the target, not the activator.
  • D. Viral protease: Viral protease plays no part in aciclovir activation.
  • E. Human cytosine deaminase: Human cytosine deaminase does not activate aciclovir.

Key point: Aciclovir is activated first by viral thymidine kinase, so it acts only in infected cells, inhibiting viral DNA polymerase.

Question 6 · Haematological drugs: anticoagulants, antiplatelets and haematinics · easy

A 57-year-old man in Dir takes warfarin 5 mg daily for a mechanical mitral valve and has had stable INRs of 2.5-3.5. During the winter he begins eating large helpings of sarson ka saag (mustard greens) and spinach every day. At his next clinic visit his INR is 1.6 (target 2.5-3.5) although he has taken every dose and no other medicine has changed. He asks what has gone wrong.

Which explanation is correct?

  1. A. Spinach and mustard greens induce hepatic CYP2C9
  2. B. Dietary vitamin K opposes the effect of warfarin
  3. C. Leafy vegetables bind warfarin in the gut and stop its absorption
  4. D. Dietary fibre increases renal excretion of warfarin
  5. E. Greens raise plasma albumin and so lower the free fraction of warfarin
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Dietary vitamin K opposes the effect of warfarin

Warfarin inhibits vitamin K epoxide reductase (VKORC1), the enzyme that recycles oxidised vitamin K to its active reduced form. Reduced vitamin K is needed to carboxylate clotting factors II, VII, IX and X and proteins C and S, so inhibition leaves them non-functional. Foods rich in vitamin K, such as spinach, sarson ka saag, methi, kale, broccoli and some oils, supply more of the vitamin and overcome part of the block, lowering the INR; a sudden fall in intake raises it. Patients do not need to avoid these foods, but should keep their intake consistent and report changes, such as seasonal diets, Ramadan fasting, illness or weight loss. Alcohol, antibiotics, herbal products and other drugs also change the INR. In a patient with a mechanical mitral valve, a subtherapeutic INR carries a high risk of valve thrombosis and embolism, so the dose may need adjustment with more frequent INR checks.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Spinach and mustard greens induce hepatic CYP2C9: Leafy greens do not induce this enzyme in a meaningful way.
  • C. Leafy vegetables bind warfarin in the gut and stop its absorption: Warfarin is well absorbed and is not bound by vegetables.
  • D. Dietary fibre increases renal excretion of warfarin: Warfarin is not excreted by the kidney to a significant degree, and fibre does not change this.
  • E. Greens raise plasma albumin and so lower the free fraction of warfarin: Vegetables do not alter albumin concentration enough to change warfarin binding.

Key point: Explain how dietary vitamin K lowers the INR and counsel on consistent intake.

Question 7 · CNS, psychiatric and anaesthetic drugs · easy

A 68-year-old man with Parkinson disease has slowness, rigidity and a resting tremor. He is started on co-careldopa, a tablet combining levodopa and carbidopa. His doctor explains that the second drug allows a much smaller dose of levodopa and reduces nausea and palpitations, but it does not itself cross into the brain.

How does carbidopa help?

  1. A. It inhibits peripheral dopa decarboxylase
  2. B. It blocks dopamine D2 receptors in the gut
  3. C. It inhibits COMT in the brain
  4. D. It inhibits MAO-B
  5. E. It stimulates dopamine receptors
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. It inhibits peripheral dopa decarboxylase

Levodopa crosses the blood-brain barrier on the large neutral amino acid transporter and is converted to dopamine in the remaining nigrostriatal neurons. Given alone, most is converted to dopamine in the gut and blood by aromatic L-amino acid (dopa) decarboxylase; peripheral dopamine causes nausea and vomiting (via the chemoreceptor trigger zone, outside the barrier), hypotension and arrhythmias, and little levodopa reaches the brain. Carbidopa (or benserazide) inhibits this enzyme only outside the brain, since it does not cross the barrier, so levodopa doses can be cut by about 75% and peripheral side effects fall. Central effects such as dyskinesia and hallucinations are not reduced.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. It blocks dopamine D2 receptors in the gut: Blocking gut D2 receptors is the action of domperidone.
  • C. It inhibits COMT in the brain: Inhibiting COMT is the action of entacapone or tolcapone.
  • D. It inhibits MAO-B: That is selegiline or rasagiline.
  • E. It stimulates dopamine receptors: That is a dopamine agonist.

Key point: Carbidopa inhibits peripheral dopa decarboxylase, increasing brain levodopa and reducing peripheral side effects.

Question 8 · Cardiovascular drugs · easy

A 54-year-old woman with hypertension started ramipril 5 mg daily six weeks ago. Her blood pressure is now 128/80 mmHg, but she has a dry, tickly cough that keeps her awake. She does not smoke, her chest is clear and her chest X-ray is normal.

What causes the cough, and what is the best change?

  1. A. Angiotensin II excess; add a diuretic
  2. B. Asthma; add an inhaler
  3. C. Bradykinin; switch to losartan
  4. D. Heart failure; add furosemide
  5. E. Allergy; switch to enalapril
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Bradykinin; switch to losartan

Angiotensin-converting enzyme also breaks down bradykinin. ACE inhibitors raise bradykinin and substance P in the airways, causing a dry cough in about 10-15% of patients (more often in women and in people of East and South Asian origin), weeks to months after starting. It is a class effect, so changing to another ACE inhibitor does not help; angiotensin receptor blockers (losartan, valsartan) block AT1 receptors without affecting kinins and rarely cause cough. Bradykinin also explains ACE-inhibitor angioedema. Other ACE inhibitor effects: first-dose hypotension, hyperkalaemia, a rise in creatinine (especially with renal artery stenosis) and fetal toxicity.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Angiotensin II excess; add a diuretic: Angiotensin II is reduced by ramipril.
  • B. Asthma; add an inhaler: Nothing suggests asthma; her chest is clear and the cough began after the ACE inhibitor.
  • D. Heart failure; add furosemide: No signs of heart failure.
  • E. Allergy; switch to enalapril: All ACE inhibitors cause it.

Key point: ACE inhibitor cough is caused by bradykinin; switch to an ARB, which does not raise bradykinin.

Question 9 · Endocrine and reproductive drugs · easy

A 22-year-old woman with type 1 diabetes is moved from NPH to insulin glargine at night. Her nurse tells her it gives a steady background level for about 24 hours without a clear peak, that the solution is clear and acidic in the vial, and that it must not be mixed in the same syringe as her rapid-acting insulin.

Which property gives glargine its long, flat action?

  1. A. It precipitates under the skin
  2. B. It is bound to protamine in the vial
  3. C. It binds albumin in the blood
  4. D. It is inhaled into the lungs
  5. E. It is cleared by the kidney unchanged
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. It precipitates under the skin

Insulin glargine has two arginines added to the B chain and a glycine substitution in the A chain, which shift its isoelectric point towards neutral. It is soluble in its acidic vial, but when injected into the neutral pH of subcutaneous tissue it forms microprecipitates that dissolve slowly, giving a fairly constant absorption for about 24 hours with no marked peak. Mixing it with other insulins changes the pH and alters both profiles, so it is given separately. Detemir has a fatty acid chain that binds albumin; degludec forms multi-hexamers and binds albumin, lasting over 40 hours. NPH relies on protamine and has a peak at 4-8 hours.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. It is bound to protamine in the vial: Binding to protamine is how NPH insulin is made long-acting.
  • C. It binds albumin in the blood: That is detemir and degludec.
  • D. It is inhaled into the lungs: Glargine is injected under the skin, not inhaled.
  • E. It is cleared by the kidney unchanged: Kidney clearance does not explain its flat profile; it forms a slowly dissolving depot under the skin.

Key point: Glargine precipitates at neutral pH under the skin, giving slow, peakless absorption over about 24 hours.

Question 10 · General pharmacology and toxicology · easy

A 58-year-old man with stable angina gets chest tightness when he climbs the stairs to his flat. He is told to put a glyceryl trinitrate tablet under his tongue and let it dissolve rather than swallow it.

How is the sublingual route different from the oral route of administration?

  1. A. The drug is absorbed straight into the general circulation, bypassing the liver
  2. B. The drug is absorbed more slowly, over several hours
  3. C. The drug is activated by enzymes in saliva before absorption
  4. D. The drug has to be given in a larger dose to have the same effect
  5. E. The drug passes through the portal vein before it reaches the heart
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. The drug is absorbed straight into the general circulation, bypassing the liver

The two routes differ in where the drug is absorbed and which way it travels next.

Oral route: the tablet is swallowed and absorbed from the small intestine. The blood from the gut drains into the portal vein and goes through the liver before reaching the rest of the body, so the liver can break down much of the dose (first-pass metabolism). Glyceryl trinitrate is almost completely destroyed this way. Onset is slow, from many minutes to hours.

Sublingual route: the tablet dissolves under the tongue and the drug is absorbed through the thin lining of the mouth into veins that drain to the superior vena cava. The drug therefore reaches the heart and general circulation directly, without passing through the liver first. Onset is within one to three minutes, which is why it is used to relieve an attack of angina, and the dose is small (300 to 500 micrograms).

The tablet must be left to dissolve under the tongue; if it is swallowed it behaves as an oral dose.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. The drug is absorbed more slowly, over several hours: Sublingual absorption is fast, with onset in about one to three minutes; slow absorption over hours describes a swallowed sustained-release tablet.
  • C. The drug is activated by enzymes in saliva before absorption: Saliva does not activate glyceryl trinitrate; it is converted to nitric oxide in vascular smooth muscle after absorption.
  • D. The drug has to be given in a larger dose to have the same effect: The reverse is true: the sublingual dose is small, while a swallowed dose would have to be much larger to make up for what the liver removes.
  • E. The drug passes through the portal vein before it reaches the heart: That describes the oral route: swallowed drugs are absorbed from the gut into the portal vein and pass through the liver first.

Key point: Sublingual drugs are absorbed through the mouth lining directly into the general circulation, so they bypass the liver and act within minutes; oral drugs pass through the liver first.
