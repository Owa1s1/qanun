Behavioural sciences and ethics MCQs for MBBS

Question 1 · Behavioural sciences and medical ethics · medium

A 70-year-old man is found to have advanced stomach cancer. Before the doctor can speak to him, his sons ask the doctor not to tell their father, because they believe the news will make him lose hope and die sooner. The father is alert, has capacity, and has been asking the nurses why he is losing weight and what the tests showed.

What is the most appropriate response?

  1. A. Find out what the father wants to know
  2. B. Agree and tell the father he has an ulcer
  3. C. Tell the father everything at once
  4. D. Refuse to speak to the sons
  5. E. Transfer him to another doctor
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Find out what the father wants to know

Requests from families to withhold a diagnosis ("collusion") are common in Pakistan and other family-centred cultures and usually come from love and a wish to protect. The doctor should listen to the family's fears, explain that she will not force information on their father but cannot lie to him, and that patients often already suspect the truth and feel isolated when it is hidden. She should then ask the patient himself how much he wants to know and whom he wants involved; some patients prefer their family to receive detailed information, and that choice should be respected. Honest, gradual, compassionate disclosure, with hope focused on comfort and support, is the aim.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Agree and tell the father he has an ulcer: Telling him he has an ulcer is a direct lie, and he will discover it when the treatment does not match.
  • C. Tell the father everything at once: Delivering everything at once ignores both the sons' fears and the possibility that he wants the news in stages.
  • D. Refuse to speak to the sons: The sons' worry about his hope is genuine and worth hearing, even though it cannot override his right to know.
  • E. Transfer him to another doctor: Handing him to another doctor leaves exactly the same conflict for someone else to face.

Key point: When family ask to withhold bad news, listen to them but ask the capable patient what he wants to know; do not lie to him.

Question 2 · Behavioural sciences and medical ethics · medium

A doctor has the results of a CT scan showing that a 60-year-old man's lung cancer has spread to his liver. She has arranged a private room, switched off her phone and the man's wife is present at his request. They are all seated. She wants to follow the next step of the SPIKES approach before giving the news.

What should she do next?

  1. A. Give the result straight away in full
  2. B. Discuss chemotherapy options first
  3. C. Ask his wife to leave
  4. D. Show him the scan images
  5. E. Ask what he already understands
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Ask what he already understands

After setting up the conversation, the next step in SPIKES is Perception: asking open questions such as "What have you been told so far about why we did the scan?" or "What do you think is going on?". This shows the doctor what the patient knows, how serious he believes the situation is, and any misunderstandings or hopes, so the news can be pitched at the right level. The doctor then seeks an Invitation (how much he wants to know), gives a warning shot ("I am afraid the results are not as we had hoped"), then the Knowledge in clear, simple language, followed by responding to Emotions and agreeing a Strategy.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Give the result straight away in full: Giving the result in full before knowing what he understands risks either repeating what he knows or overwhelming him.
  • B. Discuss chemotherapy options first: Treatment options come after the news, once he has taken in what the scan shows.
  • C. Ask his wife to leave: He asked for his wife to be present, so sending her out removes the support he chose.
  • D. Show him the scan images: The images may help later in the conversation, but they are not how the news is broken.

Key point: After preparing the setting, ask what the patient already understands before giving bad news (Perception).

Question 3 · Behavioural sciences and medical ethics · medium

A nurse on a medical ward notices that a patient with a known penicillin allergy has been prescribed co-amoxiclav. She stops the dose before it is given and the prescription is changed. No harm comes to the patient. The prescribing doctor says there is no need to write anything down because nothing happened.

What is the most appropriate action?

  1. A. Do nothing as no harm occurred
  2. B. Discipline the nurse for interfering
  3. C. Report it as a near miss
  4. D. Tell the patient, but record nothing
  5. E. Stop all antibiotics on the ward
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Report it as a near miss

A near miss is an error that was caught before it reached the patient or caused harm. Near misses are far more common than harmful events and share the same causes, so reporting them through incident systems allows hospitals to find and fix weaknesses before someone is hurt: for example, allergy alerts on prescribing systems, checking allergy status before prescribing, and pharmacist review. A just, learning culture encourages reporting by focusing on systems rather than blame, while still holding individuals accountable for reckless behaviour. The patient can be told about the near miss, and the staff member who caught it should be thanked.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Do nothing as no harm occurred: No harm reached the patient this time, but the same prescription could be written again tomorrow for someone else.
  • B. Discipline the nurse for interfering: The nurse checked the allergy and stopped a dose that could have caused anaphylaxis; she acted exactly as she should.
  • D. Tell the patient, but record nothing: Being open with the patient is good, but without a report nobody can find out why the allergy was missed, so it may happen again.
  • E. Stop all antibiotics on the ward: Other patients need their antibiotics; the problem is one prescription, not the whole ward's treatment.

Key point: Near misses should be reported so that system weaknesses can be fixed before harm occurs.

Question 4 · Behavioural sciences and medical ethics · medium

A physician in a private clinic in Islamabad has recently become a part-owner of a diagnostic laboratory. He begins ordering more blood tests than before for his patients and tells them they must have the tests done at "his" lab, without mentioning that he owns part of it. A colleague notices that many of the tests are not needed.

What is the main ethical problem?

  1. A. Breach of confidentiality
  2. B. Lack of informed consent for surgery
  3. C. Failure to break bad news
  4. D. An undeclared conflict of interest
  5. E. Poor communication with nurses
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. An undeclared conflict of interest

A conflict of interest exists when a doctor's professional judgement about a patient's care could be influenced by a secondary interest, such as financial gain. Self-referral to a facility in which the doctor has a financial interest can lead to unnecessary tests, higher costs and loss of trust. Doctors should avoid such conflicts where possible; where they cannot, they must declare them openly to patients, allow free choice of where tests are done, and make sure that every investigation is clinically justified. Ordering unnecessary tests for profit is a breach of professional ethics and may be dealt with by the regulator.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Breach of confidentiality: Nothing in the account suggests his patients' information has been shared with anyone who should not have it.
  • B. Lack of informed consent for surgery: No surgery is being proposed; the issue is the tests he orders and where he sends them.
  • C. Failure to break bad news: No bad news is involved here; the problem lies in why the tests are being ordered at all.
  • E. Poor communication with nurses: His dealings with nursing staff are not described and are not what makes the arrangement wrong.

Key point: Financial interests that influence clinical decisions are conflicts of interest; they must be avoided or openly declared.

Question 5 · Behavioural sciences and medical ethics · medium

A 30-year-old unmarried woman has been seeing a male general practitioner regularly for treatment of anxiety for a year. At her latest appointment she gives him a personal letter expressing romantic feelings and asks to meet him privately outside the clinic. The doctor is also unmarried and finds her attractive.

What is the most appropriate response?

  1. A. Accept the invitation outside clinic hours
  2. B. Ignore the letter and say nothing
  3. C. Decline kindly
  4. D. Discuss her letter with her family
  5. E. Stop treating her abruptly
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Decline kindly

Doctors must not pursue sexual or improper emotional relationships with current patients, or use their professional position to do so. Patients may be vulnerable, especially those receiving treatment for mental health problems, and the power imbalance in the relationship means that such relationships can harm them and undermine trust. The doctor should respond kindly but clearly, explaining that a personal relationship is not possible, and maintain professional boundaries. If the situation makes continued care difficult, her care should be transferred to a colleague, handled sensitively so that she does not feel abandoned. He should keep a record and may seek advice from a senior colleague.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Accept the invitation outside clinic hours: A private meeting turns a year-long therapeutic relationship for anxiety into something else, and she is vulnerable within it.
  • B. Ignore the letter and say nothing: Saying nothing leaves her feelings unanswered and makes the next consultation harder for both of them.
  • D. Discuss her letter with her family: Her letter is confidential, and telling her family would expose her feelings and breach her trust.
  • E. Stop treating her abruptly: She is being treated for anxiety and still needs care; stopping abruptly leaves her without a doctor.

Key point: Doctors must not pursue personal relationships with patients; decline kindly, keep boundaries and transfer care if needed.

Question 6 · Behavioural sciences and medical ethics · medium

A surgical trainee arrives in theatre at 8 am to assist a consultant with a bowel resection. The consultant smells strongly of alcohol, his speech is slightly slurred and his hands are unsteady while scrubbing. The patient is already anaesthetised. The trainee is worried about her own career if she says anything.

What should she do?

  1. A. Assist and keep quiet
  2. B. Assist, then tell the patient afterwards
  3. C. Report it to the regulator after the list
  4. D. Wait until after the operation to speak
  5. E. Stop him operating; tell a senior
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Stop him operating; tell a senior

Doctors have a duty to act when they believe a colleague's health, conduct or performance puts patients at risk. The immediate priority is the safety of the patient: the trainee should, if possible, speak to the consultant and prevent him from operating, and immediately inform someone with authority, such as the anaesthetist, another consultant, the clinical director or the medical superintendent, so that the operation can be postponed or another surgeon found. Later, the concern must be raised through proper channels. Colleagues with alcohol problems also need confidential support and treatment, and raising concerns in good faith should not harm a junior doctor's career.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Assist and keep quiet: Assisting in silence means a bowel resection is done by a surgeon with slurred speech and unsteady hands.
  • B. Assist, then tell the patient afterwards: The patient is at risk now; action cannot wait until afterwards.
  • C. Report it to the regulator after the list: Reporting later does not protect the patient on the table.
  • D. Wait until after the operation to speak: The harm would already be done; this patient is on the table and cannot wait for a conversation afterwards.

Key point: If a colleague is unfit to practise and puts patients at risk, act at once to protect patients and escalate to a senior.

Question 7 · Behavioural sciences and medical ethics · medium

A medical officer notices over several months that one of her colleagues repeatedly misses abnormal blood results, has had several patients deteriorate without review, and has become withdrawn and tired. She has spoken to him informally, but nothing has changed, and another patient was harmed this week. She does not want to get him into trouble.

What is the most appropriate next step?

  1. A. Keep quiet out of loyalty
  2. B. Tell the department head
  3. C. Speak to him privately and take no further step
  4. D. Tell his patients to see another doctor
  5. E. Report it only if a patient is harmed
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Tell the department head

When informal discussion has not resolved a concern about a colleague's performance or health that is putting patients at risk, the doctor must raise it formally with the person responsible, usually the head of department, clinical director or medical superintendent, giving factual, specific information. Many such problems stem from illness, burnout or personal difficulties, so the aim is both to protect patients and to help the colleague get support and remediation. Only if local channels fail should concerns be taken to the regulator. Loyalty to colleagues must not come before patient safety, and staff who raise concerns honestly should be protected.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Keep quiet out of loyalty: Loyalty to a colleague cannot outweigh the patients who keep deteriorating unreviewed; one was harmed this week.
  • C. Speak to him privately and take no further step: A private word may be a start, but repeated unsafe practice must reach someone who can act.
  • D. Tell his patients to see another doctor: Warning patients individually is unprofessional and does not address the problem.
  • E. Report it only if a patient is harmed: Waiting for harm fails the duty to protect patients.

Key point: Concerns about a colleague that put patients at risk must be raised formally through the proper channel.

Question 8 · Behavioural sciences and medical ethics · medium

After a bus crash on the Karakoram Highway, 30 injured passengers arrive at a small district hospital with two doctors and limited blood supplies. A triage system is used. One patient has a severe head injury with fixed dilated pupils and no breathing effort; another has a bleeding leg wound that can be controlled; others have minor injuries.

How should treatment be prioritised in this mass casualty situation?

  1. A. First come, first served
  2. B. Most important person first
  3. C. The most severely injured first
  4. D. Those most likely to benefit first
  5. E. Treat everyone equally at the same time
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. Those most likely to benefit first

In a mass casualty incident, resources are overwhelmed, and the usual approach of giving maximum care to each individual changes to doing the greatest good for the greatest number. Triage sorts patients by urgency and the likelihood of benefit: those with life-threatening but treatable injuries (such as controllable haemorrhage) are treated first; those with minor injuries can wait; those whose injuries are unsurvivable with the resources available are given comfort care rather than consuming resources that could save others. Triage decisions must be based only on clinical criteria, applied consistently, and reviewed as patients' conditions and resources change.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. First come, first served: Treating in arrival order would let a man bleeding from a controllable leg wound wait behind minor cuts.
  • B. Most important person first: Social rank has no place in triage; with two doctors and little blood, the decision must rest on who can be saved.
  • C. The most severely injured first: The head injury is the most severe but, with fixed pupils and no breathing effort, treatment cannot save him.
  • E. Treat everyone equally at the same time: Two doctors cannot treat thirty people at once, so some order of priority has to be chosen.

Key point: In mass casualty triage, priority goes to those most likely to benefit from immediate treatment, using clinical criteria only.

Question 9 · Behavioural sciences and medical ethics · medium

A wealthy 50-year-old man with end-stage kidney failure arrives for transplant work-up at a private hospital with a 24-year-old man he introduces as his "cousin". On interview, the young man cannot name any shared relatives, comes from a poor village in another province, and admits he has been promised money to clear his family's debts. The surgeon is under pressure from the hospital owner to proceed.

What should the transplant team do?

  1. A. Proceed, since the donor consents
  2. B. Proceed if the donor is paid fairly
  3. C. Refuse; organ selling is illegal
  4. D. Ask the donor to sign a waiver
  5. E. Proceed if the ethics committee approves
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Refuse; organ selling is illegal

Pakistan's Transplantation of Human Organs and Tissues Act 2010 prohibits commercial dealing in human organs. Living donation is allowed mainly from close relatives, and donations from other people require approval by an official evaluation committee to make sure that consent is free and no payment is involved. Buying organs exploits poor and vulnerable people, exposes them to surgical risks and long-term harm, and has been a serious problem in the region. The team must refuse to proceed, document the findings and report the case to the relevant authority. Ethical alternatives include donation from related donors, deceased-donor programmes and dialysis.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Proceed, since the donor consents: His consent is bought: he is in debt, has been promised money, and is not the relative he is claimed to be.
  • B. Proceed if the donor is paid fairly: Paying more does not make organ trade lawful, and a larger sum increases the pressure on a man in debt.
  • D. Ask the donor to sign a waiver: A waiver cannot make an illegal transaction lawful, and it offers the young man no protection at all.
  • E. Proceed if the ethics committee approves: No committee can approve a paid donation; buying and selling organs is illegal under the 2010 Act.

Key point: Organ trading is illegal in Pakistan; transplant teams must refuse paid donation and report it.

Question 10 · Behavioural sciences and medical ethics · medium

A 33-year-old man with newly diagnosed type 1 diabetes says, "I can't believe I have to inject myself every day for the rest of my life. I feel like my whole future has changed." The doctor wants to respond in a way that shows she has understood his feelings before moving on to practical teaching about insulin.

Which response best shows empathy?

  1. A. "Many people have it worse than you."
  2. B. "Don't worry, it's easy."
  3. C. "Let me show you the pen now."
  4. D. "You should have looked after yourself."
  5. E. "It sounds overwhelming to face this."
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. "It sounds overwhelming to face this."

Empathy means understanding the patient's situation and feelings and communicating that understanding back. An empathic response identifies the emotion, links it to its cause and shows acceptance, for example, "It sounds overwhelming to face a lifelong change like this." It is different from sympathy (feeling sorry for someone) and from false reassurance, which can make patients feel unheard. Empathic responses build trust, help patients share their concerns, improve satisfaction and adherence, and can be brief. Other active listening skills include attentive silence, encouraging nods, reflecting back key words, summarising and checking understanding.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. "Many people have it worse than you.": Comparing him with people who are worse off tells him his reaction is unreasonable and closes the conversation.
  • B. "Don't worry, it's easy.": Calling daily injections easy denies what he has just said about his future and is not true for most people starting insulin.
  • C. "Let me show you the pen now.": Reaching for the pen answers the practical problem while leaving his feelings about the diagnosis unacknowledged.
  • D. "You should have looked after yourself.": Type 1 diabetes is not caused by how he looked after himself, so this blames him for something he could not prevent.

Key point: Empathy means recognising and naming the patient's feelings and showing understanding, not false reassurance.
