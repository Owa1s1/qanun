Question 1 · Renal physiology, body fluids and acid-base · easy

A 55-year-old man with poorly controlled diabetes, who has stopped his metformin, is thirsty and passes urine often. His blood glucose is 290 mg/dL. His urine dipstick shows glucose 3+, without ketones. His kidney function is normal. When his glucose was 144 mg/dL last month, his urine had no glucose.

Why does glucose appear in his urine?

  1. A. The transport maximum is exceeded
  2. B. His glomeruli have become permeable to glucose
  3. C. The collecting ducts secrete glucose
  4. D. Insulin is needed for the tubules to reabsorb glucose
  5. E. The loop of Henle has stopped reabsorbing glucose
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. The transport maximum is exceeded

Glucose is freely filtered and normally completely reabsorbed in the proximal tubule by sodium-glucose cotransporters (SGLT2 early, SGLT1 later), then leaves the cells through GLUT2. These carriers have a transport maximum (about 375 mg/min). Glucose begins to appear in the urine when plasma glucose exceeds the renal threshold of about 180-200 mg/dL, because some nephrons saturate before others (splay). Glycosuria causes an osmotic diuresis, explaining polyuria and thirst. SGLT2 inhibitors (empagliflozin, dapagliflozin) lower blood glucose by blocking this reabsorption.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. His glomeruli have become permeable to glucose: Glucose is always freely filtered; the filter has not changed.
  • C. The collecting ducts secrete glucose: The tubules do not secrete glucose into the urine.
  • D. Insulin is needed for the tubules to reabsorb glucose: Kidney glucose reabsorption by SGLT transporters does not need insulin.
  • E. The loop of Henle has stopped reabsorbing glucose: Glucose is reabsorbed in the proximal tubule, not the loop of Henle.

Key point: Glycosuria occurs when filtered glucose exceeds the proximal tubule's transport maximum (threshold about 180-200 mg/dL).

Question 2 · Respiratory system · easy

A 25-year-old schoolteacher has had a night cough, wheeze and chest tightness, worse with cold air and exercise, for 3 months. She records her peak expiratory flow (PEF) twice daily for two weeks. Her readings are about 300 L/min in the early morning and 420 L/min in the evening.

What does this pattern most suggest?

  1. A. Asthma
  2. B. Pulmonary fibrosis
  3. C. Normal variation in a healthy person
  4. D. Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease
  5. E. Poor technique only
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Asthma

Peak expiratory flow reflects the calibre of the large and medium airways and depends on effort. In asthma, airway narrowing varies with time, often worst in the early morning ("morning dipping"). Variability is calculated as (highest − lowest) ÷ mean; here (420 − 300) ÷ 360 ≈ 33%, well above the normal range. Peak flow diaries help diagnose asthma, judge severity and give early warning of attacks.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Pulmonary fibrosis: Fibrosis gives a fixed restrictive pattern with little day-to-day change in peak flow.
  • C. Normal variation in a healthy person: Healthy people vary by only a few per cent through the day, much less than her 300 to 420 L/min.
  • D. Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease: COPD gives persistent obstruction with little daily variation.
  • E. Poor technique only: A regular morning dip over two weeks is a consistent pattern, not random poor technique.

Key point: Marked diurnal variation in peak expiratory flow (morning dipping) suggests asthma.

Question 3 · Haematology and immunity · easy

A patient's haematocrit rises from 45% to 65% because of polycythaemia.

What effect does this have on blood flow?

  1. A. Viscosity and resistance rise
  2. B. Viscosity falls, increasing flow
  3. C. Oxygen delivery increases
  4. D. Resistance falls
  5. E. There is no effect on flow
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Viscosity and resistance rise

By Poiseuille's law, resistance is proportional to viscosity. Blood viscosity depends mainly on the haematocrit and rises steeply at higher values: a haematocrit of 60–70% can make blood several times as viscous as at 40%. Flow falls and cardiac work rises, so oxygen delivery may fall despite the higher oxygen content, and the risk of thrombosis increases. In anaemia, low viscosity lowers resistance, which, with vasodilatation, explains the high-output circulation.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Viscosity falls, increasing flow: Viscosity rises with more red cells, so flow falls; viscosity falls only in anaemia.
  • C. Oxygen delivery increases: Each unit of blood carries more oxygen, but at very high haematocrit flow falls so much that delivery to tissues can fall.
  • D. Resistance falls: Resistance is proportional to viscosity, so thicker blood raises resistance.
  • E. There is no effect on flow: Viscosity is one of the main factors in resistance (Poiseuille), so flow is strongly affected.

Key point: Viscosity rises steeply with haematocrit, increasing vascular resistance and thrombosis risk.

Question 4 · Cardiovascular system · easy

A healthy 20-year-old medical student listens to her friend's heart in the second left intercostal space. The second heart sound is single during expiration but splits into two components during inspiration, with the second part coming later. When her friend holds his breath in expiration, the sound is single again.

What is the main reason for this splitting during inspiration?

  1. A. Increased venous return to the right ventricle delays pulmonary valve closure
  2. B. Decreased venous return to the right ventricle makes the pulmonary valve close early
  3. C. Increased left ventricular filling delays aortic valve closure
  4. D. The aortic valve closes after the pulmonary valve during inspiration
  5. E. Delayed conduction in the right bundle branch
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Increased venous return to the right ventricle delays pulmonary valve closure

In inspiration the fall in intrathoracic pressure draws more blood into the right side of the heart and holds more blood in the expanded pulmonary vessels. The right ventricle has a larger stroke volume and a longer ejection, so the pulmonary component (P2) is delayed, while the aortic component (A2) comes slightly earlier. This physiological splitting disappears in expiration. Fixed splitting is typical of an atrial septal defect; reversed (paradoxical) splitting occurs with left bundle branch block or severe aortic stenosis.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Decreased venous return to the right ventricle makes the pulmonary valve close early: Venous return to the right heart rises, not falls, during inspiration.
  • C. Increased left ventricular filling delays aortic valve closure: Left ventricular filling falls slightly in inspiration, so A2 comes a little earlier, not later.
  • D. The aortic valve closes after the pulmonary valve during inspiration: Normally A2 comes first; P2 after A2 is reversed only in paradoxical splitting.
  • E. Delayed conduction in the right bundle branch: Right bundle branch block gives wide splitting, but it is a disease, not the cause of normal splitting.

Key point: Physiological splitting of S2 in inspiration results from delayed pulmonary valve closure due to increased right heart filling.

Question 5 · Endocrine and reproduction · easy

A 60-year-old man with a flare of rheumatoid arthritis is started on prednisolone 40 mg daily. He has no diabetes and his fasting glucose was 94 mg/dL before. Two weeks later his blood glucose is high, at 252 mg/dL in the afternoon, and he is thirsty. His appetite has increased.

Which action of glucocorticoids mainly raises blood glucose?

  1. A. Increased hepatic gluconeogenesis
  2. B. Increased insulin secretion
  3. C. Increased glucose uptake into muscle
  4. D. Increased glycogen breakdown in muscle
  5. E. Reduced glucagon secretion
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Increased hepatic gluconeogenesis

Cortisol stimulates gluconeogenesis in the liver, provides amino acids for it by breaking down muscle protein, and reduces glucose use by peripheral tissues (insulin resistance). It also mobilises fatty acids, suppresses inflammation and immune responses, is needed for the full vasoconstrictor action of catecholamines (permissive effect), and helps maintain blood pressure. Its secretion follows a diurnal rhythm, highest in the early morning. Long-term excess causes the features of Cushing's syndrome.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Increased insulin secretion: Insulin rises in response, but this lowers glucose rather than raising it.
  • C. Increased glucose uptake into muscle: Cortisol reduces glucose uptake by muscle and fat; it does not increase it.
  • D. Increased glycogen breakdown in muscle: Cortisol increases liver glycogen; muscle glycogen cannot release glucose into the blood anyway.
  • E. Reduced glucagon secretion: Lower glucagon would reduce glucose, not raise it.

Key point: Cortisol raises blood glucose mainly by increasing gluconeogenesis and causing insulin resistance.

Question 6 · Cell, nerve and muscle · easy

A 30-year-old man stubs his bare toe on a door. He feels a sharp, well-localised pain at once, making him pull his foot back, followed a second or two later by a dull, burning, throbbing, poorly localised ache that lasts for minutes. Rubbing the toe eases the ache a little.

Which fibres carry the second, dull ache?

  1. A. C fibres
  2. B. Aδ fibres
  3. C. Aα fibres
  4. D. Aβ fibres
  5. E. B fibres
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. C fibres

Fast pain is carried by thinly myelinated Aδ fibres (about 6–30 m/s) and is sharp and well localised; it uses glutamate and the neospinothalamic tract. Slow pain is carried by unmyelinated C fibres (about 0.5–2 m/s) and is burning, aching and poorly localised; it uses substance P and glutamate and the paleospinothalamic pathway. Aα fibres (largest, fastest) supply skeletal muscle and carry muscle spindle signals; Aβ fibres carry touch and pressure; B fibres are preganglionic autonomic.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Aδ fibres: A-delta fibres are thinly myelinated and faster, so they carry the first sharp, well-localised pain.
  • C. Aα fibres: A-alpha fibres are the largest; they carry motor signals and muscle spindle input, not pain.
  • D. Aβ fibres: A-beta fibres carry touch and pressure; rubbing activates them, which is why rubbing eases the ache.
  • E. B fibres: B fibres are preganglionic autonomic fibres and do not carry pain.

Key point: Fast sharp pain travels in Aδ fibres; slow burning pain in unmyelinated C fibres.

Question 7 · Gastrointestinal system · easy

The smell and sight of food, and even thinking about a meal, increase gastric acid secretion before any food reaches the stomach.

Through which pathway does this cephalic phase work?

  1. A. The vagus nerve
  2. B. Secretin from the duodenum
  3. C. Sympathetic nerves
  4. D. Cholecystokinin
  5. E. The enteric nervous system alone
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. The vagus nerve

Gastric secretion has three phases. The cephalic phase (about 30%) is triggered by sight, smell, taste and thought of food through the vagus; vagotomy abolishes it. The gastric phase (about 60%) is triggered by distension and peptides in the stomach through local and vagovagal reflexes and gastrin. The intestinal phase is small; chyme in the duodenum mostly inhibits gastric secretion and emptying through secretin, CCK, GIP and the enterogastric reflex.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Secretin from the duodenum: Secretin is released only when acid reaches the duodenum, and it inhibits acid secretion rather than starting it.
  • C. Sympathetic nerves: Sympathetic activity reduces gut secretion and movement; the cephalic phase is parasympathetic.
  • D. Cholecystokinin: CCK is released when fat and protein reach the duodenum, after the meal has been eaten, so it cannot act before food arrives.
  • E. The enteric nervous system alone: The enteric nerves respond to stretch and chemicals inside the gut; the cephalic phase starts in the brain before food reaches the stomach.

Key point: The cephalic phase of gastric secretion is vagally mediated; the gastric phase is the largest.

Question 8 · Nervous system and special senses · easy

A soldier badly wounded in battle reports feeling little pain until the fighting stops.

Which system is mainly responsible for this suppression of pain?

  1. A. Periaqueductal grey and endogenous opioids
  2. B. Destruction of the spinothalamic tract by the wound
  3. C. Overactivity of the sympathetic nervous system blocking pain fibres
  4. D. Release of histamine
  5. E. Closure of the dorsal columns
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Periaqueductal grey and endogenous opioids

The brain has its own analgesia system. Neurons in the periaqueductal grey of the midbrain project to the nucleus raphe magnus and other brainstem nuclei, which send serotonergic and noradrenergic fibres down to the dorsal horn. There, interneurons releasing enkephalins act on opioid receptors to inhibit pain transmission, presynaptically and postsynaptically. Stress, fear and intense activity activate this system. Opioid drugs act on the same receptors; naloxone blocks them.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Destruction of the spinothalamic tract by the wound: The wound has not cut the spinothalamic tract; the pain pathway is intact.
  • C. Overactivity of the sympathetic nervous system blocking pain fibres: Sympathetic activity does not directly block pain fibres.
  • D. Release of histamine: Histamine increases pain and itch.
  • E. Closure of the dorsal columns: The dorsal columns carry touch and position sense, not pain.

Key point: The PAG-raphe descending system and endogenous opioids suppress pain in the dorsal horn.

Question 9 · Renal physiology, body fluids and acid-base · easy

Tubular fluid entering the descending limb of the loop of Henle in a juxtamedullary nephron is isotonic (about 300 mOsm/kg). At the tip of the loop, deep in the medulla, it reaches about 1200 mOsm/kg.

Why does the fluid become so concentrated in the descending limb?

  1. A. Water leaves; salt stays in
  2. B. Salt is actively pumped into the descending limb
  3. C. The descending limb is impermeable to water
  4. D. ADH acts on the descending limb
  5. E. Urea is secreted into the descending limb in large amounts
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Water leaves; salt stays in

The thin descending limb is highly permeable to water (aquaporin-1) but relatively impermeable to salt. As it dips into the increasingly hypertonic medulla, water leaves by osmosis, and the fluid becomes concentrated. The ascending limb is the opposite: impermeable to water, and the thick part actively pumps out NaCl, so the fluid becomes dilute (about 100 mOsm/kg) by the time it reaches the distal tubule.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Salt is actively pumped into the descending limb: The descending limb does not actively take up salt.
  • C. The descending limb is impermeable to water: Being impermeable to water describes the ascending limb, which lets salt out but keeps water in.
  • D. ADH acts on the descending limb: ADH acts mainly on the collecting duct, not the descending limb.
  • E. Urea is secreted into the descending limb in large amounts: Some urea enters the descending limb, but water loss is the main reason the fluid concentrates.

Key point: The descending limb is water-permeable; the ascending limb is water-impermeable and pumps out NaCl.

Question 10 · Respiratory system · easy

A 30-year-old woman has a haemoglobin of 10 g/dL and an arterial oxygen saturation of 98%. Take the oxygen-carrying capacity of haemoglobin as 1.34 mL/g and ignore dissolved oxygen.

What is her arterial oxygen content?

  1. A. About 1.3 mL/dL
  2. B. About 9.8 mL/dL
  3. C. About 13 mL/dL
  4. D. About 20 mL/dL
  5. E. About 98 mL/dL
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. About 13 mL/dL

Oxygen content = (1.34 × Hb × SaO₂) + (0.003 × PaO₂). Here 1.34 × 10 × 0.98 ≈ 13.1 mL/dL, plus about 0.3 mL/dL dissolved. Her PaO₂ and saturation are normal, yet her oxygen content is about a third lower than normal because she has less haemoglobin. This is why anaemia causes breathlessness and fatigue on effort without lowering the oxygen saturation.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. About 1.3 mL/dL: 1.3 mL/dL misplaces the decimal point by a factor of ten.
  • B. About 9.8 mL/dL: 9.8 mL/dL multiplies haemoglobin by saturation but forgets that each gram carries 1.34 mL.
  • D. About 20 mL/dL: About 20 mL/dL is the normal content with a haemoglobin of 15 g/dL, not 10.
  • E. About 98 mL/dL: 98 is the saturation as a percentage, not the oxygen content.

Key point: O₂ content ≈ 1.34 × Hb × SaO₂ (+ 0.003 × PaO₂); anaemia lowers content but not PaO₂ or SaO₂.
