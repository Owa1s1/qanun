Question 1 · Rheumatology · medium

A 48-year-old woman in Mingora has had 3 years of dry eyes, dry mouth and difficulty swallowing dry food, with recurrent parotid swelling. Schirmer test is 3 mm in 5 minutes bilaterally. Anti-Ro (SSA) and anti-La (SSB) antibodies are positive, as is rheumatoid factor. In the last 2 months she has noticed a firm, persistent enlargement of the left parotid gland, with night sweats and a 4 kg weight loss. C4 is low and serum cryoglobulins are positive.

Which complication is most likely?

  1. A. MALT B-cell lymphoma
  2. B. Thymoma
  3. C. Hepatocellular carcinoma
  4. D. Squamous cell carcinoma of the lung
  5. E. Adenocarcinoma of the colon
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. MALT B-cell lymphoma

Primary Sjögren syndrome causes dry eyes and mouth from lymphocytic infiltration of lacrimal and salivary glands, with anti-Ro and anti-La antibodies, and extraglandular disease including arthralgia, Raynaud phenomenon, interstitial lung disease, renal tubular acidosis, peripheral neuropathy and vasculitis. Non-Hodgkin lymphoma, typically extranodal marginal zone (MALT) B-cell lymphoma of the parotid, develops in about 5-10%, and risk factors are persistent parotid swelling, palpable purpura, low C4, cryoglobulinaemia, monoclonal gammopathy and lymphopenia. Biopsy of the enlarging gland establishes the diagnosis, with staging by CT or PET-CT. Anti-Ro antibodies carry a risk of congenital heart block in pregnancy, so fetal monitoring is needed.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Thymoma: Thymoma is associated with myasthenia gravis, not Sjögren syndrome, and does not cause parotid enlargement.
  • C. Hepatocellular carcinoma: Hepatocellular carcinoma complicates cirrhosis or chronic viral hepatitis; her cryoglobulins and low C4 are not explained by liver cancer.
  • D. Squamous cell carcinoma of the lung: Lung squamous cell carcinoma is linked to smoking, not Sjögren syndrome, and does not cause persistent parotid enlargement.
  • E. Adenocarcinoma of the colon: Colorectal cancer is not a feature of Sjögren syndrome, and her parotid enlargement with low C4 and cryoglobulins points to lymphoma.

Key point: Recognise lymphoma as the complication of Sjögren syndrome suggested by persistent parotid enlargement and low C4.

Question 2 · Acute medicine, poisoning and critical care · medium

An 80-year-old woman in Peshawar attends the clinic after her second fall in six months, without injury or loss of consciousness. She has osteoarthritis of the knees, takes amlodipine, paracetamol and calcium, and is slow to rise from a chair. She lives with her family in a house with steps and uses a stick. Her blood pressure is 134/80 mmHg lying and 128/76 standing. Vision with glasses and ECG are normal. Her vitamin D level is 31 ng/mL. Timed up-and-go is 20 seconds. She is afraid of falling again and has reduced activity.

Which intervention is most effective in reducing her falls?

  1. A. Prolonged bed rest
  2. B. A strength and balance exercise programme
  3. C. Hip protectors only
  4. D. High-dose vitamin D supplements added to her calcium
  5. E. A bedtime sedative
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. A strength and balance exercise programme

Falls affect about one third of people over 65 each year and cause fractures, loss of confidence and admission. Assessment includes history (circumstances, syncope), orthostatic blood pressure, medication review (sedatives, antihypertensives, anticholinergics), vision, feet and footwear, cognition, gait and balance (timed up-and-go above 12-14 seconds is abnormal), bone health and home hazards. The most effective intervention is multicomponent: exercise programmes of strength and balance training (Otago, tai chi) at least 3 hours weekly, medication reduction, treating postural hypotension, cataract surgery, and home modification. Vitamin D helps only in deficiency. Hip protectors and alarms have limited benefit. Review the cause of syncope if it occurred. In Pakistani households, steps, wet floors, poor lighting and squat toilets are common hazards.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Prolonged bed rest: Immobility causes deconditioning, weakness and further falls and is harmful.
  • C. Hip protectors only: These may reduce fractures in institutional care but do not prevent falls.
  • D. High-dose vitamin D supplements added to her calcium: Vitamin D supplementation does not reduce falls in people with sufficient levels, and high doses can increase falls.
  • E. A bedtime sedative: Sedatives increase the risk of falls and fractures and should be avoided.

Key point: Offer strength and balance exercise as the most effective intervention to prevent falls.

Question 3 · Cardiology · medium

A 28-year-old woman with hypertension since the age of 25 attends a hospital in Peshawar. Her blood pressure is 178/108 mmHg despite amlodipine. Ramipril 5 mg was started ten days ago, and now creatinine has risen from 0.9 to 2.1 mg/dL, with potassium 5.5 mEq/L (3.5-5.0). A bruit is heard over the abdomen on the right side. Ultrasound shows a right kidney of 8.6 cm and a left kidney of 11.8 cm. She is not pregnant. Urine shows no protein or blood.

Which investigation is most appropriate next?

  1. A. Renal artery duplex ultrasound
  2. B. Contrast CT angiography of the renal arteries
  3. C. Intra-arterial renal angiography
  4. D. Renal biopsy
  5. E. Plasma aldosterone-renin ratio
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Renal artery duplex ultrasound

A young woman with severe hypertension, an abdominal bruit, unequal kidney size and an abrupt rise in creatinine after starting an ACE inhibitor has bilateral or unilateral renal artery stenosis until proved otherwise, probably fibromuscular dysplasia at this age. ACE inhibitors reduce efferent arteriolar tone and glomerular pressure in a kidney dependent on angiotensin II, causing acute kidney injury and hyperkalaemia; stop the drug. The 2018 ESC/ESH guideline recommends duplex ultrasound, CT angiography or MR angiography; duplex is preferred without contrast when kidney function is impaired. Treatment of fibromuscular dysplasia is balloon angioplasty without stenting; atherosclerotic stenosis is managed medically unless there is flash pulmonary oedema or progressive kidney failure.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Contrast CT angiography of the renal arteries: Would expose the kidneys to iodinated contrast when creatinine is already 2.1 mg/dL, risking further injury.
  • C. Intra-arterial renal angiography: Is invasive and uses contrast, and is reserved for planned intervention after non-invasive diagnosis.
  • D. Renal biopsy: Is for glomerular disease with proteinuria or haematuria, which her normal urinalysis does not suggest.
  • E. Plasma aldosterone-renin ratio: Primary aldosteronism is a cause of resistant hypertension in the young, but it does not explain unequal kidney sizes, a bruit and a creatinine rise on an ACE inhibitor; the ratio is also unreliable on ramipril.

Key point: Investigate suspected renal artery stenosis with duplex ultrasound when creatinine is raised.

Question 4 · Endocrinology and diabetes · medium

A 40-year-old woman in Gilgit with Addison disease takes hydrocortisone 15 mg on waking, 5 mg at lunch and 5 mg in the evening, and fludrocortisone 0.1 mg daily. She is planning a week-long trek to Fairy Meadows, which is hours from the nearest hospital. She asks what to do if she develops a fever. She has a steroid card but no emergency injection. She is otherwise well, and her blood pressure and electrolytes are normal.

Which advice is most appropriate?

  1. A. Stop fludrocortisone during any illness
  2. B. Reduce hydrocortisone by half until the fever settles
  3. C. Make no change because her levels are stable
  4. D. Take dexamethasone tablets in place of hydrocortisone
  5. E. Double doses when febrile; carry injectable steroid
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Double doses when febrile; carry injectable steroid

In adrenal insufficiency, cortisol requirements rise with fever, infection, trauma and surgery, so replacement must be increased. Sick-day rules: double the daily hydrocortisone dose when temperature exceeds 37.5 °C and triple it above 39 °C, continued until recovery (usually 2-3 days), then return to normal. Continue fludrocortisone. If vomiting, diarrhoea or collapse occurs, give hydrocortisone 100 mg intramuscularly and go to hospital. Patients and relatives should be trained to inject, and should carry a steroid card or alert bracelet, especially when travelling to remote areas such as northern Pakistan, where help may be far away. Extra doses are needed before minor procedures. Adequate replacement allows a normal life expectancy.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Stop fludrocortisone during any illness: Fludrocortisone is continued, and there is no benefit from stopping it; the key change in illness is more hydrocortisone, not less.
  • B. Reduce hydrocortisone by half until the fever settles: Illness increases cortisol requirement, so halving the dose invites crisis; doses must be increased, not decreased, during fever.
  • C. Make no change because her levels are stable: Stable biochemistry at rest does not cover the increased need during fever; without extra steroid she risks adrenal crisis.
  • D. Take dexamethasone tablets in place of hydrocortisone: Dexamethasone lacks mineralocorticoid effect and is not the replacement drug for sick-day dosing; the usual hydrocortisone dose should be increased.

Key point: Teach sick-day rules and emergency injection for adrenal insufficiency.

Question 5 · Gastroenterology · medium

A 36-year-old woman attends a hospital in Bannu with three months of loose stools four to five times a day with crampy lower abdominal pain. She has no nocturnal diarrhoea, blood, weight loss or fever, and her examination is normal. Haemoglobin is 13.2 g/dL (12-15), CRP is 0.3 mg/dL (below 0.5), ferritin is normal and tissue transglutaminase IgA is negative. TSH is normal and stool cultures and parasite examination are negative. She has no family history of bowel cancer or inflammatory bowel disease, and is very anxious about cancer.

Which test best separates inflammatory bowel disease from a functional disorder in her?

  1. A. Colonoscopy in all patients
  2. B. Faecal elastase
  3. C. Faecal calprotectin
  4. D. Serum CA-125
  5. E. Capsule endoscopy
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Faecal calprotectin

Faecal calprotectin, a neutrophil protein released into the stool in intestinal inflammation, is a sensitive marker of inflammatory bowel disease. NICE DG11 recommends it to differentiate inflammatory bowel disease from irritable bowel syndrome in adults with suspected IBS: a value below 50 µg/g makes inflammatory disease very unlikely, 50-250 µg/g is borderline (repeat), and above 250 µg/g warrants specialist referral and colonoscopy. It can be raised by NSAIDs, infection and colorectal cancer. In the initial assessment of chronic diarrhoea, also check blood count, CRP, ferritin, coeliac serology and thyroid function. Alarm features such as bleeding, weight loss, anaemia, age over 50, or family history need endoscopy. In Pakistan, tuberculosis and parasitic infections remain important causes to exclude.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Colonoscopy in all patients: Is invasive and not needed as a first test in a young woman with no alarm features and normal blood tests.
  • B. Faecal elastase: Assesses pancreatic exocrine function and does not detect bowel inflammation.
  • D. Serum CA-125: Is an ovarian cancer marker that can be raised in benign conditions and cannot distinguish bowel inflammation from irritable bowel.
  • E. Capsule endoscopy: Is a specialised small bowel test used after other investigations, and not a first-line screening test.

Key point: Use faecal calprotectin to separate inflammatory bowel disease from functional bowel disorders.

Question 6 · Haematology and oncology · medium

A 68-year-old man in Mansehra has a routine blood test before a cataract operation. He is asymptomatic, with no fever, night sweats or weight loss. WBC is 48,000/µL with lymphocytes 40,000/µL, haemoglobin 13.2 g/dL (13-17) and platelets 190,000/µL. The film shows mature small lymphocytes and smudge cells. Flow cytometry shows CD5, CD19 and CD23 positive B cells. Examination shows small mobile cervical nodes of about 1 cm and no splenomegaly or hepatomegaly. Direct antiglobulin test is negative and LDH is normal.

What is the most appropriate management?

  1. A. Start chlorambucil now
  2. B. Watch and wait
  3. C. Rituximab alone
  4. D. Splenectomy
  5. E. Allogeneic stem cell transplant
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Watch and wait

Chronic lymphocytic leukaemia is a clonal accumulation of mature CD5+, CD19+, CD23+ B lymphocytes, the commonest adult leukaemia in Western countries and less common but present in South Asia, usually found incidentally in older people. Staging uses the Rai or Binet systems. Smudge cells on the film are typical. Treatment is indicated only for active disease: progressive marrow failure, massive or symptomatic splenomegaly or lymphadenopathy, autoimmune cytopenias unresponsive to steroids, constitutional symptoms or a rapid lymphocyte doubling time under 6 months. Asymptomatic early disease is observed. When needed, modern treatment is a BTK inhibitor (ibrutinib, acalabrutinib) or venetoclax with obinutuzumab; fludarabine-cyclophosphamide-rituximab is used for IGHV-mutated, TP53-intact fit patients. Check TP53 and IGHV before treatment. Vaccinate against pneumococcus and influenza and avoid live vaccines.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Start chlorambucil now: Early treatment of asymptomatic early-stage CLL does not improve survival and exposes him to toxicity and secondary malignancy.
  • C. Rituximab alone: Rituximab has no role in asymptomatic early disease and risks infusion reactions and infections.
  • D. Splenectomy: Splenectomy is for refractory cytopenias or massive splenomegaly and is not indicated in early CLL.
  • E. Allogeneic stem cell transplant: Transplant is a high-risk option for young fit patients with high-risk, refractory CLL and not for asymptomatic early disease.

Key point: Observe asymptomatic early-stage CLL without treatment.

Question 7 · Infectious diseases · medium

A 52-year-old farmer in Dir has an 8 cm contaminated laceration on the forearm from a sickle used in the field 4 hours ago. The wound contains soil and devitalised tissue. As far as he knows he has never received tetanus vaccine. He has no fever or muscle stiffness and is otherwise well. The wound has been irrigated and will be debrided.

Which tetanus prophylaxis is most appropriate?

  1. A. Tetanus toxoid given as a single dose
  2. B. Toxoid and immunoglobulin, different sites
  3. C. Human tetanus immunoglobulin given on its own
  4. D. Oral penicillin V for seven days
  5. E. Wound cleaning and observation
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Toxoid and immunoglobulin, different sites

Tetanus prophylaxis depends on the wound and immunisation history. Tetanus-prone wounds include contamination with soil or manure, devitalised tissue, puncture wounds, burns and delayed presentation. For people with no or uncertain history of vaccination, a tetanus-prone wound needs thorough debridement, tetanus toxoid (starting a primary course, with later doses at 1 and 6-12 months) and human tetanus immunoglobulin 250-500 IU intramuscularly at a different site. Fully immunised people need only a booster if the last dose was long ago, and clean minor wounds need toxoid only when the course is incomplete. Antibiotics are adjuncts for heavily contaminated wounds. Equine antitoxin is used if human immunoglobulin is unavailable. Community vaccination, including in pregnancy, is the main prevention.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Tetanus toxoid given as a single dose: Toxoid alone gives active immunity only after days to weeks; an unvaccinated patient with a tetanus-prone wound needs immediate passive protection as well.
  • C. Human tetanus immunoglobulin given on its own: Immunoglobulin alone gives temporary passive protection but no lasting immunity; he also needs the toxoid course started.
  • D. Oral penicillin V for seven days: Antibiotics do not neutralise toxin and do not replace immunisation; penicillin may be an adjunct but not alone.
  • E. Wound cleaning and observation: Wound care is essential but insufficient for a contaminated wound in an unvaccinated man; without prophylaxis the risk of tetanus is substantial.

Key point: Give toxoid and human tetanus immunoglobulin at separate sites for a tetanus-prone wound in an unvaccinated patient.

Question 8 · Hepatology · medium

A 30-year-old woman with acute liver failure from hepatitis E is in the intensive care unit of a hospital in Karachi with grade 4 encephalopathy (Glasgow Coma Scale 6), and has been intubated. One pupil is larger and sluggish, blood pressure has risen to 168/98 mmHg and pulse has fallen to 52/min. The head of the bed is already raised to 30 degrees and sodium is 138 mEq/L (135-145). CT shows cerebral oedema with effacement of the sulci. She is not on any sedative infusion that affects the pupils.

Which treatment should be given first?

  1. A. Intravenous dexamethasone
  2. B. Prolonged hyperventilation to PaCO2 20 mmHg
  3. C. Furosemide infusion
  4. D. Oral lactulose
  5. E. IV mannitol bolus
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. IV mannitol bolus

Cerebral oedema and intracranial hypertension complicate grade 3-4 encephalopathy in acute liver failure, caused by ammonia-driven astrocyte swelling, and herniation is a leading cause of death. Signs include pupillary changes, hypertension, bradycardia and decerebrate posturing. Management includes head elevation to 30 degrees, avoidance of agitation and hypo-osmolar fluids, intubation and sedation (propofol), maintaining sodium at 145-155 mEq/L with hypertonic saline, intravenous mannitol (0.25-0.5 g/kg, if serum osmolality is below 320 mOsm/kg), brief hyperventilation as a bridge for herniation, and consideration of ICP monitoring. Steroids are ineffective. Urgent liver transplantation assessment is essential. CT can show oedema but may be normal early.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Intravenous dexamethasone: Does not reduce intracranial pressure in acute liver failure and raises the risk of infection and bleeding.
  • B. Prolonged hyperventilation to PaCO2 20 mmHg: Causes cerebral vasoconstriction and ischaemia if prolonged; brief hyperventilation is only a bridge for impending herniation.
  • C. Furosemide infusion: Dehydrates the patient and lowers blood pressure and cerebral perfusion without reliably lowering intracranial pressure.
  • D. Oral lactulose: Does not treat raised intracranial pressure and carries a risk of aspiration in a patient with cerebral oedema.

Key point: Give intravenous mannitol or hypertonic saline for cerebral oedema in acute liver failure.

Question 9 · Neurology · medium

A 34-year-old truck driver in Sukkur has had three attacks over 2 weeks, each waking him at about 2 a.m. with severe stabbing pain behind the right eye lasting 45 minutes. During attacks the right eye waters and the nose runs, the eyelid droops, and he paces the room because he cannot lie still. He is well between attacks and neurological examination is normal. He smokes 20 cigarettes a day. Blood pressure is 126/80 mmHg.

Which treatment should be used to abort an attack?

  1. A. Oral paracetamol 1 g with metoclopramide 10 mg
  2. B. Oral ibuprofen 400 mg three times daily
  3. C. Oral amitriptyline 10 mg at night
  4. D. High-flow oxygen by mask at 12-15 L/min
  5. E. Oral propranolol 40 mg twice daily
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. High-flow oxygen by mask at 12-15 L/min

The attacks are strictly unilateral periorbital pain lasting 15-180 minutes with ipsilateral lacrimation, nasal congestion or ptosis and restlessness, occurring daily in bouts: this is cluster headache, a trigeminal autonomic cephalalgia. Acute treatment is 100% oxygen at 12-15 L/min by non-rebreather mask for 15 minutes, or subcutaneous sumatriptan 6 mg or nasal zolmitriptan; oral triptans and analgesics act too slowly. Verapamil, with ECG monitoring for PR prolongation, is the first preventive drug during a bout, and short-course prednisolone is used as a bridge. Smoking and alcohol are triggers. MRI is advised at first presentation to exclude secondary causes. Home oxygen is often impractical, so injected sumatriptan is a useful alternative.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Oral paracetamol 1 g with metoclopramide 10 mg: This is a migraine regimen; oral drugs act too slowly for attacks that peak within minutes, so it will not abort his 45-minute episodes.
  • B. Oral ibuprofen 400 mg three times daily: Oral ibuprofen takes 30-60 minutes to act, as long as his whole attack; oral analgesics are ineffective for this sudden, intense pain.
  • C. Oral amitriptyline 10 mg at night: Amitriptyline is a preventive for tension-type headache and chronic migraine; it does nothing to abort a 45-minute attack and is not used for his pattern.
  • E. Oral propranolol 40 mg twice daily: Propranolol is a daily migraine preventive that does not abort an attack; prophylaxis for his pattern of nightly bouts would instead be verapamil.

Key point: Choose oxygen or injected triptan for the acute treatment of cluster headache.

Question 10 · Nephrology · medium

A 26-year-old woman in Abbottabad was found to have a blood pressure of 172/104 mmHg at a pre-employment medical. Repeated clinic readings and ambulatory monitoring confirm hypertension. She does not smoke, takes no oral contraceptives or other drugs, and has no family history of hypertension. A soft bruit is heard in the right upper abdomen. Potassium is 3.6 mEq/L (3.5-5.0), creatinine 0.8 mg/dL (0.6-1.1) and urinalysis is normal. Ultrasound shows a right kidney 1.2 cm smaller than the left. CT angiography shows a 'string of beads' appearance of the mid and distal right renal artery with no atheroma. Imaging of the cerebral arteries is normal.

What is the most appropriate treatment?

  1. A. Lifelong amlodipine without any procedure
  2. B. Angioplasty with stent placement
  3. C. Surgical aortorenal bypass graft
  4. D. Laparoscopic right nephrectomy
  5. E. Balloon angioplasty without stenting
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Balloon angioplasty without stenting

Fibromuscular dysplasia is a non-atherosclerotic, non-inflammatory arterial disease that mainly affects women aged 15-50. Multifocal disease gives the characteristic string-of-beads appearance of the mid and distal renal artery, unlike atherosclerotic stenosis, which affects the ostium in older smokers with vascular disease. Atherosclerotic renal artery stenosis is managed medically because trials such as ASTRAL and CORAL showed no benefit from routine stenting. In contrast, international consensus and ESC guidance recommend percutaneous balloon angioplasty without stenting for hypertension due to renal fibromuscular dysplasia; cure is more likely in younger patients with a shorter history. Stents are reserved for dissection or failed angioplasty, and surgery for complex branch disease or aneurysm. Because fibromuscular dysplasia often affects several vascular beds, patients should have imaging from head to pelvis once to look for aneurysms and dissections, as was done here.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Lifelong amlodipine without any procedure: Drugs may control her pressure, but a young woman with fibromuscular dysplasia often has a lasting cure with angioplasty, sparing decades of medication.
  • B. Angioplasty with stent placement: Stenting is not routine in fibromuscular dysplasia; it is reserved for dissection or a failed balloon procedure, as it adds restenosis and limits later surgery.
  • C. Surgical aortorenal bypass graft: Bypass surgery is kept for complex lesions involving branches or aneurysms, or for failed angioplasty.
  • D. Laparoscopic right nephrectomy: Her right kidney is only slightly small and still functions; nephrectomy is for a non-functioning kidney driving resistant hypertension.

Key point: Treat renovascular hypertension from fibromuscular dysplasia with balloon angioplasty without stenting.
