Question 1 · Trauma and burns · medium

A 45-year-old truck driver from Abbottabad is admitted after a steering wheel impact during a collision. Examination reveals paradoxical inward movement of a segment of his left anterior chest wall during inspiration. RR is 32/min and shallow. His SpO2 is 88% on room air. Arterial blood gas shows pO2 55 mmHg and pCO2 50 mmHg.

What is the main cause of the hypoxia in this condition?

  1. A. Compression of the lung by massive intrapleural haemorrhage
  2. B. Underlying pulmonary contusion disrupting alveolar gas exchange
  3. C. Pain-induced splinting of the surrounding respiratory musculature
  4. D. Pendelluft phenomenon transferring deoxygenated air between lungs
  5. E. Rupture of the left hemidiaphragm with visceral herniation
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Underlying pulmonary contusion disrupting alveolar gas exchange

This patient has a flail chest, defined by fractures of two or more adjacent ribs in two or more places, creating a free-floating segment that moves paradoxically (inward on inspiration). Historically, the hypoxia was attributed to the mechanical inefficiency of this paradoxical movement (the "pendelluft" theory). However, modern ATLS principles dictate that the primary cause of life-threatening hypoxia in flail chest is the invariable presence of a severe underlying pulmonary contusion. The tremendous blunt force required to fracture multiple ribs severely crushes the underlying lung parenchyma, leading to alveolar haemorrhage, oedema, and massive intrapulmonary right-to-left shunting (ventilation-perfusion mismatch). Management focuses on excellent analgesia, supplemental oxygen, and ventilatory support if the contusion worsens.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Compression of the lung by massive intrapleural haemorrhage: While haemothorax can coexist, the defining paradoxical movement points to a chest wall defect where underlying alveolar damage is the primary cause of poor gas exchange.
  • C. Pain-induced splinting of the surrounding respiratory musculature: Pain undoubtedly limits chest expansion, but treating pain alone will not reverse the profound intrinsic alveolar gas exchange deficit caused by the underlying trauma.
  • D. Pendelluft phenomenon transferring deoxygenated air between lungs: The paradoxical movement (pendelluft) contributes slightly to ventilatory inefficiency, but modern trauma physiology recognises this as a minor factor compared to parenchymal damage.
  • E. Rupture of the left hemidiaphragm with visceral herniation: A diaphragmatic rupture causes bowel sounds in the chest and scaphoid abdomen, not the classic paradoxical inward chest wall movement defined by a flail segment.

Key point: Recognise that underlying pulmonary contusion, not mechanical paradox, is the primary driver of hypoxia in a flail chest injury.

Question 2 · Upper gastrointestinal surgery, small bowel and hernia · medium

A 62-year-old man from a village near Dadu attends a tertiary care hospital in Karachi with three months of vomiting large volumes of food eaten many hours earlier. He had burning epigastric pain relieved by meals for years and has recently lost 9 kg. He is thin and dehydrated, with a visible peristaltic wave and a succussion splash. After five days of nasogastric decompression and intravenous saline with potassium, pulse is 84/min, blood pressure 122/76 mmHg, sodium 138 mEq/L (135-145), potassium 4.1 mEq/L (3.5-5.0) and bicarbonate 26 mEq/L (22-28), and the nasogastric aspirate has fallen from 1.8 L to 300 mL daily.

What is the most appropriate next step in management?

  1. A. Gastrojejunostomy with vagotomy
  2. B. Barium meal and follow-through
  3. C. CT of the abdomen
  4. D. Serum gastrin level
  5. E. Upper endoscopy with biopsies
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Upper endoscopy with biopsies

Gastric outlet obstruction arises from a scarred peptic ulcer in the pyloric channel or duodenum, or from malignancy such as antral gastric cancer or periampullary tumours. Initial care is resuscitation, correction of hypochloraemic hypokalaemic alkalosis, nasogastric decompression for several days so the dilated stomach regains tone, and a proton pump inhibitor. Endoscopy with multiple biopsies is then essential, because malignancy must be excluded and this determines treatment. A benign peptic stricture is treated with Helicobacter pylori eradication, acid suppression and endoscopic balloon dilatation, with gastrojejunostomy or antrectomy for failure. Cancer needs staging and resection, or a stent or bypass if incurable. Give thiamine to malnourished patients before feeding.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Gastrojejunostomy with vagotomy: Operating before a tissue diagnosis risks the wrong operation; with a 9 kg weight loss at 62, cancer must be excluded first, as it changes the plan.
  • B. Barium meal and follow-through: Outlines the narrowing but gives no tissue; with the stomach already decompressed for five days, endoscopy shows the lesion and allows biopsy in the same sitting.
  • C. CT of the abdomen: Shows wall thickening, masses and nodes but cannot give histology; it is used to stage a proven cancer, not as the next step before a tissue diagnosis.
  • D. Serum gastrin level: Tests for gastrinoma in recurrent or multiple ulcers; he has a single long-standing ulcer history with obstruction, so the priority is a tissue diagnosis, not a hormone level.

Key point: Obtain endoscopic biopsies in gastric outlet obstruction to exclude malignancy before choosing treatment.

Question 3 · Urology · medium

A newborn baby boy from Peshawar is evaluated in the neonatal clinic. On physical examination, the urethral meatus is located abnormally on the ventral (undersurface) aspect of the penile shaft, approximately midway between the glans and the scrotum. There is a ventral curvature of the penis during erection, and the prepuce (foreskin) is hooded, with a full dorsal hood and deficient ventral skin.

What is the term for the ventral curvature of the penis in this condition?

  1. A. Peyronie's disease
  2. B. Epispadias
  3. C. Phimosis
  4. D. Chordee
  5. E. Paraphimosis
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. Chordee

Hypospadias is a common congenital anomaly of the male urethra occurring in roughly 1 in 300 live births, characterised by the abnormal opening of the urethral meatus on the ventral (undersurface) aspect of the penile shaft, scrotum, or perineum. The condition typically consists of three anatomical features: 1. An ectopic ventral urethral meatus. 2. An incomplete prepuce with a characteristic dorsal hood (excess foreskin dorsally and absent ventrally). 3. Chordee, which is the ventral curvature of the penile shaft caused by fibrous bands or disproportionate development of the corpus spongiosum and dartos fascia on the ventral side. Surgical correction (urethroplasty and chordee release) is typically performed between 6 and 18 months of age.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Peyronie's disease: Peyronie's disease is an acquired fibrotic plaque of the tunica albuginea causing penile curvature in adult men, completely unrelated to congenital hypospadias.
  • B. Epispadias: Epispadias is a rare anomaly where the urethral meatus opens on the dorsal (upper) surface of the penis, directly opposite to hypospadias.
  • C. Phimosis: Phimosis is the inability to retract the foreskin over the glans penis, which can be physiological in newborns or pathological, distinct from abnormal meatal location.
  • E. Paraphimosis: Paraphimosis is an emergency where a retracted foreskin becomes trapped behind the glans and constricts the penis, entirely separate from congenital curvature.

Key point: Define chordee as the ventral penile curvature associated with congenital hypospadias.

Question 4 · Vascular surgery · easy

A 72-year-old woman with known atrial fibrillation who stopped her warfarin attends the emergency department of a hospital in Rawalakot, AJK, three hours after sudden severe pain in her left leg. The foot is pale, cold and numb, with the toes moving weakly. The femoral pulse is palpable, the popliteal and pedal pulses are absent, and the right leg is normal. Pulse is 126/min and irregularly irregular, and blood pressure 148/86 mmHg. Creatinine is 1.0 mg/dL.

Which drug should be given immediately?

  1. A. Oral aspirin 75 mg
  2. B. Prophylactic subcutaneous enoxaparin
  3. C. Oral warfarin loading
  4. D. Intravenous unfractionated heparin
  5. E. Intravenous alteplase
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. Intravenous unfractionated heparin

Acute limb ischaemia presents with pain, pallor, pulselessness, paraesthesia, paralysis and perishing cold, and embolism from the heart in atrial fibrillation is a leading cause. The Rutherford classification guides urgency: this limb is threatened but salvageable if revascularised within hours. Give immediate intravenous unfractionated heparin (commonly a 5,000 unit bolus then an infusion) to prevent propagation of thrombus, with analgesia and oxygen, and arrange urgent imaging or direct embolectomy. Unfractionated heparin is preferred to LMWH because it is titratable, reversible and can be given before an operation. Revascularisation is by Fogarty catheter embolectomy, catheter-directed thrombolysis or bypass. Later, anticoagulation for the atrial fibrillation is resumed.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Oral aspirin 75 mg: Is an antiplatelet that has no effect on clot already in the artery and is far too weak to prevent propagation of thrombus in a threatened limb.
  • B. Prophylactic subcutaneous enoxaparin: A prophylactic dose gives too little anticoagulation to stop propagation of clot in an occluded artery; a full-dose intravenous drug is needed.
  • C. Oral warfarin loading: Takes several days to act and cannot be the first drug; she needs immediate anticoagulation to stop clot propagation while revascularisation is arranged.
  • E. Intravenous alteplase: Systemic thrombolysis is rarely used because of bleeding risk and a slow effect on arterial clot; heparin is given first, and direct revascularisation follows.

Key point: Start intravenous unfractionated heparin immediately in suspected acute limb ischaemia.

Question 5 · Breast · easy

A 31-year-old woman attends a clinic in Muzaffarabad with a lump in the upper outer quadrant of her right breast, noticed three weeks ago. She is not pregnant or breastfeeding. Examination shows a firm, discrete, mobile 2 cm lump with no skin change, nipple discharge or palpable axillary nodes. She has no family history of breast cancer, and the left breast is normal.

Which imaging test should be done first?

  1. A. Mammography of both breasts
  2. B. MRI of both breasts
  3. C. CT of the chest
  4. D. PET-CT
  5. E. Breast ultrasound
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Breast ultrasound

In women under 40, breast tissue is dense, which reduces the sensitivity of mammography, so ultrasound is the first imaging test for a palpable lump, with mammography added if the lump is clinically or sonographically suspicious, or after a core biopsy confirms cancer. From 40 years, mammography and ultrasound are done together. Ultrasound distinguishes solid from cystic lesions, characterises margins (a benign lesion is oval, wider than tall and well circumscribed, while a malignant one is irregular, taller than wide with shadowing), and guides needle biopsy. This is one of the three elements of triple assessment, with clinical examination and pathology, and should be completed at a single visit where possible. MRI and CT are not first-line.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Mammography of both breasts: Is less sensitive in the dense breasts of women under 40 and exposes the breast to radiation; ultrasound is the first test, with mammography added only if suspicious.
  • B. MRI of both breasts: Is a problem-solving tool for dense breasts, lobular cancer or high-risk women and is not first-line for a 2 cm palpable lump in a 31-year-old.
  • C. CT of the chest: Looks for metastases in proven cancer, not a palpable breast lump, and delivers radiation without characterising the lump.
  • D. PET-CT: Is used for staging and recurrence in proven advanced cancer, has poor resolution for small breast lumps and cannot replace ultrasound as a first test.

Key point: Choose ultrasound as the first imaging test for a breast lump in a woman under 40.

Question 6 · Cardiothoracic surgery · medium

A 22-year-old tall, thin man attends a hospital in Gilgit with sudden right-sided pleuritic chest pain and mild breathlessness that began at rest two hours ago. He has never smoked and has no previous chest disease. Respiratory rate is 20/min, oxygen saturation 95% on air, pulse 90/min and blood pressure 120/74 mmHg. Breath sounds are reduced on the right. An erect chest radiograph shows a 3 cm rim of air at the level of the hilum, with no mediastinal shift.

What is the most appropriate management?

  1. A. Observation only
  2. B. Needle aspiration
  3. C. Large-bore chest drain
  4. D. Thoracotomy and pleurectomy
  5. E. Chemical pleurodesis
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Needle aspiration

Primary spontaneous pneumothorax affects tall, thin young men, most often from rupture of apical subpleural blebs, and smoking is a major risk factor. Management follows BTS 2023 guidance. A breathless patient, or a rim of 2 cm or more, needs intervention: needle aspiration (14-16 gauge) up to 2.5 L, or a small-bore chest drain (8-14 Fr) if aspiration fails, with the patient observed or managed ambulatory. Patients who are not breathless with a small rim can be observed. Tension pneumothorax needs immediate decompression. Advise stopping smoking and avoiding air travel until resolution. Refer for surgery after a second ipsilateral episode, a persistent air leak beyond several days, or bilateral pneumothorax. High-altitude residence and trekking are recognised triggers.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Observation only: Observation suits small, minimally symptomatic pneumothoraces; with a rim above 2 cm and breathlessness, active drainage is advised.
  • C. Large-bore chest drain: Large drains are no more effective than aspiration or a small-bore tube for primary pneumothorax and cause more pain.
  • D. Thoracotomy and pleurectomy: Surgery is for recurrence, persistent air leak, bilateral or professional-risk cases, not for a first uncomplicated episode.
  • E. Chemical pleurodesis: Pleurodesis prevents recurrence in patients with a persistent leak or recurrent episodes, and is not used at a first presentation.

Key point: Treat a symptomatic primary pneumothorax with a 3 cm rim by needle aspiration or small-bore drainage.

Question 7 · Colorectal surgery and the appendix · easy

A 19-year-old man attends the emergency department of a hospital in Bajaur with 24 hours of abdominal pain that began around the umbilicus and has since moved to the right lower abdomen. He has lost his appetite and vomited once, and his bowels opened normally this morning. Temperature is 37.9 °C, pulse 96/min and blood pressure 122/76 mmHg. There is marked tenderness with guarding and rebound at McBurney point, and pressing on the left iliac fossa causes pain on the right. White cell count is 14,200/µL (4,000-11,000), CRP 5.4 mg/dL (below 0.5) and urinalysis is normal. Ultrasound is not available.

What is the most appropriate management?

  1. A. Oral antibiotics and review in 48 hours
  2. B. CT of the abdomen before any decision
  3. C. Appendicectomy
  4. D. Colonoscopy
  5. E. Barium follow-through
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Appendicectomy

Acute appendicitis typically begins with central periumbilical pain from visceral stretch that migrates to the right iliac fossa once the parietal peritoneum is inflamed, with anorexia, nausea, low-grade fever, leucocytosis and a raised CRP. In a young man with classic features and peritonism the diagnosis is clinical, supported by scores such as Alvarado, and imaging is kept for atypical presentations, women of reproductive age and older adults. Appendicectomy, laparoscopic or open, is the standard treatment, with a single dose of antibiotic prophylaxis. Antibiotics alone are an option only in selected patients with uncomplicated disease confirmed on imaging without an appendicolith, with a significant failure and recurrence rate, and imaging is often unavailable in district hospitals.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Oral antibiotics and review in 48 hours: Antibiotic-only treatment is a studied option only for uncomplicated cases confirmed on imaging without an appendicolith; he has guarding and rebound, and no imaging, so delay risks perforation.
  • B. CT of the abdomen before any decision: Imaging helps when the diagnosis is unclear; in a young man with classic migration, guarding and rebound, CT adds delay and radiation without changing management.
  • D. Colonoscopy: Examines the colon for tumour or colitis over weeks of symptoms; it is unsafe and unhelpful in acute right iliac fossa peritonism lasting one day with normal bowel habit.
  • E. Barium follow-through: Studies the small bowel for Crohn disease or strictures over weeks of symptoms; barium adds delay and risk of leakage in a patient with acute peritonism of one day.

Key point: Recognise classic acute appendicitis and proceed to appendicectomy without unnecessary imaging.

Question 8 · Endocrine surgery, head and neck · medium

A 38-year-old woman attends a hospital in Mingora, Swat, with three months of episodic pounding headaches, sweating and palpitations, each lasting about 20 minutes. Blood pressure is 184/112 mmHg and pulse 104/min. Plasma free normetanephrine is 6.2 times the upper limit of normal. CT shows a 4.5 cm heterogeneous right adrenal mass with an unenhanced density of 38 Hounsfield units. She is otherwise well and is booked for laparoscopic adrenalectomy in two weeks.

Which drug treatment should be started now to prepare her for surgery?

  1. A. Phenoxybenzamine
  2. B. Propranolol alone
  3. C. Hydrochlorothiazide
  4. D. Spironolactone
  5. E. Intravenous labetalol bolus
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Phenoxybenzamine

Phaeochromocytoma is a catecholamine-secreting tumour of the adrenal medulla. Episodic headache, sweating and palpitations with hypertension and raised plasma or urinary metanephrines establish the diagnosis, and a heterogeneous mass with unenhanced density above 10 HU fits. Before surgery, all functional tumours need alpha-adrenergic blockade for 10-14 days, with phenoxybenzamine (non-selective, irreversible) or a selective alpha-1 blocker such as doxazosin. A high-salt diet and fluids restore volume. Only after alpha-blockade is a beta-blocker added if tachycardia persists. Targets are seated blood pressure below 130/80 mmHg and a standing systolic above 90 mmHg. Genetic testing is advised because up to a third of cases are hereditary.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Propranolol alone: Starting a beta-blocker without prior alpha-blockade leaves alpha-mediated vasoconstriction unopposed and can precipitate a hypertensive crisis or acute heart failure in this woman.
  • C. Hydrochlorothiazide: Lowers pressure by volume contraction, which is harmful because these patients are already volume-depleted from catecholamine excess and prone to profound hypotension after tumour removal.
  • D. Spironolactone: Is used for primary aldosteronism; her pounding headaches and raised normetanephrine point to catecholamine excess, which an aldosterone antagonist does not block.
  • E. Intravenous labetalol bolus: Has stronger beta than alpha action and is not suitable for preparation over two weeks; it is a rescue drug for crises, not a preoperative regimen.

Key point: Start alpha-blockade before any beta-blocker in the preoperative preparation of phaeochromocytoma.

Question 9 · Principles of surgery · medium

A 60-year-old man from Abbottabad is diagnosed with locally advanced, non-metastatic adenocarcinoma of the middle third of the rectum. The tumour heavily invades the mesorectal fascia on MRI. The multidisciplinary tumour board recommends a prolonged course of chemoradiotherapy prior to attempting definitive surgical resection.

What is the main reason for giving this treatment before surgery rather than after?

  1. A. To make surgery unnecessary in most patients
  2. B. To test the tumour's sensitivity to chemotherapy
  3. C. To lower the rate of anastomotic leak
  4. D. To downstage the tumour and allow an R0 resection
  5. E. To shorten the overall treatment time
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. To downstage the tumour and allow an R0 resection

In surgical oncology, "neoadjuvant" therapy refers to treatments (chemotherapy, radiotherapy, or hormones) administered prior to definitive surgery. In locally advanced tumours (like this bulky rectal cancer threatening the surgical margin), attempting immediate surgery carries a massive risk of leaving microscopic disease behind (a positive margin, or R1/R2 resection), guaranteeing local recurrence. The primary goal of neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy in this setting is to shrink and downstage the primary tumour. By pulling the tumour away from the mesorectal fascia, neoadjuvant therapy converts an unresectable or margin-threatening tumour into one that can be completely excised with negative microscopic margins (an R0 resection). Secondary benefits include treating early micrometastases and utilising intact local blood supply for drug delivery before surgery destroys it.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. To make surgery unnecessary in most patients: A complete clinical response occurs only in a minority; neoadjuvant treatment for locally advanced rectal cancer is given to make surgery succeed, not to replace it.
  • B. To test the tumour's sensitivity to chemotherapy: While observing response provides prognostic information, it is a secondary benefit; the primary goal of preoperative radiation is local tumour shrinkage and margin clearance.
  • C. To lower the rate of anastomotic leak: Pelvic radiotherapy damages the tissues and, if anything, raises the risk of leaks and wound complications.
  • E. To shorten the overall treatment time: Neoadjuvant treatment adds weeks before surgery; it is given because it improves margin clearance and local control, not to save time.

Key point: Define the primary goal of neoadjuvant therapy in locally advanced solid tumours as downstaging the disease to facilitate a complete (R0) surgical resection.

Question 10 · Hepatobiliary and pancreatic surgery, and the spleen · easy

A 34-year-old woman attends a clinic in Swabi with three episodes in two months of severe right upper abdominal pain, starting an hour after fatty meals and lasting about three hours before settling with hyoscine. She is afebrile between episodes and has no jaundice. The abdomen is soft and non-tender between attacks. Bilirubin 0.8 mg/dL, ALT 28 U/L (below 40) and white cell count 7,400/µL. Ultrasound shows multiple 5-8 mm gallstones, a gallbladder wall 2 mm thick and a common bile duct of 4 mm. BMI is 31 kg/m².

What is the most appropriate management?

  1. A. Oral ursodeoxycholic acid for dissolution
  2. B. ERCP with sphincterotomy
  3. C. Elective laparoscopic cholecystectomy
  4. D. Extracorporeal shock wave lithotripsy
  5. E. Low-fat diet and observation
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Elective laparoscopic cholecystectomy

Biliary colic is episodic right upper abdominal pain from a stone transiently blocking the cystic duct, with normal inflammatory markers and liver tests between attacks. NICE CG188 advises laparoscopic cholecystectomy for people with symptomatic gallbladder stones, as the symptoms usually recur and complications such as acute cholecystitis, choledocholithiasis and gallstone pancreatitis may follow. Normal bilirubin, ALT and duct calibre make a duct stone unlikely, so MRCP or ERCP is not needed. Dissolution therapy and lithotripsy have high recurrence and are rarely used. Advise weight loss and a lower-fat diet, but these do not replace surgery. Lack of timely theatre access is common in district hospitals, so referral is worthwhile.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Oral ursodeoxycholic acid for dissolution: Dissolves only small cholesterol stones over a year or more, with frequent recurrence; with recurrent attacks and multiple stones, surgery is the definitive option.
  • B. ERCP with sphincterotomy: Treats stones in the bile duct; her bilirubin and liver enzymes are normal and the duct is 4 mm, so no duct stone is suggested.
  • D. Extracorporeal shock wave lithotripsy: Is rarely used, suits only a single stone in a functioning gallbladder and has high recurrence; she has multiple stones with recurrent attacks.
  • E. Low-fat diet and observation: Dietary change does not remove the stones; three attacks in two months with multiple stones carry a continuing risk of cholecystitis and pancreatitis.

Key point: Offer elective laparoscopic cholecystectomy for recurrent biliary colic with gallstones and normal liver tests.
