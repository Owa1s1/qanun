Ophthalmology MCQs for MBBS

10 free ophthalmology questions for every year of MBBS at KMU, UHS and the other Pakistani universities, written in the style of the real exam. Try each one, then open the answer: every wrong option is explained too. Qanun has 126 ophthalmology questions for MBBS, covering ophthalmology.

Question 1 · Ophthalmology · easy

A 3-month-old girl has had a watering left eye since she was 2 weeks old, with yellow crusting on the lashes in the morning. Gentle pressure over the inner corner of the eye brings mucus back out of the punctum. The eye is white, the cornea is clear and of normal size, and she is not bothered by light. She is feeding and growing well.

What is the most appropriate management now?

  1. A. Probing of the duct this week
  2. B. Dacryocystorhinostomy
  3. C. Long-term oral antibiotics
  4. D. Urgent eye pressure check under anaesthesia
  5. E. Lacrimal sac massage
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Lacrimal sac massage

Congenital nasolacrimal duct obstruction is common, affecting about 1 in 10 to 1 in 5 newborns. A membrane at the lower end of the duct, where it opens into the inferior meatus (valve of Hasner), fails to open, so tears and mucus collect in the sac, causing watering and sticky discharge with a white eye. Most cases (about 90%) resolve by 1 year. Treatment is lid hygiene and lacrimal sac massage (pressing over the sac and stroking downwards) to raise pressure in the duct, with antibiotic drops only for infection. Probing is offered if it persists beyond about 12 months. Watering with photophobia and a large or cloudy cornea suggests congenital glaucoma instead, which is urgent.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Probing of the duct this week: Kept for cases that persist past about one year.
  • B. Dacryocystorhinostomy: Not used in infants as a first step.
  • C. Long-term oral antibiotics: Long-term oral antibiotics are not needed for a blocked nasolacrimal duct.
  • D. Urgent eye pressure check under anaesthesia: There are no signs of glaucoma, such as large cornea or photophobia.

Key point: Congenital nasolacrimal duct obstruction usually resolves by one year with sac massage; probing is for persistent cases.

Question 2 · Ophthalmology · easy

A 46-year-old man has had type 2 diabetes for 8 years, with an HbA1c of 8.4%. He has no eye symptoms and visual acuity is 6/6 in each eye. At his first retinal screening, the photographs show several tiny, sharply defined red dots near the temporal vessels in both eyes, with no other changes. Fluorescein angiography would show these as small bright spots.

What are these red dots?

  1. A. New vessels
  2. B. Cotton wool spots
  3. C. Hard exudates
  4. D. Drusen
  5. E. Microaneurysms
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Microaneurysms

In diabetic retinopathy, chronic hyperglycaemia damages retinal capillaries: pericytes are lost and the basement membrane thickens. Weak points in the capillary wall balloon out into microaneurysms, the earliest clinically visible sign, seen as tiny red dots that fill with dye on angiography. As disease progresses, leaking capillaries cause dot and blot haemorrhages and hard exudates (lipoprotein), and closure of capillaries causes ischaemia with cotton wool spots, venous beading and intraretinal microvascular abnormalities (severe non-proliferative disease). Ischaemia then drives new vessel growth (proliferative disease). Because early disease causes no symptoms, people with diabetes need regular retinal screening from diagnosis of type 2 diabetes.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. New vessels: Grow from the disc or veins and look like fine fronds.
  • B. Cotton wool spots: White, fluffy patches of nerve fibre infarction.
  • C. Hard exudates: Hard exudates are yellow lipid deposits, not tiny red dots.
  • D. Drusen: Yellow deposits of macular degeneration.

Key point: Microaneurysms are the earliest visible sign of diabetic retinopathy; regular screening detects disease before symptoms.

Question 3 · Ophthalmology · medium

A 55-year-old woman from a rural area has had painful, watering eyes for years. Her upper lids are turned in, and several lashes rub against both corneas. Turning the upper lid shows a white horizontal scar line across the tarsal conjunctiva, and there are small depressions at the upper edge of the cornea. The lower part of the right cornea is becoming hazy. She pulls out the lashes with tweezers every week.

What is the best way to prevent blindness in this woman?

  1. A. Continue plucking the lashes
  2. B. Long-term topical steroids
  3. C. Corneal graft now
  4. D. Oral azithromycin alone
  5. E. Lid rotation surgery
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Lid rotation surgery

In late trachoma, years of repeated infection scar the upper tarsal conjunctiva (a horizontal white line called Arlt line), and limbal follicles leave depressions at the edge of the cornea (Herbert pits). Contraction of the scar turns the lid and lashes inwards (entropion with trichiasis). The lashes abrade the cornea, causing ulcers, opacity and blindness. Plucking gives only temporary relief and regrowing lashes can be stiffer. The S of SAFE is surgery: a tarsal rotation operation (bilamellar or posterior lamellar) turns the lid margin outwards, ideally done in the community. Antibiotics are added if active infection is present. A corneal graft has a poor outlook unless the lid problem is corrected first.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Continue plucking the lashes: The lashes grow back sharper.
  • B. Long-term topical steroids: Topical steroids do not correct trichiasis; the lid needs surgery.
  • C. Corneal graft now: Fails while lashes keep rubbing.
  • D. Oral azithromycin alone: Does not reverse scarring.

Key point: Trachomatous trichiasis is treated with lid rotation surgery to prevent corneal scarring and blindness.

Question 4 · Ophthalmology · medium

A 52-year-old man developed a sudden right facial palsy 5 days ago, with drooping of the mouth and inability to wrinkle his forehead. He cannot close his right eye fully: a 4 mm gap remains on gentle closure, and the eye does not roll up well during sleep. The eye now feels gritty and looks red. Fluorescein shows fine punctate staining over the lower third of the cornea.

Which measure best protects his cornea now?

  1. A. Topical steroid drops
  2. B. Antibiotic drops alone
  3. C. Wearing a soft contact lens overnight
  4. D. Lubricants and night taping
  5. E. No action until the palsy recovers
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. Lubricants and night taping

In facial nerve palsy, the orbicularis oculi is weak, so the eye cannot close fully (lagophthalmos), blinking is poor and the tear film is not spread. If Bell phenomenon (the eye rolling upwards on closure) is weak, the lower cornea stays exposed, especially during sleep, causing exposure keratopathy: punctate erosions, then epithelial defects, ulceration and infection. Protection is with frequent preservative-free lubricant drops by day, ointment and lid taping at night, and moisture chambers. If the cornea worsens or the palsy is long-lasting, a temporary or permanent tarsorrhaphy, botulinum toxin to the levator to produce ptosis, or an upper lid gold or platinum weight is used.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Topical steroid drops: Steroid drops increase infection risk and do not protect an exposed cornea.
  • B. Antibiotic drops alone: Antibiotic drops alone do not stop the cornea drying.
  • C. Wearing a soft contact lens overnight: Raises the risk of infection.
  • E. No action until the palsy recovers: Waiting risks corneal ulceration; the cornea must be protected now.

Key point: Lagophthalmos from facial palsy causes exposure keratopathy; protect the cornea with lubricants and taping at night.

Question 5 · Ophthalmology · medium

A 77-year-old woman noticed 5 days ago that door frames and lines of print look wavy and there is a grey patch in the centre of her right eye's vision. Visual acuity is 6/36. The right macula shows a grey-green lesion under the retina surrounded by a small haemorrhage, and OCT shows fluid under and within the retina. The left macula shows drusen. Her Amsler grid is distorted on the right.

What is the most appropriate treatment?

  1. A. Antioxidant vitamin supplements alone
  2. B. Panretinal photocoagulation
  3. C. Observation for 6 months
  4. D. Oral steroids
  5. E. Anti-VEGF injections
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Anti-VEGF injections

Wet (neovascular) age-related macular degeneration occurs when new vessels from the choroid grow through Bruch membrane under or into the retina (choroidal neovascularisation). They leak fluid and blood, causing sudden distortion (metamorphopsia) and a central scotoma; untreated, they form a disciform scar with permanent loss of central vision. Distortion on the Amsler grid needs urgent referral, ideally seen within days. Optical coherence tomography and angiography confirm the diagnosis. Treatment is repeated intravitreal injections of anti-VEGF drugs (ranibizumab, aflibercept, bevacizumab, faricimab), which stabilise or improve vision in most patients if started early. Peripheral vision is preserved, so patients do not become completely blind.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Antioxidant vitamin supplements alone: Do not treat active new vessels.
  • B. Panretinal photocoagulation: For diabetic retinopathy.
  • C. Observation for 6 months: Vision may be lost permanently.
  • D. Oral steroids: Oral steroids do not treat wet macular degeneration; intravitreal anti-VEGF does.

Key point: Wet AMD causes sudden distortion and central loss from choroidal new vessels and is treated with intravitreal anti-VEGF.

Question 6 · Ophthalmology · easy

A 24-year-old university student in Faisalabad has found it harder to read the whiteboard over the past year, though reading his phone is easy. He has no pain, redness or flashes. Visual acuity is 6/24 in each eye, improving to 6/6 when he looks through a pinhole. Pupils react normally, the red reflex is bright and the fundus looks normal.

What does the improvement with the pinhole show?

  1. A. A disease of the macula
  2. B. Damage to the optic nerve
  3. C. A refractive error
  4. D. Amblyopia
  5. E. A dense cataract
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. A refractive error

A pinhole lets only a narrow beam of light through the centre of the optical system, so rays reach the retina without needing to be focused by the cornea and lens. If reduced acuity is caused by a refractive error (myopia, hypermetropia or astigmatism), vision improves to near normal through a pinhole. If acuity does not improve, the cause lies in the media (dense cataract), the macula, the optic nerve or the visual pathway, or the eye is amblyopic. So the pinhole test is a quick bedside way to separate refractive blur from disease. Here, blur only for distance in a young adult points to myopia, which is corrected with glasses.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. A disease of the macula: Macular disease is not corrected by a pinhole.
  • B. Damage to the optic nerve: Optic nerve loss does not improve with a pinhole.
  • D. Amblyopia: An amblyopic eye improves little through a pinhole.
  • E. A dense cataract: A dense opacity blocks light whatever the aperture.

Key point: Vision that improves to near normal through a pinhole indicates a refractive error rather than eye disease.

Question 7 · Ophthalmology · easy

A 6-week-old girl is brought for her routine check in a basic health unit. She was born at term and is feeding well. Her mother has noticed that in some flash photographs one eye looks white instead of red. With the ophthalmoscope held at arm's length, the right eye shows a normal red reflex, but the left pupil looks white. The eyes are not red and she fixes and follows a face.

What is the most appropriate next step?

  1. A. Urgent eye specialist referral
  2. B. Reassure and review at 6 months
  3. C. Patch the right eye for an hour a day
  4. D. Start antibiotic eye drops
  5. E. Prescribe glasses
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Urgent eye specialist referral

The red reflex test is part of every newborn and 6-8 week examination: light from the ophthalmoscope is reflected from the retina back through clear media. An absent, dull or white reflex (leukocoria) means something is blocking the visual axis or lying behind the lens. The main causes are congenital cataract, retinoblastoma, retinopathy of prematurity, persistent fetal vasculature and Coats disease. Retinoblastoma can threaten life, and a cataract left in place during the first months causes dense deprivation amblyopia, so the baby is referred urgently (within days) to an ophthalmologist for a dilated examination. A white reflex noticed by parents in photographs should always be taken seriously.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Reassure and review at 6 months: Delay risks loss of vision or life.
  • C. Patch the right eye for an hour a day: Treatment must wait for a diagnosis.
  • D. Start antibiotic eye drops: A white pupil reflex is not infection; it may be cataract or retinoblastoma and needs urgent referral.
  • E. Prescribe glasses: Glasses do not treat a white pupil.

Key point: An absent or white red reflex in an infant needs urgent referral to exclude congenital cataract and retinoblastoma.

Question 8 · Ophthalmology · easy

A 9-year-old girl in Lahore has been moved to the front of her class because she cannot read the board, and she screws up her eyes to see distant objects. She reads her books comfortably. There is no squint. Visual acuity is 6/24 in each eye, improving to 6/6 with a pinhole. Cycloplegic refraction confirms that parallel light comes to a focus in front of the retina in both eyes.

Which lens corrects her vision?

  1. A. Convex (plus) lens
  2. B. Cylindrical lens
  3. C. Prism
  4. D. Bifocal lens
  5. E. Concave lens
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Concave lens

In myopia (short sight), parallel rays from a distant object focus in front of the retina, usually because the eyeball is too long (axial myopia) or, less often, because the cornea or lens is too strongly curved. Near objects send diverging rays that focus further back, so near vision is clear. A concave (minus) lens diverges incoming light, moving the focal point back onto the retina. Myopia usually starts in school-age children and progresses through adolescence. In children, the refraction is measured after cycloplegic drops (cyclopentolate) to stop accommodation from hiding the true error. Squinting the eyelids makes a narrow slit that works like a pinhole.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Convex (plus) lens: A convex (plus) lens corrects hypermetropia; she is short-sighted and needs a concave lens.
  • B. Cylindrical lens: A cylindrical lens corrects astigmatism, which her history does not suggest.
  • C. Prism: Used for some double vision, not for blur.
  • D. Bifocal lens: For presbyopia; she reads well.

Key point: Myopia focuses distant light in front of the retina and is corrected with a concave (minus) lens.

Question 9 · Ophthalmology · easy

A 46-year-old schoolteacher says that over the past year she has had to hold her phone and books further away to read them, especially in dim light, and she gets tired after reading. Her distance vision is unchanged. Unaided visual acuity is 6/6 in each eye. Near vision improves with a +1.50 dioptre lens. The eyes are white and the lenses are clear.

What is the main cause of her difficulty?

  1. A. Ciliary muscle paralysis
  2. B. Loss of lens elasticity
  3. C. Steepening of the cornea
  4. D. A short eyeball
  5. E. Early cataract
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Loss of lens elasticity

To focus on near objects, the ciliary muscle contracts, the zonules relax and the elastic lens becomes more rounded, increasing its power (accommodation). With age the lens grows and hardens, so it cannot change shape as much, and the amplitude of accommodation falls steadily. By the early to mid 40s, the near point moves beyond comfortable reading distance: this is presbyopia. Symptoms are worse in dim light, when the pupil dilates and depth of focus falls. It is corrected with convex (plus) lenses for near work, as reading glasses, bifocals or progressive lenses, and the power needed increases until about 60. Hypermetropes notice it earlier.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Ciliary muscle paralysis: The ciliary muscle still contracts; the lens cannot respond.
  • C. Steepening of the cornea: Corneal steepening does not cause age-related loss of near focus.
  • D. A short eyeball: Would also affect distance vision when young.
  • E. Early cataract: Her lenses are clear, so cataract is not the cause.

Key point: Presbyopia is age-related loss of accommodation from reduced lens elasticity, corrected with plus lenses for near.

Question 10 · Ophthalmology · easy

A 30-year-old woman has had a painless, firm, pea-sized lump in her right upper eyelid for 6 weeks. It started as a slightly tender swelling that settled. It is not red or warm, and when the lid is turned over, there is a purplish area on the conjunctival side. She has greasy lid margins with some crusting at the base of the lashes. Vision is normal.

Which gland is blocked?

  1. A. Gland of Zeis
  2. B. Meibomian gland
  3. C. Gland of Moll
  4. D. Lacrimal gland
  5. E. Gland of Krause
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Meibomian gland

A chalazion is a chronic, sterile lipogranuloma of a meibomian gland. These large sebaceous glands lie within the tarsal plate and open onto the lid margin; when their duct is blocked, lipid leaks into the surrounding tissue and causes a granulomatous reaction, forming a painless firm lump that points towards the conjunctiva. It is associated with meibomian gland dysfunction, posterior blepharitis and rosacea. Many settle with warm compresses and lid massage; persistent ones are treated by incision and curettage from the conjunctival side or steroid injection. An acutely infected meibomian gland is an internal hordeolum; infection of a lash follicle with its gland of Zeis or Moll is an external hordeolum (stye).

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Gland of Zeis: Its infection causes an external stye at the lash line.
  • C. Gland of Moll: A sweat gland at the lid margin.
  • D. Lacrimal gland: Lies in the upper outer orbit.
  • E. Gland of Krause: An accessory lacrimal gland in the fornix.

Key point: A chalazion is a sterile lipogranuloma of a blocked meibomian gland; a stye is an infection of a lash follicle gland.
