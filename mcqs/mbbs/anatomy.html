Question 1 · Upper limb · medium

A 58-year-old woman from Peshawar fell on her outstretched hand. She cannot lift her right arm away from her body and the arm drops when she tries to hold it up. There is bruising over the front of the shoulder. X-ray shows no fracture.

Through which space does the torn tendon pass, and what narrows it?

  1. A. The quadrangular space, narrowed by the long head of triceps
  2. B. The subacromial space, under the coracoacromial arch
  3. C. The carpal tunnel, narrowed by the flexor retinaculum
  4. D. The triangular interval, narrowed by teres major
  5. E. The suprascapular notch, narrowed by its transverse ligament
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. The subacromial space, under the coracoacromial arch

Supraspinatus arises from the supraspinous fossa and its tendon passes under the coracoacromial arch, formed by the acromion, the coracoid process and the coracoacromial ligament, to insert on the greater tubercle. The subacromial bursa lies between tendon and arch. The tendon is relatively avascular near its insertion and is repeatedly compressed in abduction, so it degenerates with age and tears, often after a fall on the outstretched hand. The patient cannot start abduction, and the arm drops if it is released from a raised position. Deltoid, supplied by the axillary nerve, can maintain abduction once the arm has been lifted past about 30 degrees, which is how a cuff tear is distinguished from axillary nerve injury.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. The quadrangular space, narrowed by the long head of triceps: The quadrangular space transmits the axillary nerve and posterior circumflex humeral artery, not this tendon.
  • C. The carpal tunnel, narrowed by the flexor retinaculum: The carpal tunnel is at the wrist.
  • D. The triangular interval, narrowed by teres major: The triangular interval transmits the radial nerve and profunda brachii artery.
  • E. The suprascapular notch, narrowed by its transverse ligament: The suprascapular notch transmits the suprascapular nerve.

Key point: Place the supraspinatus tendon in the subacromial space beneath the coracoacromial arch and relate this to impingement and tearing.

Question 2 · Abdomen and pelvis · easy

A surgeon making a lower midline incision notes that, below a curved line about halfway between the umbilicus and the pubis, the back of rectus abdominis lies directly on transversalis fascia.

Why is the posterior rectus sheath absent here?

  1. A. Transversus abdominis ends at this level
  2. B. All three aponeuroses pass in front of rectus
  3. C. Rectus abdominis ends at the arcuate line
  4. D. The inferior epigastric artery replaces the sheath
  5. E. The linea alba widens and covers the back of rectus
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. All three aponeuroses pass in front of rectus

Above the arcuate line, the internal oblique aponeurosis splits around rectus abdominis: the external oblique and the anterior layer of internal oblique form the anterior sheath, and the posterior layer of internal oblique and the transversus aponeurosis form the posterior sheath. Below the arcuate line, all three aponeuroses pass in front of rectus, so its back lies on transversalis fascia and peritoneum. The inferior epigastric artery enters the sheath at the arcuate line, where it can bleed into the sheath (rectus sheath haematoma).

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Transversus abdominis ends at this level: Transversus continues below the line.
  • C. Rectus abdominis ends at the arcuate line: Rectus continues to the pubis.
  • D. The inferior epigastric artery replaces the sheath: The artery enters the sheath at this line; it does not replace it.
  • E. The linea alba widens and covers the back of rectus: The linea alba is a midline structure and does not cover rectus posteriorly.

Key point: Below the arcuate line all three aponeuroses pass anterior to rectus; behind it lies only transversalis fascia.

Question 3 · Lower limb · easy

After a deep laceration in the back of his knee from a fall onto broken glass, a 35-year-old man cannot stand on his toes on that side, his toes cannot curl, and the sole of his foot is numb. He can lift his foot and turn it outwards, and the top of his foot feels normal. The ankle jerk is absent.

Which nerve is injured?

  1. A. Common fibular nerve
  2. B. Femoral nerve
  3. C. Tibial nerve
  4. D. Obturator nerve
  5. E. Sural nerve
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Tibial nerve

The tibial nerve is the larger division of the sciatic nerve. It passes through the middle of the popliteal fossa, then down the posterior compartment of the leg, supplying gastrocnemius, soleus, plantaris, popliteus, tibialis posterior, flexor digitorum longus and flexor hallucis longus. Behind the medial malleolus it divides into the medial and lateral plantar nerves, which supply the small muscles and skin of the sole. Injury causes loss of plantarflexion and toe flexion, weak inversion, and a numb sole, which is prone to ulcers.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Common fibular nerve: This supplies the anterior and lateral compartments.
  • B. Femoral nerve: This supplies the quadriceps.
  • D. Obturator nerve: This supplies the adductors of the thigh.
  • E. Sural nerve: The sural nerve has no muscle branches.

Key point: Tibial nerve injury causes loss of plantarflexion and toe flexion and a numb sole.

Question 4 · Thorax and mediastinum · easy

A 32-year-old woman with rheumatic mitral stenosis in Hyderabad has breathlessness on exertion and palpitations from atrial fibrillation. For 3 months she complains that solid food sticks behind her sternum, though liquids pass easily. Endoscopy shows normal mucosa. A barium swallow shows a smooth indentation on the front of the oesophagus at the level of the heart.

Which chamber of the heart is pressing on the oesophagus?

  1. A. Right atrium
  2. B. Right ventricle
  3. C. Left ventricle
  4. D. Left atrium
  5. E. Auricle of the right atrium
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. Left atrium

The left atrium is the most posterior chamber of the heart and forms most of its base. The oesophagus lies directly behind it, separated only by the pericardium (oblique sinus). In mitral stenosis the left atrium enlarges and can indent the oesophagus, seen on barium swallow, and occasionally causes dysphagia. Transoesophageal echocardiography uses this close relation to image the left atrium and its appendage for thrombus.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Right atrium: The right atrium forms the right border of the heart.
  • B. Right ventricle: The right ventricle is the most anterior chamber, behind the sternum.
  • C. Left ventricle: The left ventricle forms the apex and the diaphragmatic surface.
  • E. Auricle of the right atrium: The right auricle overlaps the root of the aorta anteriorly.

Key point: The left atrium is the most posterior chamber and lies against the oesophagus; enlargement can indent it.

Question 5 · Upper limb · easy

A 40-year-old man with a broken ankle has used poorly fitted, long crutches that press into his armpit for several weeks, leaning his weight on them. He now has wrist drop, cannot straighten his fingers, and cannot straighten his elbow against gravity. The back of his arm and forearm are numb. His triceps reflex is absent.

Where is the nerve lesion?

  1. A. In the spiral groove of the humerus
  2. B. At the lateral epicondyle
  3. C. In the axilla
  4. D. In the supinator muscle
  5. E. At the wrist
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. In the axilla

In the axilla, the radial nerve (the largest branch of the posterior cord) lies behind the axillary artery and can be compressed by a crutch pressed into the armpit, or by the arm hanging over a chair back ("Saturday night palsy" is usually in the spiral groove). A lesion at this level paralyses triceps and anconeus as well as the wrist and finger extensors and brachioradialis. Sensory loss can include the back of the arm and forearm as well as the first web space. Crutches should bear weight on the hands, not the armpits.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. In the spiral groove of the humerus: A lesion here usually spares triceps.
  • B. At the lateral epicondyle: A lesion here spares triceps and often the wrist extensors that branch higher.
  • D. In the supinator muscle: This affects the posterior interosseous nerve only, with no wrist drop.
  • E. At the wrist: This affects only the superficial sensory branch.

Key point: Radial nerve compression in the axilla weakens triceps as well as causing wrist drop.

Question 6 · Abdomen and pelvis · easy

A 40-year-old woman has a laparoscopic cholecystectomy. During laparoscopic surgery, a 10 mm port pushed through the lower abdominal wall at the lateral edge of rectus abdominis, below the umbilicus, causes brisk bleeding into the abdominal wall. A tense, growing bruise appears around the port site, and her haemoglobin falls.

Which artery is most likely injured?

  1. A. Superficial circumflex iliac artery
  2. B. External iliac artery
  3. C. Inferior epigastric artery
  4. D. Superior epigastric artery
  5. E. Musculophrenic artery
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Inferior epigastric artery

The inferior epigastric artery arises from the external iliac artery just above the inguinal ligament, passes medial to the deep inguinal ring, and runs upwards behind rectus abdominis, entering the rectus sheath at the arcuate line. It lies near the lateral edge of rectus in the lower abdomen. Ports placed there can injure it; surgeons place lateral ports well lateral to the rectus, often seeing the vessels through the laparoscope first. It anastomoses with the superior epigastric artery inside the sheath.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Superficial circumflex iliac artery: This runs in the superficial fascia towards the iliac crest.
  • B. External iliac artery: This lies deep in the pelvis behind the peritoneum.
  • D. Superior epigastric artery: This supplies the upper part of rectus.
  • E. Musculophrenic artery: This runs along the costal margin.

Key point: The inferior epigastric artery runs behind the lateral part of rectus and is at risk from lower lateral laparoscopic ports.

Question 7 · Lower limb · easy

A 50-year-old woman with flat feet who stands all day as a teacher has had burning pain and tingling in the sole of her foot for 4 months, worse at night and after standing. The top of the foot and the heel feel normal. Tapping just behind and below her medial malleolus reproduces the tingling into the sole.

Which nerve is compressed, and where?

  1. A. Deep fibular nerve under the extensor retinaculum
  2. B. Sural nerve behind the lateral malleolus
  3. C. Saphenous nerve in front of the medial malleolus
  4. D. Common plantar digital nerve between the metatarsal heads
  5. E. Tibial nerve under the flexor retinaculum
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Tibial nerve under the flexor retinaculum

The tarsal tunnel lies behind and below the medial malleolus, roofed by the flexor retinaculum. It carries the tendons of tibialis posterior, flexor digitorum longus and flexor hallucis longus, the posterior tibial vessels and the tibial nerve. Compression of the tibial nerve or its plantar branches here causes burning pain and numbness of the sole, like carpal tunnel syndrome in the hand. Causes include ganglia, varicose veins, ankle injuries, flat feet and swelling.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Deep fibular nerve under the extensor retinaculum: This would cause numbness of the first web space on the dorsum.
  • B. Sural nerve behind the lateral malleolus: This would affect the lateral border of the foot.
  • C. Saphenous nerve in front of the medial malleolus: This supplies skin of the medial leg and foot, not the sole.
  • D. Common plantar digital nerve between the metatarsal heads: This is Morton neuroma, with pain between two toes.

Key point: Tarsal tunnel syndrome is compression of the tibial nerve behind the medial malleolus, causing burning of the sole.

Question 8 · Thorax and mediastinum · easy

A 35-year-old woman with long-standing severe mitral stenosis has breathlessness and an irregular pulse. Echo shows a very large left atrium and raised pulmonary artery pressure. Over 2 months she develops a hoarse, weak voice, with no throat infection or neck surgery. Laryngoscopy shows a paralysed left vocal cord; the right cord moves normally.

Which nerve has most likely been compressed?

  1. A. Right recurrent laryngeal nerve
  2. B. Left phrenic nerve
  3. C. Left recurrent laryngeal nerve
  4. D. External laryngeal nerve
  5. E. Left vagus above the aortic arch
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Left recurrent laryngeal nerve

The left recurrent laryngeal nerve leaves the vagus as it crosses the aortic arch, hooks under the arch behind the ligamentum arteriosum and climbs in the groove between the trachea and oesophagus. A grossly enlarged left atrium or a dilated pulmonary artery (pulmonary hypertension) can trap the nerve against the aorta, causing hoarseness (Ortner syndrome, cardiovocal syndrome). Aortic arch aneurysm, lung cancer at the left hilum and mediastinal nodes can do the same.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Right recurrent laryngeal nerve: The right recurrent nerve hooks around the right subclavian artery, away from the heart.
  • B. Left phrenic nerve: The phrenic nerve supplies the diaphragm, not the larynx.
  • D. External laryngeal nerve: This nerve supplies cricothyroid in the neck and is far from the heart.
  • E. Left vagus above the aortic arch: The vagus above the arch is not close to the left atrium.

Key point: The left recurrent laryngeal nerve hooks under the aortic arch, where cardiac or vascular enlargement can compress it (Ortner syndrome).

Question 9 · Upper limb · easy

A 52-year-old woman with hypothyroidism has woken at night with tingling in her thumb, index and middle fingers for 6 months; shaking her hand helps. She drops cups. Her thenar eminence is wasted and thumb abduction is weak. Tapping over the front of the wrist reproduces the tingling. The skin over the centre of her palm feels normal.

Why is sensation over the palm normal?

  1. A. The palm is supplied by the ulnar nerve
  2. B. The palm is supplied by the radial nerve
  3. C. Sensory fibres resist compression better
  4. D. The palm is supplied by the musculocutaneous nerve
  5. E. The palmar cutaneous branch passes above the retinaculum
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. The palmar cutaneous branch passes above the retinaculum

The palmar cutaneous branch of the median nerve arises in the distal forearm and passes superficial to the flexor retinaculum, so it escapes compression in the carpal tunnel. Carpal tunnel syndrome therefore causes sensory symptoms in the lateral three and a half digits with a normal central palm. Sensory loss over the palm suggests a lesion higher in the forearm. Motor fibres to the thenar muscles (abductor pollicis brevis, opponens pollicis, flexor pollicis brevis) and the first two lumbricals pass through the tunnel. Causes include pregnancy, hypothyroidism, diabetes, rheumatoid arthritis and acromegaly.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. The palm is supplied by the ulnar nerve: The ulnar nerve supplies only the medial part of the palm.
  • B. The palm is supplied by the radial nerve: The radial nerve supplies the back of the hand, not the palm.
  • C. Sensory fibres resist compression better: Sensory fibres are usually affected first in nerve compression.
  • D. The palm is supplied by the musculocutaneous nerve: Its cutaneous branch ends at the wrist.

Key point: In carpal tunnel syndrome the central palm keeps sensation because the palmar cutaneous branch passes over the retinaculum.

Question 10 · Abdomen and pelvis · easy

A 60-year-old woman who has had two pregnancies has an intermittent, tender lump in the lower abdomen at the lateral edge of rectus abdominis, below the level of the umbilicus, for a year. It is hard to feel when she lies down. Ultrasound shows a hernia deep to an intact external oblique aponeurosis, in the line where the muscles' aponeuroses meet the rectus sheath.

Through which part of the wall has this hernia passed?

  1. A. The linea alba
  2. B. The deep inguinal ring
  3. C. The inferior lumbar triangle above the iliac crest
  4. D. The femoral canal
  5. E. The spigelian fascia at the linea semilunaris
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. The spigelian fascia at the linea semilunaris

The linea semilunaris is the curved line along the lateral edge of rectus abdominis. The aponeurosis lateral to rectus, between it and the muscular parts of the flat muscles, is the spigelian fascia. A spigelian hernia passes through it, usually near the arcuate line where the posterior sheath is missing. The sac often spreads between the muscle layers under an intact external oblique aponeurosis, so it can be hard to feel and is often diagnosed by ultrasound or CT. It has a narrow neck and a real risk of strangulation.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. The linea alba: Hernias here are epigastric or umbilical, in the midline.
  • B. The deep inguinal ring: This would give an indirect inguinal hernia.
  • C. The inferior lumbar triangle above the iliac crest: This lies in the back, above the iliac crest.
  • D. The femoral canal: This lies below the inguinal ligament.

Key point: A spigelian hernia passes through the spigelian fascia at the linea semilunaris, usually near the arcuate line.
