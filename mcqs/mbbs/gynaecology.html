Question 1 · Subfertility and contraception · medium

A 32-year-old woman attends a clinic in Hyderabad 3 months after copper intrauterine device insertion because she cannot feel the threads when she checks. She has regular periods and mild cramps, no abnormal discharge or fever and has used condoms since insertion. Urine hCG is negative. On speculum examination, the cervix is normal, no threads are visible at the external os and none can be found with a cytobrush in the canal.

What is the most appropriate next step?

  1. A. Remove the device blindly with forceps
  2. B. Hysteroscopy under general anaesthesia
  3. C. Diagnostic laparoscopy
  4. D. Pelvic ultrasound to locate the device
  5. E. Plain abdominal radiograph only
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. Pelvic ultrasound to locate the device

Missing threads after intrauterine device insertion occur in about 1-2% and may reflect threads retracted into the cervical canal or uterus, device expulsion (commonest in the first 3 months, especially in nulliparous women and postpartum), or perforation. Pregnancy must be excluded first, and the woman advised to use condoms or abstain until the device is located. Use a cytobrush or thread retriever to look for threads in the canal; if not found, perform transvaginal ultrasound to locate the device. If it is in the uterus, it can remain, or be removed under direction, with the woman counselled; if it is not seen, a plain abdominal radiograph looks for an extrauterine device (needing laparoscopic retrieval); expulsion is likely if neither shows the device. Women should know how to check threads, return for review at 3-6 weeks, and report pain, bleeding or discharge.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Remove the device blindly with forceps: Blind removal risks uterine perforation and bleeding; the device location must first be established by imaging.
  • B. Hysteroscopy under general anaesthesia: Hysteroscopy is used if ultrasound suggests an embedded device; it is not the first step, and a simple scan is cheaper and less invasive.
  • C. Diagnostic laparoscopy: Laparoscopy is for a device that has perforated into the abdominal cavity, confirmed on imaging; it is not a first test.
  • E. Plain abdominal radiograph only: A radiograph shows whether the device is within the pelvis but not whether it lies in the uterus; ultrasound is first, with a radiograph if it is not seen.

Key point: Locate a device with ultrasound after pregnancy is excluded when IUD threads are missing.

Question 2 · Genital tract infections · medium

A 22-year-old woman attends a clinic in Larkana with 3 days of painful genital blisters and ulcers, burning on passing urine, fever of 38.1 °C, headache and tender swollen inguinal lymph nodes. She has had a new sexual partner 10 days ago. On examination, there are multiple shallow painful ulcers with erythematous bases over the labia and a vesicle on the cervix. She has difficulty passing urine because of pain. Pregnancy test is negative. She has no allergies. HIV and syphilis serology have been sent.

What is the most appropriate treatment?

  1. A. Topical aciclovir cream applied daily
  2. B. Benzathine benzylpenicillin injection
  3. C. Oral valaciclovir for one day only
  4. D. Oral aciclovir for five days
  5. E. Doxycycline for seven days
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. Oral aciclovir for five days

Genital herpes is caused by herpes simplex virus (HSV-2 mostly, but HSV-1 increasingly). First episodes present with painful vesicles and ulcers, dysuria, inguinal lymphadenopathy and systemic symptoms (fever, headache); urinary retention may occur. Diagnosis is by NAAT or PCR on a lesion swab. BASHH advises oral aciclovir 400 mg three times daily for 5 days (or 200 mg five times daily), started within 5 days of onset or while new lesions are forming, with analgesia, topical lidocaine and saline bathing; admit if there is urinary retention or meningitis. Recurrences are shorter and may be managed with episodic therapy, or with suppressive aciclovir 400 mg twice daily if frequent. Counsel about asymptomatic shedding, condoms, partner notification and neonatal risk (caesarean section if a first episode occurs near term). Screen for other STIs including HIV and syphilis.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Topical aciclovir cream applied daily: Topical antivirals are ineffective for genital herpes and are not recommended; oral therapy reduces duration and severity.
  • B. Benzathine benzylpenicillin injection: Penicillin treats syphilis, which causes a single painless ulcer; she has multiple painful vesicles typical of herpes.
  • C. Oral valaciclovir for one day only: A one-day course is not appropriate for a first episode; the recommended duration is 5-10 days.
  • E. Doxycycline for seven days: Doxycycline treats chlamydia and lymphogranuloma venereum, not herpes simplex virus.

Key point: Treat first-episode genital herpes with oral aciclovir.

Question 3 · Menstrual and endocrine gynaecology · medium

A 16-year-old girl attends a hospital in Skardu with heavy menstrual bleeding since menarche at 13, lasting 8-10 days with clots and flooding, and fatigue. She has frequent nosebleeds, easy bruising and prolonged bleeding after a tooth extraction last year. Her mother and maternal aunt also have heavy periods and nosebleeds. Her body mass index is 20 kg/m2 and pelvic ultrasound is normal. Haemoglobin is 8.6 g/dL (12-15), MCV 72 fL (80-100), ferritin 6 ng/mL, platelets 280,000/µL (150,000-400,000), PT normal and APTT 48 seconds (25-37). TSH is normal.

What is the most likely underlying cause?

  1. A. Polycystic ovary syndrome
  2. B. Adenomyosis
  3. C. Endometrial polyp
  4. D. Hypothyroidism
  5. E. Von Willebrand disease
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Von Willebrand disease

Heavy menstrual bleeding since menarche, especially with other mucosal bleeding (epistaxis, gum bleeding), easy bruising, bleeding after dental extraction or surgery and a positive family history, points to a bleeding disorder (PALM-COEIN: C for coagulopathy). Von Willebrand disease is the commonest, affecting about 1% of the population and up to 13-20% of adolescents with heavy menstrual bleeding; platelet function disorders and haemophilia carriage are others. Screen with full blood count, ferritin, PT, APTT and fibrinogen, then von Willebrand antigen, ristocetin cofactor activity and factor VIII, ideally before starting hormones and with haematology support. Treat with tranexamic acid, hormonal therapy (combined pill or levonorgestrel system), desmopressin or von Willebrand factor concentrate, and correct iron deficiency (oral or intravenous). Consanguinity increases inherited bleeding disorders in Pakistan.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Polycystic ovary syndrome: PCOS causes anovulatory bleeding with hyperandrogenism, but not nosebleeds, easy bruising or a prolonged APTT with a family history.
  • B. Adenomyosis: Adenomyosis causes heavy painful periods in older women with a bulky uterus, not in a 16-year-old with bleeding at other sites and a normal scan.
  • C. Endometrial polyp: A polyp causes intermenstrual bleeding in older women, not heavy bleeding since menarche with mucosal bleeding and a family history.
  • D. Hypothyroidism: TSH is normal, and hypothyroidism does not explain nosebleeds, bruising and a prolonged APTT.

Key point: Suspect von Willebrand disease in an adolescent with heavy bleeding since menarche and mucosal bleeding.

Question 4 · Gynaecological oncology and pelvic masses · medium

A 32-year-old woman, para 2, is seen at a colposcopy clinic of a tertiary care hospital in Peshawar after cytology reported a high-grade abnormality. Her family is complete. She is not pregnant and has no symptoms. At colposcopy the whole transformation zone is visible and shows a dense acetowhite area with coarse mosaic and punctation on the anterior lip. Punch biopsy shows CIN 3 with no invasion. Her HPV test is positive. She has no immunosuppression and attends reliably for follow-up.

What is the most appropriate treatment?

  1. A. Loop excision (LLETZ)
  2. B. Total abdominal hysterectomy
  3. C. Repeat cytology in six months
  4. D. Cryotherapy of the whole cervix
  5. E. Cold-knife cone biopsy
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Loop excision (LLETZ)

CIN 3 is a high-grade precancer that, if untreated, may progress to invasive cancer over years. Treatment is excision of the transformation zone, usually by large loop excision (LLETZ) under local anaesthetic in the colposcopy clinic. Excision provides a specimen for histology, which shows the margins and excludes hidden invasion. Cold-knife conisation is reserved for glandular lesions or suspected invasion, and ablation such as cryotherapy is used only for a fully visible type 1 transformation zone when invasion is not suspected, especially in screen-and-treat settings. Hysterectomy is not first-line. Warn her that excision slightly raises the risk of preterm birth in later pregnancies, and arrange follow-up testing for HPV after treatment.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Total abdominal hysterectomy: Is major surgery with greater morbidity and is not needed for CIN 3 with a fully visible transformation zone; excision of the cervix is enough.
  • C. Repeat cytology in six months: Surveillance alone suits CIN 1; biopsy-proven CIN 3 carries a substantial risk of progression to invasive cancer and needs treatment now.
  • D. Cryotherapy of the whole cervix: Ablation destroys tissue without giving histology, and for biopsy-proven CIN 3 excision is preferred so that margins and hidden invasion can be assessed.
  • E. Cold-knife cone biopsy: Carries more bleeding and later preterm birth risk and is kept for suspected glandular disease or invasion; neither is suggested by her colposcopy.

Key point: Treat biopsy-proven CIN 3 by excision of the transformation zone.

Question 5 · Urogynaecology and fistula · medium

A 29-year-old woman, para 3, attends a gynaecology clinic in Gwadar with her husband waiting outside. She had a cervical HPV test and treatment for a precancerous lesion last month and returns for her result. She asks the doctor not to tell her husband about the result or her sexual history. After the consultation the husband enters, says he is the head of the family and pays for the treatment, and demands to be told everything. The woman is competent and has not given permission to share anything.

What is the most appropriate response?

  1. A. Disclose the full result to the husband
  2. B. Share the result with her mother-in-law
  3. C. Tell the husband only the diagnosis name
  4. D. Ask the hospital director to decide
  5. E. Keep confidential unless she consents
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Keep confidential unless she consents

Confidentiality is a core duty in medicine, and a competent adult woman has the right to decide who is told about her health, including her husband. Paying for treatment or family status does not give others access. Information may be disclosed without consent only in limited situations, such as a legal requirement or serious risk to others, and even then the patient should be told first. Pakistani professional codes, including the PMDC code of ethics, and the FIGO ethical guidelines support this. The doctor should explain her position kindly to the husband, offer to discuss general information if she wishes, and encourage her to share when she is ready, perhaps with counselling. Document the conversation. Pressure from relatives and fear of stigma are common in gynaecology, so privacy and respect are essential.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Disclose the full result to the husband: Breaches her confidentiality; paying for treatment or being the head of the family gives him no right to her medical information.
  • B. Share the result with her mother-in-law: Is a further breach of confidentiality and exposes her to family pressure, with no justification in her care.
  • C. Tell the husband only the diagnosis name: Even partial disclosure without her consent breaches confidentiality and could harm her, since a name can reveal her sexual and medical history.
  • D. Ask the hospital director to decide: The decision belongs to the competent patient, and a manager cannot override her wish; referral to an administrator does not change the duty.

Key point: Protect the confidentiality of a competent woman by disclosing only with her consent.

Question 6 · Subfertility and contraception · easy

A 29-year-old woman and her 33-year-old husband attend an infertility clinic at a hospital in Mingora, Swat, after 2 years of regular unprotected intercourse without conception. She has regular 28-day cycles and no pelvic pain. He has no history of mumps, testicular surgery or medications. Both have a normal general and genital examination. She has had a normal rubella status and cervical smear. The clinic wants to plan the first set of investigations.

Which investigation should be requested first for the male partner?

  1. A. Testicular biopsy
  2. B. Serum testosterone alone
  3. C. Scrotal ultrasound
  4. D. Semen analysis
  5. E. Karyotype
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. Semen analysis

Infertility is the failure to conceive after 12 months of regular unprotected intercourse (6 months if the woman is 36 or over). Initial investigation of both partners is recommended: semen analysis for the man, and assessment of ovulation and tubal patency for the woman. Male factor is responsible in about 30% and contributes in another 20% of couples. The WHO 2021 lower reference limits include a concentration of 16 million/mL, total motility 42%, progressive motility 30% and normal forms 4%. Semen should be collected after 2-7 days of abstinence and repeated if abnormal, at least 3 months apart. Further tests (hormones, karyotype, Y microdeletion, scrotal ultrasound) are guided by results. In Pakistan, male factor is often neglected and women are wrongly blamed, so counsel couples together.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Testicular biopsy: Biopsy is invasive and reserved for azoospermia to determine the cause; it is not a screening test.
  • B. Serum testosterone alone: Testosterone is useful with abnormal semen or hypogonadal symptoms; it does not assess sperm production by itself.
  • C. Scrotal ultrasound: Scrotal ultrasound is for a suspected varicocele or testicular mass on examination; it is not the first test.
  • E. Karyotype: Karyotyping is for severe oligospermia or azoospermia, not as the first test.

Key point: Request semen analysis first when investigating the male partner of an infertile couple.

Question 7 · Genital tract infections · easy

A 27-year-old woman attends a gynaecology clinic at a hospital in Mingora, Swat, with a 2-week history of a thin, grey-white, fishy-smelling vaginal discharge, worse after intercourse and during menses. She has no itching, dysuria or pelvic pain. Speculum examination shows a thin, homogeneous grey discharge coating the vaginal walls, with a normal-looking cervix and no inflammation. The vaginal pH is 5.6 (normal 3.8-4.5) and a drop of 10% potassium hydroxide on the discharge releases a fishy odour. Microscopy shows clue cells and few leucocytes.

What is the most likely diagnosis?

  1. A. Bacterial vaginosis
  2. B. Vulvovaginal candidiasis
  3. C. Trichomoniasis
  4. D. Physiological discharge
  5. E. Gonococcal cervicitis
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Bacterial vaginosis

Bacterial vaginosis (BV) is the commonest cause of abnormal vaginal discharge in women of reproductive age: an imbalance of vaginal flora with loss of lactobacilli and overgrowth of anaerobes (Gardnerella vaginalis, Prevotella, Mobiluncus). It is not an STI, but is associated with a new partner, douching and smoking. Amsel criteria require three of four: thin homogeneous discharge, vaginal pH above 4.5, a positive whiff (amine) test and clue cells on microscopy. Treat if symptomatic: oral metronidazole 400 mg twice daily for 5-7 days (or 2 g as a single dose), or intravaginal metronidazole gel or clindamycin cream; there is no benefit in treating the partner. BV increases the risk of preterm birth, PID and acquisition of HIV and other STIs. Recurrence is common. In Pakistan, discuss hygiene products and douching practices.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Vulvovaginal candidiasis: Candidiasis causes a thick white curd-like discharge with itching and inflammation, and the pH remains normal; she has no itching and has clue cells.
  • C. Trichomoniasis: Trichomonas causes a frothy yellow-green discharge, a strawberry cervix and motile flagellates on microscopy, with marked leucocytes, which are absent here.
  • D. Physiological discharge: Physiological discharge has an acid pH, no odour and lactobacilli on microscopy; she has an abnormal pH, odour and clue cells.
  • E. Gonococcal cervicitis: Gonorrhoea causes a purulent cervical discharge, cervicitis and dysuria; her cervix is normal and the discharge is thin, grey and fishy.

Key point: Diagnose bacterial vaginosis using Amsel criteria.

Question 8 · Menstrual and endocrine gynaecology · easy

A 28-year-old woman with regular 28-day cycles attends a gynaecology clinic at a hospital in Peshawar for preconception advice. She has been using a urine ovulation predictor kit and noticed a positive result on day 13 of her cycle. She asks what causes ovulation to occur. Examination is normal and her body mass index is 23 kg/m2. A mid-cycle ultrasound on day 13 shows a dominant follicle of 21 mm and an endometrium of 9 mm with a trilaminar pattern.

Which hormonal event triggers ovulation?

  1. A. A peak of progesterone
  2. B. A rise of FSH on day 1
  3. C. Mid-cycle LH surge
  4. D. A fall in inhibin B
  5. E. A rise in hCG
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. Mid-cycle LH surge

The menstrual cycle is regulated by the hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian axis. In the follicular phase, FSH recruits a cohort of follicles and one becomes dominant; its granulosa cells produce rising oestradiol, which stimulates endometrial proliferation. When oestradiol stays high for about 50 hours, it switches from negative to positive feedback, triggering the mid-cycle LH surge, which induces final oocyte maturation and follicle rupture about 36 hours later. After ovulation, the corpus luteum secretes progesterone and oestradiol, creating a secretory endometrium; if pregnancy does not occur, luteolysis causes falling hormone levels and menstruation about 14 days after ovulation. Urine ovulation kits detect the LH surge, which helps time intercourse; the fertile window is the 5 days before and the day of ovulation.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. A peak of progesterone: Progesterone rises after ovulation from the corpus luteum, maintaining the secretory endometrium; it does not trigger ovulation.
  • B. A rise of FSH on day 1: The early follicular rise of FSH recruits follicles for growth but does not trigger rupture of the dominant follicle.
  • D. A fall in inhibin B: Inhibin B rises in the follicular phase and suppresses FSH; its fall does not trigger ovulation.
  • E. A rise in hCG: hCG is produced after implantation and rescues the corpus luteum; it plays no role in triggering natural ovulation.

Key point: Identify the mid-cycle LH surge as the trigger of ovulation.

Question 9 · Gynaecological oncology and pelvic masses · easy

A 36-year-old woman, para 4, attends a primary care clinic in Mingora, Swat, and asks to be checked for cancer of the neck of the womb because her sister died of it last year. She has never been screened. She has no postcoital bleeding, discharge or pelvic pain, is not pregnant, and her husband works abroad. Pulse is 78/min and blood pressure 118/74 mmHg. The cervix looks healthy on speculum examination. The clinic has trained staff, a treatment room and a link to a laboratory, but there is no organised screening programme in the district.

Which primary screening test is preferred by the World Health Organization for her?

  1. A. Annual pelvic ultrasound
  2. B. Visual inspection with acetic acid
  3. C. HPV DNA testing
  4. D. Liquid-based cytology every year
  5. E. Colposcopy for every woman
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: C. HPV DNA testing

Cervical cancer is caused by persistent infection with high-risk human papillomavirus, so testing for the virus finds women at risk earlier than cytology. The WHO 2021 guideline recommends HPV DNA testing as the preferred primary screening method, starting at 30 years and repeated every 5-10 years in the general population (every 3-5 years, from 25 years, for women living with HIV). Positive women are either treated directly (screen and treat) with ablation or excision, or triaged first by genotyping, cytology or visual inspection. Visual inspection with acetic acid is an alternative where HPV testing is not available. Pakistan has no national organised programme, so opportunistic offers like this, together with HPV vaccination of girls, matter.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Annual pelvic ultrasound: Shows uterine and ovarian disease but cannot detect cervical precancer; she has no pelvic symptoms and a screening test must sample or examine the cervix.
  • B. Visual inspection with acetic acid: Is an acceptable alternative where HPV testing is unavailable, but it is subjective and less accurate; WHO prefers HPV testing for women aged 30-49.
  • D. Liquid-based cytology every year: Needs trained cytologists and repeated visits, and annual testing is unnecessary; for an unscreened woman HPV testing at long intervals performs better.
  • E. Colposcopy for every woman: Is a diagnostic procedure for women with an abnormal screening result, not a screening test; offering it to everyone is impractical and costly.

Key point: Choose HPV DNA testing as the WHO-preferred primary cervical screening test.

Question 10 · Urogynaecology and fistula · easy

A 63-year-old woman, para 7, attends a hospital in Battagram with a year of a dragging sensation in the vagina and a lump that appears at the entrance when she strains or stands for long. She has to press the lump upward with her fingers to pass urine fully, and she passes urine often. She has been postmenopausal for 11 years. In the left lateral position with a Sims speculum, straining makes the anterior vaginal wall bulge to the introitus, while the cervix stays well supported high in the vagina. The posterior vaginal wall is normal.

What is the most likely diagnosis?

  1. A. Rectocele
  2. B. Enterocele
  3. C. Uterine procidentia
  4. D. Cystocele
  5. E. Urethral diverticulum
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. Cystocele

Cystocele is descent of the bladder into the anterior vaginal wall through weakness of the pubocervical fascia and its attachments. Risk factors are vaginal birth (especially multiple, prolonged or instrumental deliveries), oestrogen deficiency after the menopause, chronic cough, constipation and heavy lifting. Symptoms are a dragging sensation, a lump at the introitus, urinary frequency, incomplete emptying (needing to reduce the lump), recurrent urinary infection and sometimes stress incontinence. Examination uses a Sims speculum and the POP-Q system to stage descent of each compartment. Treatment depends on symptoms: pelvic floor muscle training, vaginal oestrogen, a ring pessary, or surgery (anterior repair) for women who choose it. Grand multiparity and early heavy work are common contributors in Pakistan.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Rectocele: Bulges the posterior vaginal wall and causes difficulty emptying the bowel, often needing finger splinting; her posterior wall is normal.
  • B. Enterocele: Presents as a bulge in the upper posterior vagina with a pulling sensation, commonly after hysterectomy; her bulge is anterior.
  • C. Uterine procidentia: Means the cervix and uterus descend through the introitus; her cervix stays high and well supported.
  • E. Urethral diverticulum: Causes a tender anterior mass, post-micturition dribbling and recurrent infection, and does not enlarge to the introitus on straining.

Key point: Diagnose cystocele from an anterior vaginal wall bulge with a well-supported cervix.
