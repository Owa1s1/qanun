Question 1 · Systemic pathology: gastrointestinal tract · medium

A 28-year-old woman has had abdominal pain for a year, relieved by defecation, with alternating constipation and diarrhoea and bloating that is worse when she is stressed at work. She has no weight loss, bleeding, anaemia or nocturnal symptoms, and no family history of bowel cancer or coeliac disease. Blood tests, coeliac serology and faecal calprotectin are normal.

What is the most likely diagnosis?

  1. A. Crohn disease
  2. B. Irritable bowel syndrome
  3. C. Coeliac disease
  4. D. Colorectal cancer
  5. E. Microscopic (collagenous) colitis
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Irritable bowel syndrome

Irritable bowel syndrome is a functional disorder of gut-brain interaction: recurrent abdominal pain related to defecation with change in stool form or frequency, often with bloating, more common in young women and linked to stress, visceral hypersensitivity and altered motility and microbiota. Red flags (age over 50 at onset, weight loss, bleeding, anaemia, nocturnal symptoms, family history of colon cancer or IBD) need investigation. Lactose intolerance (lactase deficiency, common in South Asia) causes bloating and diarrhoea after milk, with a positive hydrogen breath test and normal mucosa.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Crohn disease: Crohn disease would raise faecal calprotectin and often cause weight loss.
  • C. Coeliac disease: Coeliac serology is negative.
  • D. Colorectal cancer: Colorectal cancer is unlikely at 28 without red flags such as bleeding, anaemia or weight loss.
  • E. Microscopic (collagenous) colitis: Microscopic colitis causes watery diarrhoea in older women, often on certain drugs.

Key point: IBS is a functional pain-and-bowel-habit disorder without red flags; lactase deficiency causes bloating after milk.

Question 2 · Systemic pathology: haematology · medium

A 30-year-old woman has had tiredness, fevers and bruising for three weeks. Blood counts show anaemia, low platelets and blasts. She is diagnosed with AML. Her blasts contain Auer rods and cytogenetics show t(8;21) RUNX1-RUNX1T1. Her doctor tells her this finding is helpful when planning her treatment. She asks why a broken chromosome could be good news.

Why is it helpful?

  1. A. It means she needs no treatment at present
  2. B. It means imatinib will cure her
  3. C. It predicts DIC that responds to ATRA
  4. D. It shows the leukaemia came from earlier chemotherapy
  5. E. It predicts a good response to chemotherapy
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. It predicts a good response to chemotherapy

AML genetic risk groups guide treatment. Favourable: t(8;21) RUNX1-RUNX1T1 and inv(16) (core binding factor leukaemias, responsive to high-dose cytarabine), NPM1 mutation without FLT3-ITD, t(15;17) PML-RARA (APL, cured by ATRA and arsenic). Adverse: complex karyotype, loss of 5 or 7, TP53 mutation, KMT2A rearrangements. Favourable groups may avoid stem cell transplant in first remission.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. It means she needs no treatment at present: AML always needs treatment; the translocation only guides which treatment.
  • B. It means imatinib will cure her: Imatinib targets BCR-ABL1, which she does not have.
  • C. It predicts DIC that responds to ATRA: DIC responding to ATRA applies to t(15;17) APL.
  • D. It shows the leukaemia came from earlier chemotherapy: t(8;21) is not a therapy-related change, and she has had no earlier chemotherapy.

Key point: In AML, t(8;21), inv(16), NPM1 and t(15;17) are favourable; complex karyotype and -5/-7 are adverse.

Question 3 · Systemic pathology: hepatobiliary system and pancreas · medium

A 50-year-old man has drunk heavily for many years. He has recurrent epigastric pain radiating to his back, weight loss, oily foul-smelling stools that are hard to flush, and diabetes that started last year. His lipase is now normal. X-ray shows calcification over the pancreas.

What is the diagnosis?

  1. A. Acute pancreatitis
  2. B. Pancreatic carcinoma
  3. C. Coeliac disease
  4. D. Chronic pancreatitis
  5. E. Cystic fibrosis
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. Chronic pancreatitis

Chronic pancreatitis: irreversible fibrosis, loss of acini and duct dilatation with calcified stones; causes: alcohol (most adults), cystic fibrosis (children), hereditary, autoimmune (IgG4-related, responds to steroids) and tropical (calcific) pancreatitis seen in South Asia. Features: chronic pain, steatorrhoea and fat-soluble vitamin deficiency (exocrine loss), diabetes (endocrine loss), weight loss; raised risk of pancreatic cancer. A pseudocyst is a collection of fluid and debris walled by granulation or fibrous tissue without an epithelial lining, after acute or chronic pancreatitis.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Acute pancreatitis: Acute pancreatitis is a single attack with high lipase; his course is long with calcification.
  • B. Pancreatic carcinoma: Pancreatic cancer causes painless jaundice and weight loss, not years of pain with calcification.
  • C. Coeliac disease: Coeliac disease causes malabsorption but does not calcify the pancreas.
  • E. Cystic fibrosis: Cystic fibrosis presents in childhood with chest infections, not at 50 in a heavy drinker.

Key point: Chronic pancreatitis causes pain, calcification, steatorrhoea and diabetes; alcohol is the main adult cause.

Question 4 · Systemic pathology: musculoskeletal system and skin · medium

A 45-year-old fair-skinned man who worked outdoors in the Gulf for years has a mole on his back that his wife says has grown over 6 months, with irregular borders, several colours and a diameter of 9 mm. It sometimes itches. Excision shows atypical melanocytes spreading through the epidermis and invading the dermis to a depth of 2.1 mm.

Which feature best predicts his outcome?

  1. A. The colour of the lesion
  2. B. Breslow thickness
  3. C. The patient's skin type
  4. D. The number of other moles
  5. E. Whether it itches
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Breslow thickness

Melanocytic lesions: freckles, lentigines, benign naevi (junctional, compound, intradermal), dysplastic naevi (risk markers). Melanoma: ABCDE (asymmetry, border irregularity, colour variation, diameter over 6 mm, evolution). Risks: UV exposure (intermittent intense sun and sunburn), fair skin, many or dysplastic naevi, family history (CDKN2A), xeroderma pigmentosum. Growth phases: radial (in-situ spread) then vertical (invasion, metastatic potential). Types: superficial spreading (commonest), nodular, lentigo maligna (sun-damaged face, elderly), acral lentiginous (palms, soles, nails; commonest type in darker-skinned people including South Asians). Prognosis: Breslow thickness, ulceration, mitoses, sentinel node. BRAF V600E (targeted therapy), checkpoint inhibitors. S100, HMB-45, Melan-A.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. The colour of the lesion: Colour raises suspicion of melanoma but does not predict outcome once it is removed.
  • C. The patient's skin type: Skin type affects the risk of getting melanoma, not the outcome of this one.
  • D. The number of other moles: The number of other moles affects risk, not prognosis.
  • E. Whether it itches: Itching is common in changing moles but does not predict spread; depth of invasion does.

Key point: Melanoma prognosis depends mainly on Breslow thickness; acral lentiginous type is commonest in darker skin.

Question 5 · Systemic pathology: renal and urinary tract · medium

A 60-year-old man with stage 5 chronic kidney disease, not yet on dialysis, bleeds heavily for hours after a tooth extraction. He takes no aspirin or anticoagulants. Platelets are 220 × 10⁹/L; PT and APTT are normal. Urea is 270 mg/dL, and he has a uraemic frost on his skin and a pericardial rub. He needs another extraction tomorrow.

Why is he bleeding, and what helps quickly?

  1. A. Uraemic toxins impair platelet adhesion; desmopressin
  2. B. Thrombocytopenia from marrow failure; platelet transfusion
  3. C. Vitamin K deficiency; vitamin K
  4. D. Haemophilia; factor VIII
  5. E. Liver failure; fresh frozen plasma
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Uraemic toxins impair platelet adhesion; desmopressin

Uraemia impairs platelet function: reduced adhesion through abnormal vWF-GpIb interaction, reduced granule release and aggregation, and anaemia (fewer red cells pushing platelets to the vessel wall). The platelet count and clotting tests are normal, but bleeding time is prolonged. Options: desmopressin (releases vWF; acts within an hour), dialysis, correcting anaemia (EPO or transfusion), conjugated oestrogens and cryoprecipitate.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Thrombocytopenia from marrow failure; platelet transfusion: His platelet count is 220 x 10⁹/L, so low platelets do not explain the bleeding; their function is impaired.
  • C. Vitamin K deficiency; vitamin K: His PT is normal, so vitamin K deficiency is unlikely.
  • D. Haemophilia; factor VIII: His APTT is normal, so haemophilia is unlikely.
  • E. Liver failure; fresh frozen plasma: His PT and APTT are normal, so liver failure and plasma replacement are not the answer.

Key point: Uraemia impairs platelet function; desmopressin and dialysis help.

Question 6 · Systemic pathology: breast and reproductive system · medium

A 35-year-old woman with poorly controlled HIV (CD4 120 cells/µL), who stopped her antiretroviral treatment a year ago, has bleeding after intercourse. She has never been screened. Examination shows an ulcerated cervical mass, and biopsy shows invasive squamous carcinoma of the cervix. Her doctor discusses what this means for her HIV classification.

Which statement is correct?

  1. A. HIV directly transforms cervical cells
  2. B. Invasive cervical cancer defines AIDS
  3. C. Cervical cancer is less common in HIV
  4. D. Screening is not needed in HIV
  5. E. It is caused by EBV in HIV
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: B. Invasive cervical cancer defines AIDS

Persistent high-risk HPV causes nearly all cervical cancer. Immunosuppression (HIV, transplant drugs) allows persistence and faster progression, so invasive cervical cancer is AIDS-defining, and women with HIV are screened more often. Other risks: early sexual activity, many partners, smoking, high parity and long-term oral contraceptives. In Pakistan, where organised screening is limited, many women present at advanced stages.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. HIV directly transforms cervical cells: HPV, not HIV, transforms cervical cells; HIV acts by letting HPV persist.
  • C. Cervical cancer is less common in HIV: Cervical cancer is more common and more aggressive in women with HIV, especially with a CD4 of 120.
  • D. Screening is not needed in HIV: Women with HIV need more frequent screening, not less.
  • E. It is caused by EBV in HIV: Cervical cancer is caused by HPV; EBV drives some lymphomas in HIV.

Key point: Invasive cervical cancer is AIDS-defining; immunosuppression lets HPV persist.

Question 7 · Systemic pathology: respiratory system · medium

A 28-year-old woman from Rawalpindi has had fever, tiredness and painful, swollen ankles for two weeks. On both shins there are tender, raised red nodules that look like bruises. She has no cough, weight loss, rash elsewhere or eye symptoms. Chest X-ray shows bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy with clear lung fields. Serum ACE is 85 U/L, and her calcium is normal. She asks whether she will need long treatment.

What is the expected course?

  1. A. It progresses to fibrosis in most patients
  2. B. It needs immediate high-dose steroids
  3. C. It shows that lymphoma has developed
  4. D. It usually settles on its own
  5. E. It means tuberculosis is reactivating
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: D. It usually settles on its own

Löfgren syndrome, an acute presentation of sarcoidosis, is the triad of erythema nodosum, bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy and ankle arthritis (often periarticular), with fever. It resolves spontaneously in most within 6 to 24 months, so NSAIDs suffice. Serum ACE (made by granuloma macrophages) is raised in many but is neither sensitive nor specific. Chronic sarcoidosis may progress to fibrosis, especially with stage III or IV chest X-ray changes, and needs steroids when organs are threatened (eye, heart, nervous system, high calcium).

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. It progresses to fibrosis in most patients: Fibrosis develops in only a minority of sarcoidosis patients and rarely after Löfgren syndrome.
  • B. It needs immediate high-dose steroids: Most patients need only anti-inflammatory drugs for pain; steroids are not needed at once.
  • C. It shows that lymphoma has developed: Löfgren syndrome is benign; lymphoma would cause weight loss and bulky nodes.
  • E. It means tuberculosis is reactivating: Löfgren syndrome is a form of sarcoidosis, not reactivating tuberculosis.

Key point: Löfgren syndrome (erythema nodosum, hilar nodes, ankle arthritis) usually resolves on its own.

Question 8 · Systemic pathology: nervous system and special senses · medium

A 55-year-old man collapses at home and is resuscitated after 15 minutes of cardiac arrest from ventricular fibrillation. He never wakes and dies 2 days later. At autopsy the brain is swollen. Microscopy shows neurons with shrunken bodies, deeply eosinophilic cytoplasm and dark pyknotic nuclei, especially in the hippocampus and Purkinje layer of the cerebellum.

What are these cells?

  1. A. Acute ischaemic red neurons
  2. B. Reactive astrocytes
  3. C. Microglial nodules
  4. D. Neurofibrillary tangles of tau
  5. E. Lewy bodies
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Acute ischaemic red neurons

Neuronal and glial reactions: acute neuronal injury ("red neurons", 12-24 h after hypoxia or ischaemia), axonal reaction (central chromatolysis), inclusions (Negri, Lewy, Pick, tangles). Astrocytes: gliosis (hypertrophy and proliferation, the main repair in the CNS, which does not form fibrous scars), Rosenthal fibres. Microglia: microglial nodules in viral infection, phagocytosis. Most vulnerable neurons in global hypoxia: hippocampus (CA1, Sommer sector), Purkinje cells of the cerebellum, pyramidal neurons of cortical layers 3, 5 and 6 (laminar necrosis); watershed infarcts between arterial territories. Liquefactive necrosis follows infarction.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. Reactive astrocytes: Reactive astrocytes appear days later and are larger, with pink processes.
  • C. Microglial nodules: Microglial nodules suggest viral infection, not cardiac arrest.
  • D. Neurofibrillary tangles of tau: Neurofibrillary tangles form slowly in Alzheimer disease.
  • E. Lewy bodies: Lewy bodies are found in Parkinson disease.

Key point: Red neurons mark acute ischaemic injury; the hippocampus, Purkinje cells and cortex are most vulnerable.

Question 9 · Systemic pathology: cardiovascular system · easy

A 32-year-old man is seen in clinic after his younger brother had chest pain. He has firm yellow nodules on his Achilles tendons and knuckle tendons, yellow plaques on his eyelids and a grey ring around his corneas. He does not drink, his thyroid and kidneys are normal, and he is slim. His father died of a heart attack at 44. LDL is 329 mg/dL with normal triglycerides.

What is the most likely cause?

  1. A. Lipoprotein lipase deficiency (type I)
  2. B. Primary biliary cholangitis
  3. C. Hypothyroidism
  4. D. Nephrotic syndrome
  5. E. Familial hypercholesterolaemia
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: E. Familial hypercholesterolaemia

Signs of hyperlipidaemia: tendon xanthomas (especially Achilles and extensor tendons of hands) are typical of familial hypercholesterolaemia (autosomal dominant LDL receptor, apoB or PCSK9 defects, with premature coronary disease); xanthelasma (eyelid plaques) occurs with or without hyperlipidaemia; corneal arcus is significant in young people; eruptive xanthomas (crops of yellow papules on buttocks and extensors) and lipaemia retinalis with very high triglycerides (risk of pancreatitis); palmar xanthomas in dysbetalipoproteinaemia. Secondary causes: hypothyroidism, nephrotic syndrome, diabetes, cholestasis, alcohol and drugs.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. Lipoprotein lipase deficiency (type I): Lipoprotein lipase deficiency raises triglycerides with pancreatitis and eruptive xanthomas, not tendon xanthomas.
  • B. Primary biliary cholangitis: Primary biliary cholangitis causes itching, cholestasis and xanthomas in middle-aged women.
  • C. Hypothyroidism: Hypothyroidism raises LDL modestly and is excluded by his normal thyroid tests.
  • D. Nephrotic syndrome: Nephrotic syndrome causes oedema and heavy proteinuria; his kidneys are normal.

Key point: Tendon xanthomas and early coronary disease suggest familial hypercholesterolaemia (LDL receptor defect).

Question 10 · Systemic pathology: endocrine system · easy

A 60-year-old woman with severe asthma, needing many courses of steroid tablets and now on oral prednisolone daily for a year, has gained weight. She has a round face, thin skin, easy bruising, purple stretch marks, central obesity and proximal muscle weakness. Her random glucose is 230 mg/dL. Her ACTH is under 5 pg/mL.

What is the most likely cause?

  1. A. Long-term glucocorticoid treatment
  2. B. A pituitary ACTH adenoma
  3. C. An adrenal adenoma
  4. D. Ectopic ACTH from small cell carcinoma
  5. E. Primary aldosteronism
Show the answer and explanation

Answer: A. Long-term glucocorticoid treatment

Iatrogenic glucocorticoid use (oral, inhaled at high dose, injected, topical) is the commonest cause of Cushing syndrome. Endogenous causes: pituitary ACTH adenoma (Cushing disease, about 70%), adrenal adenoma or carcinoma, ectopic ACTH (small cell lung carcinoma, carcinoids). Features: central obesity, moon face, buffalo hump, purple striae, thin skin, bruising, proximal myopathy, hypertension, diabetes, osteoporosis and infections. Exogenous steroids suppress ACTH and atrophy both adrenals.

Why the other options are wrong

  • B. A pituitary ACTH adenoma: A pituitary adenoma raises ACTH; hers is suppressed.
  • C. An adrenal adenoma: An adrenal adenoma is a less common endogenous cause, and she takes daily prednisolone.
  • D. Ectopic ACTH from small cell carcinoma: Ectopic ACTH raises ACTH and causes rapid weight loss.
  • E. Primary aldosteronism: Primary aldosteronism causes hypertension and low potassium, not Cushing features.

Key point: Long-term glucocorticoid treatment is the commonest cause of Cushing syndrome.
